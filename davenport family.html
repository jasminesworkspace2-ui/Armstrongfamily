Francis Davenport

Albemarle County, Virginia · 1740 — Newberry County, South Carolina · 1803

Before there was a Waldrop in this story, there was a Davenport. Every document naming Patience begins here — in one man's hand, in one week in August 1795, when he divided everything he owned among his own children.

scroll
Confirmed by primary source · FamilySearch Family Tree · Isaac Davenport Sr. (L1WK-5S2)

Where the Davenports Came From

Isaac Davenport Sr. was born in 1693 and died in 1749. He married Phoebe Eva Sawyer, born 1700, also dead by 1749 — no marriage record survives, only the seven children who confirm the union. Their eldest sons scattered across the Carolina backcountry as grown men: Elizabeth, born 1716; Francis, born 1718; Isaac, born 1719; Joseph, also born 1719; William, born 1721; Isaiah, born 1726 and dead by 1774; and Phoebe.

It is the second son, Francis Davenport, whose own hand appears on the document that first names Patience. His own beginning is more complicated than his father's. FamilySearch's own record of him carries a formal caution — "Important research has been done on this person. Please read these alert notes before making changes" — because Francis married four times, and the family that resulted from those four marriages was never fully reconciled by any one researcher until now.

Four Marriages

Francis Davenport's first marriage was not in the Carolinas at all. On July 20, 1740, in Campbell, Albemarle County, British Colonial Virginia, he married Ann Whaley, born 1718, who would die fifty years later in 1790. Ten children came from this marriage — James Madison Davenport (remembered in family records as "the Patriot," born 1742), Frances, Abigail, Isaac, Elizabeth, William, a second Francis Jr., Sarah, Nancy, and David.

A separate, independently compiled research record — the work of a different genealogist entirely, cataloguing decades of Newberry County deed books — places this same Francis in Granville County, North Carolina through the 1750s and 60s, then in Newberry County, South Carolina by 1771. Reconciling a Virginia marriage in 1740 with a North Carolina land trail through the following decades is a real, open task. This archive has not yet closed that gap, and says so plainly rather than pretending the two records already agree.

Ann Whaley was not his only wife. Sometime before May 18, 1785, in Newberry, South Carolina, he married Mary Young — no children recorded from this marriage. Then, sometime after September 20, 1794 and before May 18, 1795, he married a third time, to a woman named Mary Turner — the widow of a man named William Turner. No children came from this marriage either. But her name did not disappear from this story: three years later, in his own final will, Francis left an enslaved man named Peter to this same Mary, for the rest of her life.

There was a fourth union — and this is the one that matters most directly to Patience. FamilySearch's own record lists two more children, Martha Davenport and Nancy Davenport, attached to no named spouse at all. No marriage date. No wife's name. Martha — born in 1776, dead by 1825 — is the woman history remembers as Patty Waldrop. She is not recorded as a daughter of Ann Whaley, Mary Young, or Mary Turner. Whoever her mother was, that woman's name has not survived in any document this research has found.

The Family Chose the King

When the British Army occupied the South Carolina backcountry in 1780, the Davenports did not side with independence. Joseph, Francis, Isaac, and David Davenport all enlisted in Major Patrick Cunningham's Little River Regiment, Ninety Six Brigade, King's Loyal Militia — soldiers paid by the Crown to hold the frontier against their own Patriot neighbors.

A British lieutenant named Anthony Allaire kept a diary of his own march through this exact country in June 1780. He recorded stopping at the Davenport home twice — once to make camp, once just to pass through — and he wrote down what the neighborhood already believed about the man who lived there.

Lt. Anthony Allaire's Diary · Tuesday, June 20, 1780
"Got in motion and marched to Davenports, fourteen miles. He was formerly a Captain of Militia under [Royal] Government. He has the name of a Tory from his neighbors; but many of his actions were doubtful."

"Wednesday, 21st. Lay encamped at Davenports, Little River."

Four months later, at the Battle of King's Mountain, the British force Joseph Davenport served under was destroyed. He was very likely taken prisoner. Cornwallis himself later wrote that after King's Mountain, "the heart had gone out of backcountry Tories" — the family's own side in the war was already losing by the time it ended.

A later researcher, working through the family's own descendants, put it plainly: this history became, in their own words, "family skeletons, best forgotten." It is not forgotten here.

Primary source · Newberry County South Carolina Deed Book C, Pages 365–369 · August 1–5, 1795

One Week, Every Name Given Away

By 1795 Francis Davenport was an old man — seventy-seven years old, three years from his own death. In the space of four days, he gave away nearly everyone he held.

It began three days early. On August 1, 1795, he gave one enslaved person, unnamed in the surviving abstract, to a woman named Nancy Waldrop. Then, on August 4, 1795 — the day that matters most to this whole archive — he executed two more deeds within hours of each other.

Deed Book C, Page 365 · August 4, 1795 · Newberry County, South Carolina
"Francis Davenport, Sr., of Newberry County, to my children Isaac Davenport, William Davenport, David Davenport, Sarah Beeks, and Abigail Davenport, all of my slaves and household goods..."

Witnessed: Ezekiel Waldrop, Edward Turner, George Goggans. Proved before Charles Griffin, J.P., August 5, 1795.
Deed Book C, Page 367 · August 4, 1795 · Newberry County, South Carolina
"Francis Davenport, Sr., to daughter Patty Waldrop, gift, one negro girl named Patience."

Witnessed: Isaac Davenport, Edward Turner, Elizabeth Towling. Proved before Charles Griffin, J.P., August 5, 1795.

Read the two deeds together and the same small circle of men appears on both — Isaac Davenport, Edward Turner, Ezekiel Waldrop, George Goggans — witnessing their own family's transactions, one after another, on the same afternoon. This was not a formal courtroom proceeding. It was a family, standing in a room, giving each other people.

Everyone Named in the August 1795 Distribution Rachel, Fanny (or Jenny), Peter, Coy, Mingo, a second Rachel (a girl) → to Isaac Davenport Dick → to William Davenport Oliver → to David Davenport, with 200 acres Tom → to Sarah Beeks Nan → to Abigail Davenport Patience → to Patty Waldrop · this family's own confirmed line One unnamed person → to Nancy Waldrop, three days earlier

None of the seven other names in that distribution — Rachel, Fanny, Peter, Coy, Mingo, the second Rachel, Dick, Oliver, Tom, Nan — has been traced forward into this family's own confirmed line. They went to different children, into different households, and this research has not yet found where any of them ended up. They are recorded here so their names are not lost simply because they didn't go with Patience.

Primary source · Newberry County South Carolina Wills, Book D, Page 40 · written January 23, 1798, probated 1803

One Man He Kept

Francis Davenport did not give everyone away in 1795. Three years later, writing his own final will, he named one more enslaved person directly — a man called Peter — and left him not to a child, but to his third wife.

Newberry County, SC, Wills D:40 · January 23, 1798
"...wife Mary, to have Negro Peter, farm animals, household furniture, life estate..."

This is very likely the same Peter named in the 1795 gift to Isaac Davenport — meaning that if the two records name the same man, he did not go with Isaac at all, or came back into Francis's own household sometime in the intervening three years. Francis died between March and July of 1803. His estate, and everything still in it, passed on from there.
