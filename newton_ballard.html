Newton Ballard

ca. 1830 Mississippi · Copiah Co. MS → Smith Co. TX → Upshur Co. TX → Rains Co. TX · ca. 1880

Born into enslavement. Survived a financial crisis that scattered two members of his community forever. Traveled from Mississippi to Texas in 1850 as an unnamed 20-year-old on a slave schedule. Filed his own 160-acre land claim in 1856 — before emancipation — and signed his own sworn affidavit. Father of Rev. George Washington Ballard. Confirmed by primary source across twelve documents spanning 1832 to 1937.

The Story

Newton Ballard was born around 1830 in Copiah County, Mississippi — held by a man named Lewis F. Ballard Sr., who had been born in Pensacola, Florida around 1792, the son of a Revolutionary War veteran from Nash County, North Carolina named Francis Ballard. Lewis F. Ballard Sr. came to Mississippi with his family, acquired land, and built what appeared to be a stable plantation life. By 1840 he held 517 acres and eight enslaved people. Newton was one of them — approximately ten years old.

That stability was already crumbling. Lewis F. Ballard Sr. had taken out three overlapping bank debts: a Union Bank Stock Mortgage in May 1839 for $8,200.00, a $3,060 Deed of Trust to the Grand Gulf Railroad and Banking Company in April 1840, and a default judgment from the Bank of Port Gibson of $476.41 in March 1840. By April 1840 his total confirmed bank debt exceeded $11,700 across three separate obligations. The Panic of 1837 had done its work. In April 1840 five of the eight enslaved people in the household were mortgaged as collateral — Judy, Scylla, Aaron, Emaline, and Rebecca. Newton, approximately ten years old, was not among those mortgaged.

In September 1845 the foreclosure came. Trustee William Barnes — the same William Barnes who had witnessed a dower release in this county in 1832 — put the plantation up for auction at the courthouse door in Gallatin, Mississippi. It was Mary Ballard, Lewis's wife, who stepped forward and purchased the entire 517-acre estate back for $917. That act kept Newton and the surviving members of his community intact. At least two of the adult men in the household — unnamed, unrecorded beyond the bracket of the 1840 census — were almost certainly lost in the years of financial crisis. They do not appear in Texas.

In 1850 the family sold their Mississippi land and joined the westward migration to East Texas, settling in Smith County. Newton appears on the September 25 1850 slave schedule as an unnamed male age 20. The other four enslaved people in the household match the age profiles of Scylla (now approximately 36), Emaline (approximately 16), and Rebecca (approximately 11) — the women and children who had survived the Mississippi crisis alongside him.

Then Newton did something remarkable. In August 1856 — six years before emancipation, while still legally enslaved — he walked into the Upshur County land office and filed a 160-acre preemption homestead claim. In February 1857 he signed a sworn Settler's Affidavit stating he was the bona fide occupant, clearing the timber, working the land. The white county clerk spelled his name Newton Bullard in the file jacket — a vowel shift that would cause modern search engines to miss him entirely. But the 1859 tax roll tells the truth: in the column for who rendered the property, the clerk wrote Ballard, with an A, exactly as the family carried the name forward.

By 1870 Newton is absent from the Upshur County census. His wife Rebecca Ballard heads the household — age 35, born Mississippi — raising their three children: Silla age 13, Sandy age 10, and George Washington Ballard age 8. The name Silla is not accidental. It carries Scylla forward — the woman from the 1840 Mississippi deed, the elder of Newton's plantation community, honored in the naming of his granddaughter two generations later.

In August 1873 Newton comes forward one last time. He signs a deed transferring his 160-acre preemption survey to E.G. Horn for 250 gold dollars. He does not sign it in Gilmer — he signs it before a notary in Rains County, the newly formed territory to the west. He walks away with 250 gold dollars and disappears from the primary source record. The Upshur County assessor moves Abstract 131 to the unrendered roll, marks the owner as Unknown, and alternates between the two spellings — Ballard and Bullard — until the land is absorbed by the state. Newton's death date and burial location have not been found.

On September 4 1937, his son George Washington Ballard died in Waco, McLennan County, Texas at age 76. His death certificate — McLennan County Volume 4, Page 483 — formally names Newton Ballard as his father. That document closes a century of distance between an unnamed child on a Mississippi plantation and the family that carries his name today.

Research Note · Disambiguation · Two Newtons in Copiah County Mississippi

A second enslaved person named Newton appears in Copiah County records — not the same individual as Newton Ballard. On August 31 1825, Margaret Byrd placed "one negro boy slave named Newton, about nine or ten years of age, and of a dark complexion" into a legal trust for her daughter Margaret Boyd/Byrd — Copiah County Deed Book B Page 74. This Newton was born ca. 1815–1816 and would have been approximately 34–35 years old in 1850 — inconsistent with the unnamed male age 20 in the Ballard slave schedule. Upon Margaret Boyd/Byrd's death, this Newton was to descend to her grandsons Joseph, Madison, Charles Storey, and Samuel Boyd. The Byrd and Ballard families lived in close proximity in Township 10 Range 8 East — these two Newtons occupied the same small geographic community for decades. Future researchers must not conflate them.

Primary Source Documents · 1832–1937
December 12 1832 · Copiah County Mississippi
William Barnes — County Clerk · Dower Release

William Barnes appears as County Clerk certifying a dower release — one of multiple confirmed appearances as Copiah County Clerk from at least 1830 through 1835. Barnes recorded Sheriff's execution sales in 1830 and 1835, served as estate appraiser for the Edmund B. Hatch estate in December 1832, and eventually became the trustee named in Lewis F. Ballard Sr.'s 1840 Grand Gulf Bank Deed of Trust. His relationship with the Ballard family and Copiah County's financial community spans at least 15 years before the 1845 courthouse auction. This is also the same month Lewis F. Ballard Sr. signed as a $15,000 bondsman for Bryan W. Hatch's estate — the peak of his financial standing before the collapse.

June 19 1838 · Copiah County Mississippi
Benjamin Chance Estate Appraisal

Sworn probate appraisal of the Benjamin Chance estate carrying the personal handwritten signature of Lewis F. Ballard Sr. as an official court appraiser. By December 1832 he had already signed as a $15,000 financial security bondsman backing the Bryan W. Hatch estate — confirming he was considered one of the most creditworthy men in Copiah County before the collapse. A juror in multiple county civil cases in the mid-1830s, serving alongside David B. Smith, Cason Scott, and Jesse Gardner. His fall from that standing to defaulting on $11,700 in bank debt by 1840 was rapid and severe.

May 22 1839 · Copiah County Mississippi
Union Bank Stock Mortgage · Deed Book G Pages 385–388

Lewis F. Ballard and his wife Mary Ballard formally acknowledge a real estate and property debt to the Mississippi Union Bank for $8,200.00 — secured under the state's $15,500,000 bond act. The contract sets a strict 10% penalty interest rate for defaults. Signed by Lewis and Mary Ballard; notarized by Rowland Johnson J.P.; recorded by Clerk N.W. Bishop via Deputy J.A. Millican. Combined with the April 1840 Grand Gulf Bank debt ($3,060) and the Bank of Port Gibson judgment ($476.41), Lewis F. Ballard Sr. carried more than $11,700 in simultaneous bank obligations by April 1840.

Copiah County Deed Book G · Pages 385–388 · Confirmed
March 9 1840 · Copiah County Circuit Court
Bank of Port Gibson Default Judgment

Default judgment of $476.41 against Lewis F. Ballard and B. Scott brought by the Bank of Port Gibson. One of three simultaneous bank crises — Union Bank, Grand Gulf Bank, and Bank of Port Gibson — collapsing at once.

April 15 1840 · Copiah County Mississippi
Grand Gulf Bank Deed of Trust · Deed Book J Pages 155–157

Deed of Trust worth $3,060 mortgaging the plantation, livestock, bank stock, and six enslaved people — Judy (age 22), an unnamed male (age 18), Scylla (age 26), Aaron (age 8), Emaline (age 6), and Rebecca (age 13 months) — to the Grand Gulf Railroad and Banking Company under trustee William Barnes. Newton, approximately age 10, is not among those mortgaged. The 1840 census confirms 8 total enslaved people in the household — meaning two people, including Newton, were held separately from this mortgage. William Barnes — who conducted this transaction as trustee — had served as Copiah County Clerk since at least 1830, recording deeds, sheriff's sales, and estate proceedings for over a decade before becoming the Ballard family's trustee.

After Newton's departure the land is marked as owner Unknown. The assessor alternates between spellings — Bullard in 1880, Ballard in 1886, Bullard again in 1891 and 1894 — until absorbed by state tax sale at $2.90 total delinquency. The land is gone. The name survives in the family.

September 4 1937 · Waco · McLennan County Texas

Rev. George Washington Ballard dies in Waco at age 76. His death certificate formally verifies his race as Colored and certifies Newton Ballard as his father. This is the document that closes the century — an enslaved child born in 1830 confirmed by name in a 1937 vital record.

The Louisa Hypothesis · Research Status
Substantially Supported · Not Yet Confirmed by Primary Source

Three primary source documents place the John Bullard estate enslaved community in Upshur County Texas at the same time Newton filed his land claim — the 1854 Faulk Donation Deed (Ouachita Parish, Louisiana) transferring Louiza and her family from the John Bullard estate to Texas; the July 30 1856 Louisiana Sheriff's Deed explicitly confirming this family descended from the estate of her father John Bullard, executed 27 days before Newton's land claim; and the 1860 Upshur County slave schedule showing William Shepperd holding a family group consistent with Louiza's children's ages. Newton's land bordered the John Bullard survey. The Anson County North Carolina Bullard family has been documented continuously from 1778 through 1893 — with consistent Bullard/Ballard surname fluidity matching what Newton's records show in Texas. However, the connection between Newton's enslaver Lewis F. Ballard Sr. — whose family originated in Nash County North Carolina, not Anson County — and the John Bullard family has not been confirmed by primary source. The geographic convergence is documented. The biological or community origin connection remains an open research question.

Voices From The Same Ground · WPA Slave Narratives

Between 1936 and 1938 the United States government sent writers across the South to interview formerly enslaved people before their voices were gone forever. The collection is called the WPA Slave Narratives. Most Americans have never heard of it. Two of the people interviewed had lived in the exact same geography as Newton Ballard — one in Copiah County, Mississippi, where Newton was born, and one in East Texas, where Newton built his life. They remembered what that world felt like. Here is what they said.

WPA Slave Narrative · Prince Johnson · Hazlehurst, Copiah County, Mississippi

"Long Lines of Wagons Heading Out West Toward Texas"

Prince Johnson was born and lived in Hazlehurst, Copiah County, Mississippi — the same county where Newton Ballard spent the first twenty years of his life, enslaved by Lewis F. Ballard Sr. Newton is in the 1840 Copiah County census as an unnamed child. Prince Johnson was there too. He remembered what happened when estates failed and when planters moved west.

"I was born right down in Copiah County, Mississippi... When master would get behind or settle up an estate, that's when folks got separated. I seen long lines of wagons heading out west toward Texas, with hands walking behind 'em for miles and miles through the mud."

— Prince Johnson · Hazlehurst, Copiah County, Mississippi · WPA Federal Writers' Project · Vol. 9, Mississippi Narrative Collection · Library of Congress · 1936–1938

Lewis F. Ballard Sr. did not move west because life was good in Copiah County. He moved because three banks had come for everything he owned — the Union Bank Stock Mortgage ($8,200), the Grand Gulf Bank Deed of Trust ($3,060), the Bank of Port Gibson judgment ($476.41). By 1845 Mary Ballard had bought the plantation back at a courthouse auction for $917 just to keep the community from being broken apart at the sale.

When the family finally moved to Smith County, Texas in 1850 — Newton was one of the hands walking. Prince Johnson's words describe the scene. Long lines of wagons. Miles of mud. The people walking behind.

WPA Slave Narrative · Harrison Beckett · Upshur & Wood Counties, Texas

"Before a Plow Could Ever Touch a Row"

Harrison Beckett was interviewed about his life in Upshur and Wood Counties, Texas — the exact geography where Newton Ballard filed his 160-acre preemption claim in August 1856. Newton signed a sworn affidavit stating he was the bona fide occupant, clearing the timber. Harrison Beckett described what clearing that timber actually meant.

"When we got to East Texas, there wasn't nothing but heavy pine woods and dense thickets. Before a plow could ever touch a row, the men had to clear trees, grub stumps, and build log cabins from scratch. It was hard, heavy work from sunup to sundown."

— Harrison Beckett · Upshur & Wood Counties, Texas · WPA Federal Writers' Project · Vol. 16, Texas, Part 1 · Library of Congress · 1936–1938

Newton Ballard's sworn affidavit in February 1857 stated he was clearing the timber on his 160-acre claim. Harrison Beckett describes exactly what that work was — heavy pine woods, dense thickets, grubbing stumps by hand before a plow could touch the ground. This is what Newton was doing on his land in Upshur County in 1856 and 1857, years before emancipation, years before anyone told him he was free.

The county clerk spelled his name wrong in the file jacket. Newton Bullard. Newton did not correct the record. He just kept clearing the timber.

WPA Slave Narrative · Harrison Beckett · Post-Emancipation · Land & Freedom

"If You Had Your Own Acreage, Nobody Could Drive You Off"

"After freedom come, every man wanted to make his own crop and get a piece of land he could call his own. If you had your own acreage, nobody could drive you off or tell you where to go. That's what everybody strived for right after the war."

— Harrison Beckett · Upshur & Wood Counties, Texas · WPA Federal Writers' Project · Vol. 16, Texas, Part 1 · Library of Congress · 1936–1938

Harrison Beckett describes what every freed man wanted after the war. Newton Ballard had it before the war. He filed his land claim in August 1856 — nine years before emancipation. He was still legally enslaved when he walked into the Upshur County land office and signed his name to a sworn affidavit claiming 160 acres as his own.

Nobody could drive him off. Nobody could tell him where to go. That is what Harrison Beckett said every man strived for after the war. Newton Ballard was already doing it when the war had not yet started.

In August 1873 Newton sold his 160 acres to E.G. Horn for 250 gold dollars — by choice, on his own terms, signing the deed himself in Rains County. He walked away with money in his hand. That is what the land had made possible. That is what Harrison Beckett's generation was still reaching for when Newton had already done it.

Where Newton Fits · The Ballard Paternal Line

Newton Ballard is the earliest confirmed ancestor of the Ballard paternal line. His son Rev. George W. Ballard is confirmed by the 1937 McLennan County death certificate. Rev. Ballard's son James Wilson Ballard Sr. married Eva Armstrong Ballard — connecting the Ballard paternal line to the Nichols-Armstrong maternal line. Their daughter Margie Nell Ballard Corbin is the grandmother of Jasmine.
