Why this page exists.
When researching an African American family, the records often do not begin with the ancestor. They begin with the person who enslaved them, the estate that held them, the person who bought or sold them, the neighbor who witnessed the deed, the administrator who inventoried them, the court that divided their value, or the family that later occupied the same land. Research therefore has to move outward before it can move homeward.

This page preserves those surrounding families and records so that a person searching for an ancestor, a parent, a child, a sibling, or an enslaved community may find a name here that was never part of the Armstrong direct line. A person does not have to be proven related to this family to deserve a place in the archive.
01 · How to read this page

A research room, not a family tree.

Direct family

Our line stays separate.

The Armstrong direct line remains in the site's existing family-story pages. This network does not silently add people to the family tree.

Historical network

Surrounding families stay visible.

Enslavers, estates, landowners, attorneys, witnesses, appraisers, administrators and neighboring households are retained when their records help reconstruct the historical environment.

Future connection

A lead can become a bridge.

If later evidence connects an enslaved person or family to the Armstrong line, the existing archive can be updated without losing the original uncertainty or research trail.

Core principle: legal ownership, biological family, geographic community and documentary association are four different kinds of evidence. They are tracked separately until a record connects them.

Later Austin County records

1884 school-land release: 260½ acres in Lampasas County, part of Austin County school lands; signed by S. R. Blake as County Judge. Underlying conveyance to C. B. Moore dated Nov. 19, 1879; filed Sept. 7, 1886.

1892 school-land release: 160 acres to A. Langham/Laughson; signed by S. R. Blake, County Judge.

1898 school-land release: 320 acres to J. W. Davis; signed by S. R. Blake, County Judge. The research also contains 1898 marriage records bearing the County Judge's signature.

1902 deed of trust: S. R. Blake, “as Guardian of Saml R. Blake a minor,” secured a $1,200 note by J. A. Johnston involving 240 acres in the John F. Stephenson league in Wharton County, trustee G. S. Cummings. The minor is not pursued here unless the record produces enslaved-property evidence.

1841 Pulaski County, Georgia — Samuel R. Blake as attorney

A July Term 1841 Pulaski Superior Court record names Samuel R. Blake, attorney for the plaintiff in Bank of Hawkinsville v. Alfred C. Bostwick. The same court material contains a separate 1830 sheriff's sale of a “Negro Girl Slave” about age 14, purchased by Lewis Wood for $340. That girl is not connected to Blake ownership by the supplied record and is retained only as contextual court-network evidence.

This record matters because it establishes Blake's presence in the Georgia legal network before the later Bostick estate litigation. It does not by itself establish that the 1841 attorney is the same individual as the later Texas S. R. Blake unless continuity is separately documented.

1844 — Mary Adeline Blake's Bostick estate interest

The supplied receipt states that Samuel R. Blake received $1,160.51 from Nathan H. Beall on Feb. 2, 1844, “in full of the interest of Mary Adeline Blake, my wife & formerly Mary Adeline Beall, in & to the Estate of Mrs. Rebecca Bostick deceased,” except for interest reserved by agreement for further adjudication.

The Jan. 13, 1844 agreement describes the estate as including “negroes, debts, Judgments, Appling Land, money, accounts, &c.” Samuel R. Blake signed “in right of his wife Mary A. Blake.” This is a documented estate-interest/legal relationship, not proof that Blake personally owned every enslaved person in the estate.

1850–1852 — the Blake / Beall accounting litigation

The litigation describes Samuel R. Blake as having been Beall's attorney or one of his attorneys for years and familiar with the business. A later filing refers to “Complainants Blake & his father Edmund Blake” resisting the claim concerning Mrs. Powers's specific legacy.

The financial accounting contains numerous names — Drury M. Cox, David G. Worsham, John Pinchard, Dr. W. B. Stephens, John Bishop, Bungay & Spicer, Bungay & Calloway, Hill/Foster/Hill/Sanford, Turner/Dunn/Cochran, C. Thorp, L. L. Griffin, A. Craft, H. Long, Davidson & Burney, Jesse Pope, I. Dunn, D. Sanford, Wm. & Wiley Pope, Fitzgerald & Mann, Eubanks/Kent/Duncan, Bush/Jackson, Thompson/Bush, Alex Smith, Burgay/Hoskins, Bungay & Blake, and A.G./J. Lockett.

These are financial/legal network names, not enslaved people. They are retained because the accounting shows how the estate's property and money moved through the wider community.

Why the Blake page belongs in this network

The 1860 schedule establishes a substantial enslaved population in the Austin County record. The later county records establish continued documentary presence in Austin County. The Georgia material establishes a separate but potentially related Blake legal/property network involving Mary Adeline Blake's interest in Rebecca Bostick's estate and a specific legacy of enslaved people.

The archive deliberately preserves both tracks without forcing them into one biography. Where a future primary document creates a bridge, that bridge can be added without losing the original evidence trail.

05 · Estate & legal network

Rebecca Bostick · Powers · Beall · Blake.

Property / legal network

This Georgia court record cluster is valuable because it shows how enslaved people can disappear from a family narrative into estate administration, litigation, hiring, and accounting. It also shows why attorneys, executors and trustees must be tracked even when their role is not ownership.

“the negroes, debts, Judgments, Appling Land, money, accounts, &c.”

The Jan. 13, 1844 agreement divided estate interests. Frances L. Powers, wife of A. P. Powers, was to receive one half in value of the negroes bequeathed to her and their hire; the other half and hire remained with the executor subject to litigation. Samuel R. Blake signed “in right of his wife Mary A. Blake.”

Beall's 1851–1852 accounting problem

The litigation alleged that Beall had used estate funds, mixed trust funds with his own, made loans, and made profits from the “hire of negroes and rent of land annually.” The court required a full accounting, “statement by statement, charge by charge, and paragraph by paragraph,” with dates, amounts, names and circumstances.

On Jan. 31, 1844, the one-half of the negroes and accrued hire retained by Beall was stated as $1,681.70, subject to litigation.

May 1852 exceptions — the specific legacy
“It does not specify the ages, the value or the amount hire of the negroes.”

The exceptions argued that Beall's answer failed to give the full and entire account of his dealings with the specific legacy, the ages and values of the enslaved people, the amount of hire, what the entire legacy consisted of, whether it was negroes alone or negroes plus other property, and the material facts necessary for the court's decree.

December 7, 1852 order — what the court demanded

The court ordered Beall to perfect his answer. As to the specific legacy of Mrs. Powers, he was required to set forth the amount retained, what it consisted of, whether money or property, the value if property, the annual value of hire, “the issues and profits thereof,” why it was retained, what had been paid over, and every other fact necessary to a decree.

Research significance: this order tells us exactly what additional records or a perfected answer might contain. It creates a documentary target for the next phase of the research.

What this does NOT prove: Samuel R. Blake personally owned every enslaved person named in the Powers legacy. His documented role here is as a litigant in right of his wife and as part of the estate/legal network. The named people remain a Bostick/Powers enslaved family group unless a separate ownership document proves otherwise.
06 · Austin County

William Cornett.

Strong research lead

Marriage: Nancy Smith married William Cornett Nov. 18, 1841 in Austin County.

1841 assessment: 160 acres, $500 real property, one enslaved person valued at $250.

1855 assessments: two separate Cornett entries appear: one with 3 enslaved people valued at $2,100 and another with 7 valued at $3,200. These are retained as separate assessment/property units and are not combined into ten without evidence.

1859 assessment: one enslaved person valued at $500; approximately 520 acres/Mill Creek reading remains partly unresolved.

1860 slave schedule: six enslaved people — F23, M18, F20, M15, F13, F1.

Continuity candidate: the F23 in the 1860 schedule is an age-continuity candidate for Easter research, but the slave schedule does not name her. This page therefore records the lead without converting it into an identity.

Texas State Library and Archives County probate/succession records remain a high-value target for Cornett estate and property transfers, especially around the reported 1861 death.

07 · Bellville

John P. Osterhout.

Connection unresolved

1860 Bellville slave schedule: two enslaved people — F22 and M4.

Osterhout's family papers also contain guardianship/probate material with enslaved people named Adaline, Lucy, Milton, Prince, Charles, Matilda, and a separate 1850–1851 Fisher/Michael guardianship document involving minors Alexander P. The archive tracks these names because the records may reveal ownership, possession, inheritance, or community relationships. A guardian or custodian is not automatically treated as the owner.

Research question: do Osterhout's records provide a documentary bridge to Easter Bell or another person in the Armstrong network? At present, that connection is unresolved.
08 · Tennessee → Texas

Gardner / Grimes network.

Community / property network

George Grimes: associated with Eurydice “Dicy” Gardner; the 1848 Stewart County, Tennessee power of attorney explicitly names Abigail Bell with husband Thomas Bell among the children/heirs of George Grimes and Dicy. The 1849 Austin County succession litigation records George's statement that Eurydice died Sept. 1, 1836 after marriage in Tennessee before immigration.

John Gardner will, Stewart County, Tennessee: signed Aug. 8, 1841 and proven Jan. 1842. Wife Easter Gardner received the farm/lifetime household property and one enslaved girl named Priss during her lifetime. The will gives Luke to James Gardner. It also names the “Dicey Grimes heirs” and “Elizabeth Grimes heirs” for $200 each.

Luke: later receipt confirms James Gardner received Luke from the executors; Luke was valued around $900.

Martha: Dec. 3, 1836 bill of sale John Gardner → Samuel Wofford: enslaved girl age 5–6, $300. This is kept as a direct transaction rather than assumed to be part of the later estate.

Bryan: 1843 deeds show fractional interests in an enslaved girl named Bryan, about 14, transferred to James Wofford after being willed to Walter/Susannah Boswell and John/Sarah Wofford at Easter Gardner's death.

Phillis: 1852 James Gardner purchase from W. A. Griffin for $200.

Network lead: a secondary source reports that in 1851 Nathan W. Bush, administrator of George Grimes's estate, conveyed a Mill Creek tract where George lived at death plus an enslaved woman “Monty” to William Frampton for $518.16, 108 acres, with Thomas Bell as witness. The primary deed is still the target. “Monty” is not silently merged with “Minty.”

12 · Why the archive keeps them

Someone else's ancestor may be here.

A name can survive sideways.

An ancestor may not appear in a direct record. A sibling, mother, child or neighbor may appear in an estate, bill of sale, court case or slave schedule instead.

Property records preserve relationships.

Transfers, inheritance, fractional interests and estate distributions can reveal family groupings that later records obscure.

Community is evidence.

Witnesses, appraisers, attorneys, administrators, landowners and neighbors can help locate the records that eventually identify an enslaved person.

13 · Research rules

What this archive will never do.

Same name does not equal same person.

Names such as Sam, Lewis, Mary, Caroline, Easter, Bluford, John, James and others recur. A match remains separate until documentary continuity is established.

A slave schedule does not supply names it does not contain.

Age and sex are recorded as evidence, not converted into personal identity without a bridge.

An administrator, guardian, appraiser or witness is not automatically an owner.

The legal role must be read from the document.

A legal ownership network is not the same as a biological family network.

The archive keeps both visible and does not merge them merely because they overlap geographically.

An unresolved connection is still a research result.

Eliminating a possible enslaver can be just as useful as confirming one, because it narrows the historical search.
