To every member of this family —

Seven years ago I started asking questions. Simple ones at first. Where did we come from? Who were the people before Grandma Margie? What were their names? Where did they live? What happened to them?

What I found changed how I understand myself, this family, and what it means to be who we are. I found Easter Bell Nichols — born enslaved on July 4, 1848, in Bellville, Texas. I found her mother Clarissa, and Clarissa's mother Patience, who was born in South Carolina in the 1780s. I found a great-grandmother named Eva Armstrong Ballard who ran a café on San Antonio's East Side and was subpoenaed to testify in a police brutality case in 1955 — and told the truth. I found a great-uncle named James Wilson Ballard Jr. who made the front page of the San Antonio Light for helping break a cover-up case. I found ministers and porters and waitresses and gamblers and community organizers. I found people who built things under impossible conditions and kept going.

The more I searched, the more I realized I didn't know much. You can live your entire life believing things were seamless or perfect because of the love you share for one another. But the more you learn, you find out your loved ones were human — their past wasn't easy or smooth. And you start to understand your own defaults, your blessings, and the generational curses that were passed down without anyone meaning to pass them. You realize you are not starting from scratch. You are starting from somewhere. That somewhere is what this history is about.

My mother is Hjordis L. Corbin — born January 20, 1960, the daughter of Margie Nell Ballard Corbin. She grew up at 622 South Townsend Street in Santa Ana and later at 12182 Candy Lane in Garden Grove — the home where I have my earliest memories of NaNa. Hjordis carried the weight of losing her mother at 28 years old, navigated a probate process no one explained to her, and kept going. She is the reason I know this family at all. She is the thread that connects me to everything in this history.

"Teach your children who they are. Tell them Easter Bell's name. Tell them that Margie Nell Ballard made it from Fort Worth to California with seven children and kept going. Tell them they come from people who kept going."

I built this website so that what I found does not disappear. So that the names don't get lost again. So that the next generation doesn't have to start from zero the way I did.

This research is ongoing. Every generation adds to the story. What matters is that the foundation is here, sourced to primary documents, and honest about what we know and what we don't. If you have family photographs, documents, stories, or information about any family member — please reach out. Every piece helps.

A Personal Memory · Jasmine M. Lee

"Growing up on Candy Lane was love — real, warm, full love — mixed with the complications of generational things none of us had names for yet."

The house was a wrap-around — you could come in through the front door or through another entrance that led straight into the pool table room, which sat right in the middle of everything like the heart of the house. Uncle Ronnie had made himself a room in the garage. Aunt Se'Taun's room was the first one on the right — she drove a yellow Bug.

NaNa's room was at the very back. Five bedrooms. She ran a licensed board and care home — adults with mental or developmental disabilities who lived with the family. Her house was always full. That was her calling — caring for people who needed a home when the world wasn't built to hold them.

NaNa drove a station wagon. She wore a reddish-colored wig but her own hair underneath was long and beautiful and naturally reddish-brown. She always smelled amazing. She made pudding for dinner. One year she gave us Nintendos. We didn't live far from Disneyland. We didn't ask why things were the way they were. We just accepted what was there, and loved what we had.

Primary Source · 1795 SC Deed · Confirmed

"Patience, a negro woman, and her increase" — given as property at the marriage of Patty Waldrop · Newberry County, South Carolina · 1795

This document names your 5th great-grandmother. She was a person. The deed treated her as property. Her daughter Clarissa was "the increase" — already born or yet to be born, also property under this document.

Primary Source · 1827 Bill of Sale · Confirmed

Clarissa is sold — separated from Patience — by Ezekiah Waldrop to his son Stephen R. Waldrop · Hinds County, Mississippi · 1827

Mother and daughter separated by a bill of sale. Clarissa was approximately 20 years old. This is the last document to place them in the same household.

Primary Source · 1864 John Nichols Estate · Confirmed

"There were ten negroes in the possession of the said John Nichols deceased at the time of his death" — Austin County Deed Records, Vol. L No. 220, Pages 238–244

Signed March 21, 1864 · Filed September 2, 1864 · This document establishes the John Nichols estate on Piney Creek, Bellville, Texas — where Easter Bell was born in 1848. The ten people named as Fanny Nichols's property almost certainly include Easter Bell and her children.

June 19, 1865 · Juneteenth

Easter Bell is freed at 17. She does not leave Bellville. She stays on the same Piney Creek land — and makes it hers.

December 18, 1865 — six months after Juneteenth — Easter Bell marries Joseph Nichols in Austin County, Texas. She is 17. They will have eight children together. She will outlive him and outlive her second husband. She dies in 1935 at 87 years old — free, on Texas soil, surrounded by her descendants.

A Note on DNA Matches to Waldrop and Nichols Descendants

The Waldrop and Nichols families who enslaved your ancestors are not your ancestors by descent. They are the enslaving families. DNA connections to their descendants appear in ThruLines because enslaved people had children with or were assaulted by enslavers — that DNA persists in your genome. But the Waldrops and Nicholses are not your family in the chosen sense. They are part of the historical record.
