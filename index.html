A Letter to the Family · From Jasmine M. Lee

Why I Built This

When I started this research in 2019 I did not know my great-great-grandmother's name. I did not know that she had been born enslaved on July 4 1838 in Bellville, Texas — that she had lived ninety-seven years — that she had buried children and outlived a husband and watched her granddaughter Eva move to California and build a new life. I did not know any of this because nobody had written it down. The silence was not an accident. It was what happened to Black families in America for two hundred years.

Seven years later I can tell you her name. Easter Bell Nichols. I can tell you when she was born and when she died. I can tell you that her mother was a woman named Rendy Blayn who was sold from Virginia to Texas. I can tell you that her husband Joseph Nichols registered to vote on January 27 1868 — he could not read, he could not write — and he voted anyway. I can tell you that the man who enslaved them was named John Nichols, that he had married a woman named Fanny Waldrup, and that the Waldrup family had held Patience — our oldest confirmed ancestor — in Newberry County South Carolina in 1795.

I built this archive because our family deserves to know where we come from. Not the simplified version. The real one — with the names of the people who were enslaved and the names of the people who enslaved them, with the documents and the gaps and the things we still do not know honestly labeled. This is not a celebration of how far we have come. It is a record of who was here.

Every fact on this site was confirmed by a primary source document — a census record, a death certificate, a deed, a slave schedule, a marriage license, a land patent, a voter registration, a church history, a newspaper article, or a sworn court testimony. When something has not been confirmed I say so. When something is a hypothesis I label it as a hypothesis. This family's history is extraordinary enough without invention.

This archive is for you. For Hjordis and her children. For Sonsery and Deanna and Se'Taun and Ronnie and Orngiel. For the cousins and the grandchildren and the people who will come after all of us. The silence stops here.

Jasmine M. Lee Founder & CEO · Dock Ellis Foundation · jlee@dockellisfoundation.com · WikiTree: Lee-59275

What African American Genealogy Research Is

Researching African American family history before the Civil War is different from other genealogy research. Before 1865 enslaved people were not named in most official records. They were listed in census slave schedules as unnamed — described only by age, gender, and the dollar value placed on their lives. Their marriages were not recorded. Their births were not registered. Their deaths went undocumented.

This means that to find your ancestors before 1865 you have to follow the paper trail of the white families who enslaved them. Their deeds, their estate inventories, their probate records, their slave schedules — these are often the only documents that confirm an enslaved person existed. This is not comfortable research. But it is the only research that works.

The 1870 Wall — What It Means

Genealogists call it the 1870 Wall. The 1870 census was the first to list formerly enslaved people by name. Before that — before emancipation in 1865 — Black people appear in records as unnamed property. For most African American families the documented history stops at 1870 and everything before it is silence.

Breaking through the 1870 Wall requires searching the records of the enslaving family — their slave schedules, probate inventories, and deed records — to find the names of the people they held. This archive has broken through the wall for this family. Patience has been confirmed in primary source documents dating to 1795 — seventy-five years before the 1870 Wall.

This archive uses what genealogists call the Reasonably Exhaustive Search standard — meaning every available primary source has been searched before a conclusion is drawn. When a fact cannot be confirmed by primary source it is labeled as a hypothesis or noted as unconfirmed. This family's history is extraordinary enough without guesswork.

The DNA evidence on this site is a second line of confirmation — not a replacement for documents. When the DNA matches point to the same communities and the same surnames as the paper trail that is when we can say with confidence that we are looking at the right family.

Research Standards · How This Archive Was Built

What You Can Trust Here

1
Primary Sources Only Every confirmed fact on this site comes from a primary source document — a census record, death certificate, deed, marriage license, slave schedule, land patent, voter registration, church history, newspaper article, or sworn court testimony. Information from other people's family trees, AI-generated content, or unverified transcriptions is not presented as confirmed fact.
2
Hypotheses Are Labeled When a connection has not been confirmed by primary source it is labeled as a hypothesis or noted as unconfirmed. The Louisa Hypothesis — a possible connection between Newton Ballard and an enslaved woman named Louisa in Louisiana — is one example. It is interesting. It is not confirmed. The archive says so clearly.
3
The Enslaving Families Are Documented as Context John Nichols, Lewis F. Ballard Sr., Jacob Blane, the Waldrop family — these white families appear in this archive because their records are the only documents that confirm our ancestors existed before emancipation. They are documented as research context — not as ancestors. The enslaver's records are the vehicle. The family is the destination.
4
Gaps Are Honest We do not know Newton Ballard's death date. We do not know what happened to Rendy Blayn after 1865. We do not know exactly which of Clarissa's daughters is Joseph Nichols' mother. These gaps are documented honestly — not filled with guesswork. The gaps themselves are part of the history of what was taken from this family.
5
This Archive Is Alive Research is ongoing. New documents are found. Dates get corrected when better evidence is discovered. The marriage date for Joseph Nichols and Easter Bell was corrected from December 18 1865 to December 23 1869 when the original marriage license was found. The archive updates when the evidence changes.
