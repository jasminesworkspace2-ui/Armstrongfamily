011790s
South Carolina
Where the paper trail opens

First, there was Molley.

The journey begins with a woman whose name survives in the deed books of South Carolina. Molley is not the end of the story — she is the first named doorway into it.

DocumentedMolley is named in a 1793 Union County, South Carolina mortgage alongside her children, including Tom and Patience.
Finding aidThe Laurens & Newberry deed index preserves additional entries for Molley, Tom, and Patience.
Family connectionPatience is documented as Molley's daughter. This is one of the foundational relationships in the working reconstruction.
DNA contextFamily DNA points toward Yoruba and Dahomey ancestry in West Africa; the precise individual-to-voyage connection remains unproven.
The important distinction

The records tell us who Molley was in the documentary record. They do not tell us exactly how she arrived in South Carolina, her original name, or which voyage brought her or her parents across the Atlantic.

021795–1839
South Carolina → Mississippi
The first major documentary anchor

Patience leaves a paper trail.

Newberry County · August 4, 1795

On August 4, 1795, Francis Davenport gave his daughter Patty Waldrop an enslaved girl named Patience. That deed is one of the strongest early anchors in the archive.

Primary document

Newberry County Deed Book C, pages 367–368. The record documents the transfer of Patience from Francis Davenport to his daughter Patty Waldrop. It is not a freedom document; Patience remained enslaved.

Later records place Patience and her family in Mississippi. A 1839 Rankin County deed names Patience, Clarissa, and Clarissa's children. That document establishes another place in the trail — but it does not document a continuous migration from South Carolina to Mississippi or from Mississippi into Texas.

03 · The Missing Bridge

We know where the trail is.
We also know where it disappears.

The archive does not need to pretend the missing section is solved. South Carolina and Mississippi are documented. Bellville, Texas is documented. What is not yet documented is the precise bridge connecting the Patience/Clarissa branch to the Texas people who appear later.

SC / MS recordsmissing documentary bridgeBellville records
Why this matters

A working reconstruction can guide the search without becoming a false fact. The archive keeps Clarissa, Amanda, Harriet, Rendy, Easter, and the Texas Nichols network visible precisely so future records can test the connection.

041830s–1860s
Bellville, Texas
A second documentary landscape

Then the records become Texas records.

By the late 1830s, a Black community is visible in the records around Bellville, Austin County. Easter Bell is documented there; Joseph Nichols is documented there; the surrounding Nichols, Bell, Waldrop, and other family networks become the next field of investigation.

061865–1870s
Reconstruction Texas
Freedom becomes visible in the records

After emancipation, the family starts leaving more records.

The documentary texture changes. Marriage records, voter registrations, census entries, land records, and later probate and tax records begin to make the family visible as free people building households and property.

January 27, 1868Joseph Nichols registered to vote in Bellville, Austin County, at Line No. 1802.
The record saysHe had lived in Texas and Austin County for 24 years. He could not read or write.
MarriageJoseph and Easter's marriage is documented December 23, 1869.
LandLater records document Black Nichols family landholding within the J. Nichols Survey; the pre-emancipation location of either spouse remains unresolved.
The record is doing something new

Before emancipation, people appear primarily through the property records of enslavers. After emancipation, Joseph and Easter appear as people building their own documented family.
