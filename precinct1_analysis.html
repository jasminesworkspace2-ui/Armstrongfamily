Justice Precinct 1, Austin County, Texas — Deep Analysis

213-Record Search Result: Black & Mulatto Women, All Census Years

Prepared for the Armstrong Family Archive · Working Research Document

SCOPE OF THIS ANALYSIS

This document analyzes every record shared from a filtered FamilySearch pull of 213 women (Sex: Female; Race: Black, Mulatto, Negro, Colored) residing in Justice Precinct 1, Austin County, Texas, across census years 1880–1930. The three batches provided for this analysis are covered below, alongside the 1860 slave schedule and succession material reviewed for this research pass.

The two live research questions this analysis is organized around: 1. Who could be Joseph Nichols's mother (needs a birth year of approximately 1810–1834, present in Austin County)? 2. Who connects, directly or circumstantially, to Easter Bell, Rendy Blayn, or any already-confirmed branch of this family?

PART THREE — RED FLAGS AND LIKELY DUPLICATES

Naming these plainly so they are not mistaken for six new people when they are more likely two or three.

  • "Caroline Sims" (1820, Louisiana) vs. the confirmed Sims research thread (Molley, James Sims Sr.) — almost certainly unconnected; different era, different state, no household overlap. Flagging only because the surname recurs.
  • "Amanda Jones" appears at minimum four separate times across the batches (Beat 2 household w/ David King; the 1905 death record; a South Carolina-born widow with sons Jim and Joe Jones; and possibly others). These may be two, three, or four different women. This needs a dedicated side-by-side comparison pass before any single "Amanda Jones" conclusion is drawn.
  • "J L Pink," wife of Hannibal Pink, married 1865 — this does not match Easter Bell Pink's own marriage to Hannibal, which came after Joseph's death, decades later than 1865. This is a possible earlier wife of Hannibal Pink — worth confirming directly, since it changes Hannibal's own biography.
  • "Easter Knox" (b. Feb. 1835, Texas) and "Easter Brookes" (daughter of Lucretia Brookes) — two more instances of the name Easter recurring independently in this same precinct, neither connected to your Easter Bell. Consistent with the established pattern that "Easter" was a common name, not evidence of any relationship.
  • Two separate "Louisa Thomas" entries (1910: b. 1835 Virginia; 1920: b. 1830 Mississippi) with the same daughter's name, Victoria Benjamin, but conflicting birthplaces — a possible same-woman hypothesis that needs corroboration.

The schedule is unnamed. “Rendy” and “Joseph” are research identifications based on continuity and age, not names written by the enumerator.

The central finding: three people in this household — ages 22, 18, and 13 — are specifically recorded as Mulatto, while the presumed matriarch (43) and the four youngest children are recorded as Black. This is a contemporary, document-level racial designation made by the enumerator's own direct observation, not a retrospective guess. The 18-year-old — already the strongest candidate for Joseph across every other line of reasoning in this research — is recorded as visibly mixed-race.

Read against Poppy's own January 2022 hypothesis (Section on the_thread.html, Gen 8.5) that John Nichols himself fathered Joseph: this is the first physical, document-based detail independent of DNA analysis that is consistent with that theory. A household where the adult enslaved women are recorded as Black, but a cluster of children in a specific age range are recorded as Mulatto, is exactly the pattern produced when a white man in the household fathered children with an enslaved woman over a period of years. This does not prove the hypothesis. It is the first piece of evidence for it that isn't Poppy's own DNA reasoning.

Town-Wide Observation

Out of roughly 220 enslaved people recorded across Bellville in 1860, Mulatto designations cluster overwhelmingly in three households: Jos. S.R. Blake (5), John Nichols (3), and J.G. Bell (2). These are not evenly distributed — most of the town's smaller households (Crump, Edwards, Portis, Stephenson, Roach, Holder, Glenn, Harris, and every household of three or fewer) show no Mulatto designations at all. Whatever produced these racial designations was concentrated in a small number of specific households, not spread evenly across the town's enslaving population.

PART SEVEN — AUSTIN COUNTY SUCCESSION/PROBATE RECORDS, 1843–1864

Source: "Slave Records of Austin County, Texas," compiled by Terrence A. Garnett from original Succession Court records, Austin County courthouse. Covers Volumes C, D, F, H, M, and N — 1843 through 1864.

The Key Finding — Martha Edwards Estate, 1864, Matches John Nichols Partition Names

John T. Edwards is already confirmed in this research as "I.T. Edwards," slaveholder of 16 enslaved people in the 1860 Bellville schedule. This compilation adds two earlier records for his household:

Date Estate Named Individuals
Sept. 23, 1862 John T. Edwards's own inventory Mary; Secley & child Lewis; Harriet & 2 children; Martha & child; Stephen; Matilda & child Eliza; Sally; Henry; Jim; Lem
June 28, 1864 Martha Edwards, wife of John Rhoda ($800); Bill ($100); John ($600); Amanda ($500); George ($400)

Read this directly against the already-confirmed John Nichols 1864 estate partition: Fanny, Jack, Rhoda, Mingo, Amanda, Warren. Two names — Rhoda and Amanda — appear in both the Nichols and Edwards 1864 estate records, in the same town, the same year. This is flagged here as a real, notable overlap worth investigating directly — not proof of any connection, given how common both names were throughout this entire research, but a specific, dated coincidence tight enough to be worth checking. A next step: compare the ages given (if any survive in the original court volume) for the Edwards's Rhoda and Amanda against the ages implied for the Nichols estate's Rhoda and Amanda, to see whether they could plausibly be the same two women, previously held jointly or split between the two households.
