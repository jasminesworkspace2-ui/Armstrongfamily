Bellville, Austin County, Texas · 1834 — 1864

John Nichols.

The town of Bellville is named for a different family. It sits, almost entirely, on land that belonged to this one. John Nichols enslaved Joseph, Rendy, and the household this whole archive is built from — and the government paid him for every one of them, in acres, the same as it paid for his own children.

Who He Was
The Landholder John Nichols Birth year disputed: 1794–1800, depending on source · d. March 3, 1864, Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Arrived in Austin's Colony, Texas by 1834, though possibly as early as 1827. Fought under Andrew Jackson at the Battle of New Orleans in January 1815, per his own 1864 death notice: "one of Jackson's men at the battle of New Orleans." His own death notice states his age as "near seventy" at death — placing his birth closer to 1794 or 1795. A separate genealogical record gives 1800. The gap is real and unresolved.

His Family
Where He Came From Origin

His own parents and siblings have not been identified by this research. One family cousin's own working hypothesis places his birth in Prosperity, Newberry County, South Carolina — the same county where the Waldrop and Davenport families this archive has documented extensively were also rooted — but no primary document has confirmed this, and it is recorded here as an open lead, not a fact.

Wife Nancy Frances "Fanny" Waldrop b. 1802 · daughter of Ezekiah Parrish Waldrop and Martha "Patsy" Davenport Waldrop

Married John Nichols on October 9, 1837, Austin County, Texas. This was Fanny's third marriage — she had married James Hillen in Maury County, Tennessee in 1817, and Anthony Hutchins in Lawrence County, Mississippi in 1821, before marrying John Nichols. She was already established in Texas two full years before her own father divided Patience's family among his children in Mississippi in 1839; she received no share of that division.

In-Laws The Waldrop Connection

Through Fanny, John Nichols was son-in-law to Ezekiah Parrish Waldrop (1774–ca. 1847), and brother-in-law to Claiborne Parrish Waldrop, James D. Waldrop, Stephen R. Waldrop, Wiley B. Waldrop, Patsy Stoddard, Sarah Robertson, and Selina Robertson — the same eight siblings who divided Patience's family among themselves in 1839. He had no direct role in that division and does not appear in the 1839 deed at all.

His Land, and What It Cost
What This Actually Bought Him The Land, Priced in People

Mexico's own colonization law paid settlers directly for bringing enslaved people across the border: 640 acres for a single man, 320 more for a wife, 160 for each child — and 80 acres for every person he enslaved. John Nichols received a 4,400-acre league — the single largest documented landholding of anyone connected to this family's history. Part of it, calculated the same way the law itself calculated it, was payment for the human beings he brought in chains.

Half of that same league was later sold to the Bell brothers — Thomas and James — who used it to found the town of Bellville itself, officially surveyed in 1848. The town this whole family's story is set in was built on land purchased directly from the man who enslaved them. Every county record naming "the John Nichols Survey" is still, to this day, referencing his original grant.

Three people in this household — ages 22, 18, and 13 — were recorded as Mulatto, while the matriarch and the youngest children were recorded as Black. That is not a guess. It is what the census taker himself wrote down, based on what he could see standing in front of him. Read against the rest of Bellville's own 1860 schedule, this concentration is unusual: most households in town, including some far larger than this one, show no Mulatto designations at all. Whatever produced this pattern was concentrated in a small number of specific households — this one among them.

A Belief, Not Yet a Document Was He Joseph's Father?

In January 2022, a DNA-confirmed cousin — she signs her own research "Poppy" — proposed directly, based on years of her own research and DNA analysis against the Waldrop and Davenport surnames, that John Nichols was not only Joseph's enslaver, but his biological father. No will, deed, or birth record found anywhere in this research states that outright. What does exist is the racial breakdown above — three people, clustered in age, recorded as visibly mixed-race, in a household where the matriarch herself was not. That pattern is consistent with the theory. It does not prove it.

This is recorded here exactly as it should be: a serious, credible belief, held by a descendant with real research behind her, not dressed up as a fact this archive cannot yet support with a signed document.

His Death, and What It Named
March 3, 1864 The Estate Partition

John Nichols died a year and three months before Juneteenth reached Texas. His estate was valued and divided that same year — and because a dying man's property had to be itemized before the law would close his affairs, five people were named directly, not as ages in a column but by name:

Fanny, Jack, Rhoda, Mingo, Amanda, Warren.

Rendy does not appear among these five names. She had likely already died, been sold, or been transferred elsewhere in the four years between the 1860 census and this 1864 partition. Fannie George — documented in the same Bellville precinct in 1880 at age 70, and again in 1900 at age 90, her own father recorded as born in Africa — is treated in this archive as the strongest candidate for this same Fanny, carrying a name of her own choosing into freedom the same way Easter took Bell and Joseph took Nichols before her.

The Name Is Still on the Land.

"The John Nichols Survey" still appears on Austin County property records today — the same 4,400 acres, the same man's name, over a century and a half after his death. Every deed this family has ever lost, including the 2006 tax foreclosure of the very land Easter and Joseph once owned, references this same original grant. The land outlived him. So did the family he enslaved.
