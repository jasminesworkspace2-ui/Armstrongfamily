July 4, 1838 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Born on Independence Day

She was born on the Fourth of July, 1838. In Bellville, Austin County, Texas — on the land that John Nichols had received as a grant from the Mexican government in 1832. The town being built around that land was named after the Bell brothers — Thomas Bell and James Bell — who had purchased part of the Nichols grant and donated 145 acres to establish the county seat.

The date is confirmed. Her death certificate — filed December 17, 1935 — states her birth as July 4, 1838. She was born on the same day the United States celebrated its independence. She was not free.

Her mother was Rendy Blayn — born ca. 1817 in North Carolina, held by John Nichols in Bellville by 1860. Rendy had been sold from Jacob Blane's household in Halifax County, Virginia sometime between 1850 and 1860 — transported more than a thousand miles from Virginia to Texas. Easter was born on that Texas land approximately two years before her mother appears in the documentary record in Texas, which means Rendy arrived in Bellville before 1838.

Her father is listed as Unknown on her death certificate. He was never named in any document that has survived.

The household she was born into was John Nichols' plantation on Piney Creek near Bellville. John Nichols was 39 years old when Easter was born. His wife Fanny Waldrop Nichols — daughter of Hezekiah Waldrop, who had held Patience and Clarissa — was approximately 36 years old. The two enslaved family lines that would eventually become one were already living in the same household. Clarissa's descendants were in that house. Easter was in that house. They grew up together without knowing they would one day become family.

1838 — 1865 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Twenty-Seven Years

Easter Bell was enslaved for twenty-seven years. From birth to freedom. The entire first chapter of her documented life happened inside captivity on the Nichols plantation in Bellville.

What those twenty-seven years looked like — what she learned, what she believed, what she called herself before she chose her own name, what she understood about the family that held her — the documents do not say. The 1860 slave schedule records her only as a female age 22. No name. No history. A number in a column.

She was 22 years old in 1860. Her mother Rendy was 43 — listed directly above her in the same household. They were together. Whatever the twenty-seven years of enslavement held, they held it together.

John Nichols died on March 3, 1864 — one year and three months before Juneteenth. He was 65 years old. The household was dissolving. The Civil War was in its final year. Easter was 26 years old. She had been alive for twenty-six years and had never been free.

Then — on April 9, 1865 — the war ended at Appomattox. And on June 19, 1865 — General Gordon Granger arrived in Galveston, Texas and read General Order No. 3. The enslaved people of Texas were free.

Easter Bell was 27 years old. She had been born on the Fourth of July. She was freed on Juneteenth. The two most important dates in American freedom — and she had lived inside the contradiction between them for twenty-seven years.

March 25, 1860 · Texas

Katie

Before emancipation. Before marriage. Before any of what came after — Easter Bell had a daughter.

Katie was born March 25, 1860. Easter was 22 years old. Katie's death certificate — filed May 11, 1923 in Texarkana, Texas — lists her mother's maiden name as Easter Bell and her father as Don't Know.

The documents do not name Katie's father. They cannot. An enslaved woman in 1860 had no legal standing to name a father, no legal marriage, no legal protection. Whatever the circumstances of Katie's birth — and the documents deliberately offer nothing — Katie was born enslaved on the Nichols plantation in Bellville on March 25, 1860.

She was five years old when Juneteenth came. She grew up free. She moved to Texarkana, Texas. She died there on May 11, 1923 — buried at Domino, Texas. Her informant was her son Charley Thornton.

She was Easter Bell's firstborn. The document that names her is the one that names her mother's maiden name — Easter Bell — and in doing so confirms forever what Easter called herself.

December 23, 1869 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Six Months After Juneteenth

Six months after Juneteenth. Six months of freedom. And on December 23, 1869 — Easter Bell married Joseph Nichols in Bellville, Austin County, Texas.

Joseph Nichols was born ca. 1847 in Texas. He had been enslaved in the same John Nichols household. He took the Nichols surname — the name of the family that had held him — as many formerly enslaved people did. Easter took the Bell surname — the name of the Bell family whose land surrounded the town, whose name was on the buildings, whose presence was everywhere in Bellville.

Two formerly enslaved people. Two different surnames. One family.

Joseph was DNA-confirmed as a descendant of Clarissa — Patience's daughter — through Amanda or Harriet, Clarissa's confirmed children. Easter was the daughter of Rendy Blayn. When Easter Bell married Joseph Nichols on December 23, 1869, two enslaved family lines that had been building toward each other across seventy years finally became one.

Patience was given to the Waldrop family in 1795.
Clarissa was born into that family ca. 1807.
Amanda or Harriet — Clarissa's daughter — was born into captivity.
Joseph Nichols was born from that line in 1847.

Rendy Blayn was born in North Carolina ca. 1817.
Easter Bell was born from Rendy in 1838.

On December 23, 1869 — four and a half years after Juneteenth —
seventy years of two families
became one.

They had nine children. They built a life in Bellville. And at some point after emancipation — the exact date and circumstances not yet confirmed by primary source — Easter Bell and Joseph Nichols acquired 2 acres of the John Nichols Survey land in Austin County, Texas. The same land where Easter had been born enslaved. The same land grant that John Nichols had received from the Mexican government in 1832. The same ground.

They owned it. Their names were on the deed. Volume 209, Page 228, Austin County deed records.

1860 — 1891 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Nine Children

Easter Bell had nine children across thirty-one years. The first was born enslaved. The rest were born free. All of them were born in Austin County, Texas — on or near the same land where Easter had been born.

Note: Hellen Nichols Armstrong and Helen Nichols Reese are confirmed as the same person — Death Certificate No. 54237 lists parents as Joe and Easter Nichols, died December 5 1931 in Bellville, buried Jeff Cemetery. After John Armstrong was shot dead in 1911, Hellen remarried into the Reese family and carried that name until her death. She is Eva's mother.

Joseph Nichols died in 1905 per Jeff Cemetery records. Easter had been married to him for forty years. She was 67 years old when he died. She had thirty more years to live.

On July 30, 1914 — Easter Bell was 76 years old — she married Hannibal Pink, a man who had been born around 1840 and had died in 1907 — which means this marriage date may need further review, or there was a second Pink family member she married after Hannibal's death. What is confirmed is that she died under the name Easter Pink, buried at Jeff Cemetery in Bellville on December 17 1935. The Find a Grave record for her memorial lists her children from both the Nichols and Pink lines. The woman who had been Easter Bell Nichols — born enslaved, freed at 27, widowed, remarried, outliving nearly everyone she ever knew — died at 97 with another family's name added to her own. The names accumulated across ninety-seven years of a life that refused to end. Her children from the Pink line — Lizzie Pink, Louis Biggs Pink, and Pearly Pink — are confirmed in family records alongside her Nichols children.

The John Nichols Survey · Abstract 73 · Austin County, Texas

The Land

141 Years the family held the land · 1865–2006

John Nichols received a land grant from the Mexican government on April 25, 1830. The town of Bellville was built on land he sold to the Bell brothers. Easter Bell was born enslaved on what remained of that grant in 1838.

After emancipation, Easter Bell and Joseph Nichols acquired 2 acres of the John Nichols Survey — Abstract 73, Austin County, Texas. Volume 209, Page 228, Austin County deed records. The same ground. Their names on a deed where once only John Nichols' name had been.

Easter died December 17, 1935. The land stayed in the family. Allen, Oscar, Willie, Clara, Helen, Mabel — Easter's children and their descendants — held that 2 acres through the Great Depression, through World War II, through the Great Migration that took Margie and her children to California.

In 1997, Austin County filed a tax suit — Cause No. 97V-003. The named defendants included Easter Pink — dead for sixty-two years — alongside her children and grandchildren. Easter's name was still on the land. The county sued a dead woman because her name was still on the title.

On August 1, 2006 — the land was taken. Tax sale. Conveyed to Austin County for Bellville Independent School District. One hundred and forty-one years after Easter Bell was freed on that same ground, the last 2 acres of the John Nichols Survey that her family had held were gone.

December 17, 1935 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Ninety-Seven Years

Clara Nezy — Easter's daughter, born Clara Nichols in 1875 — was the informant. She was 60 years old when her mother died. She sat with the death certificate and answered the questions. When they asked for her mother's maiden name she said what she had heard Easter say across sixty years of living: Rendy Blayn. The death certificate wrote it as Candy Blayn. The name survived phonetically — the way it had always survived — because Easter carried it and Clara carried it after her.

Easter Bell was born enslaved on July 4 1838. She died free on December 17 1935. She was 97 years old. She had outlasted everyone who had ever held power over her. She had outlasted the plantation, the enslavers, the Civil War, Reconstruction, Jim Crow's worst decades, her husband, most of her children. She outlasted nearly a century and died in her own home on land her family had held since emancipation.

on the Same Ground.

The date of her birth was not an accident of history. It was an insult that became a defiance. Born on the day the country celebrated its freedom — while she had none. Freed on Juneteenth twenty-seven years later. Married six months after that. Built a family. Acquired land. Raised nine children free. Outlived nearly everyone.

The land was taken in 2006. But Easter Bell was already buried at Jeff Cemetery in Bellville — the town built on John Nichols' land, the town she was born enslaved in, the town she died free in at ninety-seven years old.

She kept her mother's name alive. She kept the land. She kept the family together across a century that tried everything to break them. This page exists because she did.

Primary Source · Newberry County South Carolina · 1792

The Name Easter Was Already There

Easter Bell Nichols was born July 4 1838 in Bellville, Austin County, Texas. But the name Easter appears in the Davenport-Waldrop community forty-six years earlier — in Newberry County, South Carolina — in the same family network that would eventually hold Easter Bell herself.

On December 10 1792 — Deed Book B: 629 — Francis Davenport sold to his son James Davenport, for £15 sterling, one enslaved girl named Easther. The witnesses were George Goggans and Richard Henderson Waldrop. Three years later Francis Davenport gave Patience — Easter Bell's great-great-grandmother — to his daughter Patty Waldrop. The Davenport family was transferring enslaved people to their own children — and the name Easter was already part of that community.

This is not proof of direct descent. The Easther sold in 1792 and the Easter Bell born in 1838 are separated by forty-six years and at least two generations. But names carried meaning in enslaved communities. They were chosen deliberately. They were passed down. The name Easter — born in the Davenport household in South Carolina in 1792 — traveled with the same community to Texas and appeared again when Easter Bell was born on Independence Day 1838 in Bellville.

The Bellville Countryman · July 5 1864 · One Year Before Freedom

"Mill Creek Bottom Is Full of Runaways"

On July 5 1864 — one year before Juneteenth — the Bellville Countryman newspaper published a notice calling for action against enslaved people who had fled to the woods along Mill Creek. The paper was edited by John P. Osterhout — who himself held one enslaved woman in Bellville in 1860.

"Mill creek bottom is said to be full of runaways. Would it not be well for the Reserves to make a raid on these negroes."

The Bellville Countryman · Vol. 4 No. 41 · July 5 1864 · pg. 1 col. 1 · Texas Digital Newspaper Program · University of North Texas

Easter Bell Nichols was approximately 26 years old in July 1864. She was held in John Nichols's household — the same land that would become Bellville. The people hiding in Mill Creek bottom were her neighbors, her community. The Civil War was nearly over. The people of Bellville knew it. Freedom was close enough to run toward.

The same issue also reported that a formerly enslaved man had killed a Bohemian man who tried to arrest him near Travis — in the same Austin County community. The world was breaking open in the summer of 1864. Juneteenth would arrive eleven months later.

Historical Context · Bellville · Austin County · Texas

They Were Right Here

Between 1936 and 1938, the United States government sent writers across the South to interview formerly enslaved people before their voices were lost forever. The collection is called the WPA Slave Narratives. Most Americans have never heard of it. Two of the people they interviewed lived in the exact same place as Easter Bell Nichols. Same town. Same fields. Same memory of the same day freedom arrived.

WPA Slave Narrative · Katie Darling · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

"I Was Born Right Down Here in Bellville"

Katie Darling was born enslaved on the plantation of Dr. Willie Rogers — in Bellville, Austin County, Texas. The same town where Easter Bell Nichols was born enslaved on July 4, 1838. The same roads. The same courthouse. The same fields stretching toward the same horizon. When the government writers came to interview her in the late 1930s, Katie was elderly. She had survived everything. And she remembered everything — in her own words.

"I was born right down here in Bellville, Texas, on Dr. Willie Rogers' place... When I was just a little girl about six years old, they set me to work minding the master's children and nursing. I had to stay right by the cradle and rock it, and if that baby cried, I got whipped."

— Katie Darling · Bellville, Texas · WPA Federal Writers' Project · Vol. 16, Texas, Part 1 · Library of Congress · 1936–1938

Easter Bell Nichols was a little girl in Bellville in the 1840s. Six years old in 1844. The conditions Katie describes — the enforced work, the punishment for a baby's cry, the absence of childhood — were the conditions of Easter Bell's childhood too. Same plantation belt. Same county. Same era. Katie's words put a name to what the records can only suggest.

"We worked from before sunrise till long after dark. The field hands had to pick cotton till they couldn't see the rows no more, and the house servants stayed on their feet till the white folks went to bed. Every season had its work, from planting to picking, and nobody stayed idle."

— Katie Darling · Bellville, Texas · WPA Federal Writers' Project · Vol. 16, Texas, Part 1 · Library of Congress · 1936–1938

Austin County was part of the fertile Brazos River cotton belt. Forced agricultural labor defined the rhythm of every season. Easter Bell Nichols lived inside this rhythm from the day she was born until she was 27 years old. Katie Darling describes what that life demanded — before sunrise to after dark, from planting to picking, nobody idle.

"When freedom come, the master didn't tell us right off. We kept right on working... Some of the folks around Bellville didn't find out till weeks after the soldiers come into Galveston. When they finally told us we was free, people started looking for their folks and trying to make a way for themselves."

— Katie Darling · Bellville, Texas · WPA Federal Writers' Project · Vol. 16, Texas, Part 1 · Library of Congress · 1936–1938

General Order No. 3 was issued in Galveston on June 19, 1865. Easter Bell Nichols was 27 years old. Katie Darling confirms what the history books record but rarely emphasize — enslavers in Bellville delayed telling people they were free. They kept working them through the harvest. Freedom arrived in Austin County the way most things arrived in Austin County for Black people: late, and only after the work was done.

When Easter Bell finally heard the words — she started looking for her folks and trying to make a way for herself. She stayed in Bellville. She married Joseph Nichols. She had children who had children who had children. She made her way. She made it last 97 years.

Full citation: Works Progress Administration (WPA). Federal Writers' Project: Slave Narrative Project, Vol. 16, Texas, Part 1 (Adams–Duhon). Library of Congress, Manuscript Division, 1936–1938. Interview with Katie Darling, Bellville, Austin County, Texas.

WPA Slave Narrative · Clara Brim · Brazos River Plantation Belt · Texas

"The First Thing We Did Was Look for Each Other"

Clara Brim was interviewed about her life on a cotton plantation in the Texas coastal river plain — the same plantation belt that ran through Austin and Washington Counties, the same belt where Easter Bell Nichols and Joseph Nichols made their lives after emancipation.

Clara's account focuses on what happened the moment freedom was declared — not the celebration that people sometimes imagine, but the desperate, urgent work of survival. She described how the first thing formerly enslaved families did after emancipation was try to locate the people who had been sold away from them. Children looking for mothers. Husbands looking for wives. Siblings who had been separated on courthouse steps years earlier, trying to find their way back to each other across hundreds of miles of Texas.

She described how formerly enslaved women worked immediately to establish legal marriages, and how families sought to buy even small plots of land because they understood that land was the only protection they had for their daughters — from former enslavers who had not changed their minds just because the law had changed.

Paraphrased from Clara Brim's WPA Slave Narrative · Federal Writers' Project · Texas Collection · Vol. 16 · 1936–1938

This is exactly what Easter Bell and Joseph Nichols did. They stayed in Bellville. They acquired land. They built a community rooted enough that Easter Bell could die there at 97, buried at Jeff Cemetery, surrounded by the same land her family had worked since before she was born.

Clara Brim's account is not proof of anything specific about Easter Bell's life. But it is the pattern — the shared experience of an entire generation of Black Texans who came out of slavery with nothing but each other and built something that lasted. Easter Bell built something that lasted. The fact that you are reading this is the proof.

Primary Source · 2006 Tax Foreclosure · Austin County Texas

She Owned The Land

John Nichols received his land from the Mexican government in 1832. He held Easter Bell and Rendy Blayn as property on that land. He died in 1864. And then the land descended to the people he had enslaved.

Easter Bell Nichols — born enslaved on the John Nichols Survey — died in 1935 as a legal heir to a fractional interest in that same land. So did her children Allen, Oscar, Clara, Willie, and Hellen. So did Annie Nichols King and her sons Joseph King and Charley King. The land that had been worked by the enslaved community became the inheritance of that same community. They held it for over 140 years.

In 2006 Austin County filed a tax foreclosure suit — Cause No. 97V-003 — in the 155th Judicial District Court of Austin County, Texas. The named defendants were the heirs: Easter Pink, Allen Nichols, Oscar Nichols, Clara Nazy, Joseph King, Charley King, Annie King, Helen Armstrong also known as Helen Gilbert, Joseph Nichols, Willey Nichols, and Myrtle T. Everline. The land sold at foreclosure on August 1 2006 and transferred to Austin County for the benefit of the Bellville Independent School District.

Easter Bell had been dead for 71 years by the time the county took the land. But her name — Easter Pink — was still on the title. Still a legal heir. Still attached to the ground her mother Rendy had worked before either of them were free.

The chain of title traces to Volume 209 Page 228 of the Austin County Deed Records — the fractional split from the original 1832 John Nichols Survey master grant. That deed documents exactly how the land transferred from the Nichols estate to the people who had been held on it. It is an active research target.
