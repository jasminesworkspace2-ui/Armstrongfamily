The starting point · Easter's 1935 death certificate

A Name Survived. The Rest Is Still Being Recovered.

The strongest direct evidence for Rendy comes from her daughter, Easter. Easter's 1935 Texas death certificate identifies her mother by a name read in different ways across the surviving record pulls: Rendy Blayn in one reading and Rinde Bland in another.

That variation matters. This archive does not need to decide that one spelling is “the correct one” simply because it looks cleaner. The responsible approach is to preserve the forms actually encountered and search them as variants of the same research subject while the underlying original image and related records are examined.

Identity question · Name variants

The Name Itself — Unstable Across Every Reading

For this archive, Rendy, Rinde, Bland, and Blayn are retained as research variants. The point is not to declare that every historical appearance of those names belongs to this woman. The point is to prevent a spelling variation from closing a search before the evidence has been tested.

The “Bland” reading opened a new research path. Instead of assuming the name belonged to a particular enslaving family, the archive searched the surname independently. That search found genuine Bland families in Texas, but it did not produce a documentary bridge to Rendy.

Theory tested · Halifax County, Virginia

The Virginia Theory We Could Not Prove

Jacob Blane became an important working theory because the 1850 Halifax County slave schedule contains an enslaved woman whose reported age is compatible with Rendy's estimated birth year. Stephen Blain and Amanda Blain were also examined as possible relatives because of shared surname and geography.

But the documentary bridge never appeared. No record found so far identifies the woman in Jacob Blane's schedule as Rendy. No record found so far identifies her daughter as Easter. No record found so far establishes Stephen or Amanda as Rendy's siblings.

RESEARCH LEAD · NOT PROVEN

Jacob Blane is a theory, not Rendy's documented enslaver.

The age match is worth preserving because it explains why the theory was investigated. It is not sufficient to identify an unnamed woman with Rendy.

The disappearance of the two women from the Blane household between 1850 and 1860 is also unresolved. The old page described that disappearance as a sale. That wording is being retired. Without a deed, bill of sale, estate record, advertisement, trader record, or other direct evidence, we do not know what happened to the unnamed woman in the schedule.

Another lead · Fayette County, Texas

The Fayette County Question

A Blain family land-grant lead was also investigated in Fayette County, Texas, geographically close to Austin County. Earlier research used that proximity to suggest that Rendy could have been in Texas around the time of Easter's birth.

That argument is no longer being presented as fact. The specific land-grant evidence has not been independently re-verified to the standard required for an identity claim about Rendy.

STATUS · UNRESOLVED

Proximity is not continuity.

A Blain/Blane presence in the region would make the name worth investigating. It does not place Rendy in that family without a record connecting the people.

New direction · Austin County, Texas

The Bland Search Closed One Door

The “Rinde Bland” reading prompted a direct search for Bland families in the Texas records. Daniel Bland, born about 1827 in Kentucky, and his wife were examined through the 1870 census and their 1871 marriage record.

The marriage record was particularly useful because it established that Daniel Bland's wife, Jennie Miller, carried the Miller surname before that marriage. The Bland surname in that family therefore did not create a maternal line back to Rendy.

A separate Black Bland family holding land in Austin County by 1896 was also identified. It is a genuine historical family, but no documentary bridge currently connects it to Rendy.

RESULT

The surname search produced real people — but not Rendy.

This is useful negative evidence. A surname match alone cannot be used to manufacture an ancestry connection.

A second route into the question · Gardner → Grimes → Bell

Another Road Into Texas

A different research network now deserves attention. The Gardner, Grimes, and Bell families appear repeatedly in the historical and DNA research surrounding this branch of the family. The existence of that network is documented; the connection from that network to Rendy is not.

The current working reconstruction begins with John Gardner, born about 1755 in North Carolina, and his second wife, Easter James, born about 1758. Research indicates a daughter, Priss, and a son, Luke, who were enslaved. One active question is whether Priss — and possibly Luke — were later bequeathed to Eurydice Gardner, born about 1784 in North Carolina.

Eurydice's marriage to George Grimes places the Gardner and Grimes families together. Their daughter Abigail Grimes later married Thomas Bell, creating a documented Bellville-era family network that overlaps the period and place in which Easter's history unfolds.

Bellville · The enslaver question

The Bell Connection Is Worth Testing

Easter carried the Bell surname before her marriage to Joseph Nichols. That fact makes the Bell family an important research direction. It does not, by itself, prove how she acquired the surname.

Thomas Bell's household and estate records document enslaved people. Other Bell family members also appear in slavery-era property and estate records. The Bell family therefore belongs in the documentary search for the people who may have held Easter or members of her family.

CURRENT POSITION

Easter's Bell connection is a strong research lead.

The target record is still the direct bridge: a deed, estate inventory, will, bill of sale, probate distribution, household record, or other document naming Easter or an identifiable family unit containing her.

The archive will not fill that gap with an assumption. If the Bell connection is correct, the records should eventually give us a way to demonstrate it.

Easter's family · A separate documentary path

Katie Thornton — The Daughter Outside the Marriage

Easter's story does not end with the Nichols surname. She also had a daughter, Katie Thornton, outside her marriage to Joseph Nichols.

That fact opens another research network. The Thornton surname is not being treated as incidental. An Eli Thornton has already emerged in the research and needs to be tested against Katie's dates, location, household relationships, land, probate, and other documentary evidence.

THORNTON RESEARCH TARGET
Katie Thornton · daughter of Easter
Eli Thornton · previously identified research subject

Questions: relationship? residence? age? household? land? probate? witnesses? Bell/Grimes/Gardner overlap?

This is not a claim that Eli Thornton was Katie's father or that the Thornton family explains Rendy. It is a documentary path that may eventually tell us more about Easter's life and the community around her.

DNA evidence · Corroboration, not assignment

The DNA Signal

Gardner, Grimes, and Edmiston are surnames that recur among DNA matches connected to this broader research. That recurrence is meaningful as a research signal, but it cannot independently identify Rendy's parents, enslaver, or family relationship.

The correct use of the DNA evidence here is directional: it tells us which documented families may deserve deeper examination. The historical records must establish the relationship.

METHOD

DNA tells us where to look. Documents must tell us why.

A DNA match to a Gardner, Grimes, or Edmiston descendant is not, by itself, proof that Rendy belonged to that family. It becomes stronger when combined with a documented geographic, household, probate, land, or kinship bridge.

What has been ruled out · What remains open

The Search Did Not Fail. It Changed Direction.

Several explanations have been tested and weakened. The Jacob Blane theory has no documentary identity bridge. The Texas Bland families examined do not connect to Rendy. Rinda Hall was eliminated on chronology. John Nichols should not be presented as Rendy's enslaver.

That leaves the questions that matter most: where was Rendy born, who enslaved her, how did Easter enter the Bell network, and what does the Thornton connection reveal?

A Research Trail Still Open.

Rendy is not a finished story. She is a documented maternal relationship surrounded by unanswered historical questions. The archive will not turn a theory into a fact simply because the theory fits.

Jacob Blane remains a tested lead. Bland remains a surname trail. Gardner, Grimes, Edmiston, and Bell now form a separate research network to investigate. Katie Thornton and Eli Thornton open another path.

The goal is not to choose the most beautiful explanation. It is to find the record that makes the explanation defensible.

2026 Research Position

Do not merge Rendy into the Clarissa/Waldrop line yet. Do not identify an unnamed slave-schedule woman as Rendy without a continuity bridge. Do not identify John Nichols as her enslaver. Do not treat a Gardner, Grimes, Edmiston, Bell, Bland, or Blayn connection as established solely from surname recurrence or DNA.
