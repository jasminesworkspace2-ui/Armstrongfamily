ca. 1765 · Before the Documents

Before Anyone Wrote Her Name Down

She was born approximately thirty years before any document ever named her. The year was approximately 1765. Where she was born — which plantation, which county, which state — is not recorded anywhere that has survived. The first document that names her places her in Union County, South Carolina in 1793. Everything before that is silence.

What we know is what Newberry and Union Counties looked like in the South Carolina Upcountry in the years before the Revolution. Red clay soil along the Tyger River and the Enoree River. Dense pine and hardwood forest. A backcountry that had only recently been settled by families moving south and west from North Carolina and Virginia — families like the Sims and the Davenports and the Waldrops, who came from Granville County, North Carolina and brought their entire world with them, including the people they enslaved.

The community these families built along the Little River and the Enoree was tight. They witnessed each other's deeds. They appraised each other's estates. They married each other's children. They traded land and debts and the people they enslaved among themselves for generations. Patience lived inside this world from her birth. She knew these families. She had known them her whole life before any document ever said her name.

She had a name before she had a document. She had a life before anyone wrote it down. What that life was — who she loved, what she called herself before she was called Patience, what language her people spoke before all of this — the records do not say. They were never meant to say. The documents were not written to remember her. They were written to record transactions. She happened to be inside them.

March 22, 1793 · Union County, South Carolina

The First Time Her Name Appeared

She was approximately twenty-eight years old the first time anyone wrote her name down. It was March 22, 1793. A planter named James Sims Senior of Union County, South Carolina needed money — one thousand pounds sterling — and he went to a merchant named Peter Braselman of neighboring Newberry County to get it.

To secure the debt, Sims pledged everything he had. Eight hundred and fifty acres of land along the Tyger River. Ten horses, mares, and colts. Furniture. Hogs. Tools. And thirty-six people.

Among those thirty-six people, the deed listed Molley and her children: Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, Tom — and Patience.

Deed Book B · Page 467 · March 22, 1793
...also 36 negroes: Jack and Doll and their children Ambrose, Paul; Isaac and Tabby and their children Jack, Emille, Fanny, Robert; Molley and her children Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, Tom and Patience... Signed James Sims, Sr. Recorded 29 June 1793. Fredk. Nance, DC.
Newberry County Deed Books · Blacks Found in the Deeds of Laurens & Newberry Counties SC 1785-1827

She was listed as one of Molley's children. She was not Molley's child. She was approximately the same age as Molley — both of them around thirty years old, both of them grouped together because they had been together long enough that the deed writer understood them as family. Whatever they were to each other, the document reduced them to a line in a debt agreement. Collateral for a thousand pounds.

The deed book · Newberry County, South Carolina · This volume holds the first record of Patience's name anywhere in history

The debt was recorded by Frederick Nance, Clerk of Newberry County, on June 29, 1793. He would sign his name on every document that touched Patience's life in South Carolina. His handwriting appears on the first record of her name. It appears on the last. One clerk. One county. A woman's entire documented existence in South Carolina passed through the same man's pen.

James Sims Senior would be dead by November 1794. The debt would not be repaid before he died. And when a debt goes unpaid, the collateral is collected.

January 1, 1795 · Newberry County, South Carolina

New Year's Day

James Sims Senior died on November 27, 1794. Thirty-five days later — on the first day of a new year — the debt that had pledged Patience as collateral was settled the only way it could be.

It was January 1, 1795. New Year's Day. The Enoree River would have been cold. The red clay of Newberry County frozen at the edges. South Carolina winters are not harsh the way northern winters are, but they are grey. January is the quietest month. The fields are bare. The year has not yet started.

On this day, Moll, Patience, Hannah, Lin, Violet, Esther, and George were sold in open market to Wadsworth and Turpin, Merchants of Newberry County, to secure payment of a bond of £87 12 shillings 2 pence.

Deed Book C · Page 124 · January 1, 1795
...sold in open market to Wadsworth and Turpin, Merchants of the aforesaid County and State of South Carolina — one Negro wench named Moll about 30 years of age, Patience about 30 years old, Hannah about 12 years of age, Lin about 11 years of age, Violet 9 years of age, Esther 7 years of age and George 3 years of age — sold in open market to secure payment of £ 87 12 shillings 2p as by bond bearing date the 1 January 1795. Fredk. Nance, C.N.C.
Newberry County Deed Books · Blacks Found in the Deeds of Laurens & Newberry Counties SC 1785-1827
Deed Book C · Page 124 · The record of the open market sale · January 1, 1795 · Frederick Nance, Clerk

Seven people. Sold on the first day of the year to settle a debt that was not theirs.

Read the ages again slowly. Moll, thirty. Patience, thirty. Hannah, twelve. Lin, eleven. Violet, nine. Esther, seven. George, three years old.

George was three years old.

The document calls this an open market sale. Open market meant public. It meant witnessed. It meant recorded by Frederick Nance, who signed his name and filed the paper and went home that evening to his family while seven people stood in the cold on New Year's Day waiting to find out what happened next to them.

Seven people.
One debt.
£87 12 shillings 2 pence.

The document does not say how long they stood there.
It does not say what Moll and Patience said to each other.
It does not say what Esther, seven years old, understood about what was happening.

The document records the transaction.
Everything else happened anyway.

Wadsworth and Turpin were merchants. They did not buy people to keep them. They bought people to sell them. Seven months after this sale, Patience would be transferred again — specifically, individually, by name — to a new family. What happened to Moll, Hannah, Lin, Violet, Esther, and George after January 1, 1795 is not recorded anywhere we have found. The document named them. And then they disappeared from the record.

Only Patience can be traced forward.

August 4, 1795 · Newberry County, South Carolina

One Negro Girl Named Patience

Seven months after the open market sale, Francis Davenport Senior — a planter of the District of 96 and County of Newberry — gave a gift to his daughter.

Not Moll. Not Hannah. Not Lin or Violet or Esther or George. Patience.

Why her specifically? The document does not say. Francis Davenport on that same August 4, 1795 gave enslaved people to five of his other children — Isaac, William, David, Sarah, and Abigail — in a separate deed that distributed what he called "all of my slaves and household goods." Three days earlier he had given a girl named Eliza to his daughter Nancy Waldrop. He was distributing his estate among his children while he was still living. And Patience — specifically Patience, pulled from the group that Wadsworth and Turpin had acquired in January — went to his daughter Patty Waldrop.

Deed Book C · Pages 367-368 · August 4, 1795
Deed of Gift. Francis Davenport, Sr., of the District of 96 and County of Newberry, and State of South Carolina, planter, to Patty Waldrop, beloved daughter, of the State and County aforesaid — one Negro girl named Patience. Signed Francis Davenport. Wit. Isaac Davenport, Edward Turner, Elizabeth Towling. Proved by Isaac Davenport before Charles Griffin, J.P. August 5, 1795. Recorded August 18, 1795. Fredk. Nance, C.N.C.
Newberry County Deed Books · Confirmed by Newberry Davenport Data, Dr. John Scott Davenport
August 4, 1795 · Deed Book C:367-368 · Francis Davenport Sr. gives Patience to his daughter Patty Waldrop · Witnessed by Isaac Davenport · Recorded by Frederick Nance

The deed calls her one Negro girl named Patience. She was approximately thirty years old. The law did not change its language for her age.

Isaac Davenport — Francis's son, the same man who had bought enslaved people from Virginia merchants the year before — witnessed the deed and signed his name. The document was proven before Justice of the Peace Charles Griffin on August 5, recorded by Frederick Nance on August 18.

Patty Waldrop was the wife of Hezekiah Waldrop. The Waldrop family had come from Granville County, North Carolina — the same county where the Davenport family originated. They had been neighbors before South Carolina. They had been neighbors on the Enoree River. They witnessed each other's deeds. They bought at each other's estate sales. Patience may have known the Waldrops for years before Francis Davenport put her name in a gift deed and transferred her to them permanently.

She left South Carolina. She would never come back. The red clay and the Enoree River and the thirty years of her life in Newberry County were behind her now. The Waldrops were going west. And she was going with them.

1795 — 1827 · The Long Silence

Thirty-Two Years the Record Does Not Cover

Between the August 4, 1795 deed in South Carolina and the next document that names her in 1827, there are thirty-two years.

Thirty-two years the record does not cover.

What we know is this: the Waldrop family moved. South Carolina to Mississippi. The exact route, the exact year, the exact road — not recorded. But the 1827 document places Patience in the orbit of the Waldrop family in Mississippi, which means at some point in those thirty-two years, she traveled. Whether she walked. Whether she rode. Whether she was transported in a wagon with other people or walked behind it on a road she had never seen before — the document does not say.

What we know is that she had children. The 1827 document names them. Rachel. Clarissa. Tom. Rachel had a son also named Tom. Clarissa had children — William, Amanda, Harriet. Patience became a mother and a grandmother in those thirty-two years the record does not cover. She built something inside the silence. The documents could not see it. But it was there.

Patty Waldrop — the woman who received Patience as a gift in 1795 — died sometime before 1827. The next document comes from her husband Hezekiah, who survived her, distributing what remained of her estate to their children. In that distribution, Patience was named. Patience and everything that had come from her.

1827 · Mississippi

Patience and Her Increase

The document calls them Patience and her increase.

Increase was the legal word for children born to an enslaved woman. The law considered them property that had grown — like a crop, like livestock. But they were Rachel and Clarissa and Tom and William and Amanda and Harriet. They were people Patience had held and named and raised inside the thirty-two years the record did not cover.

Luke Waldrop Sr. Distribution Deed · 1827 · Mississippi
...paid by Claiborne P. Waldrop, Fanny Nichols (late Fanny Waldrop), James D. Waldrop and Stephen R. Waldrop, Willey B. Waldrop, Patsy Stoddard (late Patsy Waldrop), Sarah Robertson... children born of the body of my late wife, Patsey Waldrop... I have bargained and sold, given, released and confirmed unto them... the said negro slave, Patience and her increase named Rachel, Clarissa, Tom (Rachel's son), William, Amanda, and Harriet [children of Clarissa]...
Luke Waldrop Sr. Register · obcgs.com · Confirmed by DNA — 30+ Waldrop descendant matches

Read the names of the people who received them. Claiborne P. Waldrop. James D. Waldrop. Stephen R. Waldrop. Patsy Stoddard. Sarah Robertson. And second on the list — Fanny Nichols, late Fanny Waldrop.

Fanny was already married. She had already taken the name Nichols. She had married John Nichols — and John Nichols would take his family, and the people bound to them, to Bellville, Austin County, Texas.

Patience did not choose this. She did not choose the Waldrops in 1795 and she did not choose where her children went in 1827. But the chain of custody is documented. She was given to Patty Waldrop. Patty Waldrop died. Her estate was distributed to her children. And Fanny Waldrop Nichols carried Patience's family to Texas.

Into the household where, on July 4, 1848, a girl named Easter Bell would be born.

March 25, 1839 · Rankin County, Mississippi

The Last Time Her Name Appeared

She was approximately seventy-four years old.

The document is a deed transfer in Rankin County, Mississippi — the Waldrop estate distributing Patience and her family one final time. She is named. Clarissa is named. Rachel is named. Tom, William, Amanda, Harriet. A family that had grown from one woman sold on New Year's Day 1795 in Newberry County, South Carolina.

After March 25, 1839, Patience does not appear in any document we have found.

She was seventy-four years old. She had survived the mortgage. The open market sale. The transfer to the Waldrops. The journey from South Carolina to Mississippi. Thirty-two years of silence. The distribution of her children to different branches of the Waldrop family. The slow westward movement of everyone who held her. She had outlasted all of it.

She likely died before 1860. Her burial place is not recorded. The name she was called before she was called Patience — whatever name her people gave her in whatever world she came from before any of this — is also not recorded. The documents were never meant to preserve that. They were meant to record transactions. She happened to be inside them, and then she was gone from them, and then she was gone entirely.

Four documents.
Forty-six years.
South Carolina to Mississippi.

She never wrote a word of any of them.
She never chose any of what they recorded.

And yet — because of her —
everything that follows is possible.

We Are the Reason
Her Name Survived

The documents were not written to remember Patience. They were written by men who believed they owned her — to record debts, transfers, and transactions. Her name survived because she was inside those transactions. Four times in forty-six years, someone wrote her name down for their own purposes. Four times she survived what the writing recorded.

We are the fifth time. We are writing her name down for a different reason. Not as collateral. Not as property. Not as an item in an estate distribution. As the beginning. As the proof that something survived everything that was done to erase it.

This page was built from primary source documents. Every fact in it is sourced. The emotional weight of it is real — but it is not invented. The documents themselves carry the weight. We only read them slowly enough to feel it.
