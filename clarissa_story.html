ca. 1807 · Newberry County, South Carolina

Born Into the Same World as Her Mother

She was born approximately two years after her mother Patience was given to Patty Waldrop as a gift. The year was approximately 1807. The place was almost certainly Newberry County, South Carolina — the same red clay country along the Enoree River where Patience had spent the last twelve years of her life in the Waldrop household.

Clarissa did not come into the world as a free person. She was born enslaved, into a household that already held her mother. From the first day of her life, the Waldrop family had legal claim to her. She had not yet drawn her first breath before she was property.

What her childhood looked like — what Patience called her in their private language, what she learned from her mother, what she understood about her own situation — the documents do not say. They were never meant to say. The records that survive from this period are deed books and estate inventories. They named people when they were transferred. They did not record childhoods.

What we know is that she had a sister. Rachel. Both daughters of Patience. Both enslaved in the Waldrop household. They grew up together in Newberry County — and then they were moved together, from South Carolina to Mississippi, at some point in the years after 1807.

The exact year the Waldrop family left South Carolina is not recorded. But by 1827 — when the first document names Clarissa — she was in Mississippi. She had made that journey. And she had children of her own.

1827 · Mississippi

The First Time Her Name Appeared

She was born into the Waldrop household in Newberry County, South Carolina — the household that had received her mother Patience from Francis Davenport on August 4, 1795. The Waldrop family had witnessed that deed. Ezekial Waldrop stood in the room. Clarissa was born approximately two years after that transaction — into a household she would never leave by choice.

The Davenport community she came from was not small. On that same August day in 1795, Francis Davenport distributed his entire enslaved community among his children. Rachel went to one Davenport son. Mingo went to another. Tom went to a daughter. Nan went to another daughter. Clarissa's mother Patience went to Patty Waldrop. The people who had grown up together — who had shared the same fields and the same creek water and the same names — were separated in one afternoon. Clarissa was not yet born. She would enter a world where the community her mother came from had already been dispersed.

She was approximately twenty years old the first time any document ever wrote her name down. It was 1827. She was in Mississippi — in the Waldrop household that had moved west from South Carolina years before. And she already had children.

The document was a distribution deed — Luke Waldrop Sr. dividing the people he held among his children. Patience, Rachel, Clarissa, Tom, William, Amanda, Harriet. Named and distributed like livestock or land.

Luke Waldrop Sr. Distribution Deed · 1827 · Mississippi
...the said negro slave, Patience and her increase named Rachel, Clarissa, Tom (Rachel's son), Rachel's son William and Amanda and Harriet [children of Clarissa]... distributed to the children of the late Patsey Waldrop including Fanny Nichols (late Fanny Waldrop) and Claiborne P. Waldrop and James D. Waldrop and others...
Luke Waldrop Sr. Distribution Deed · 1827 · Mississippi · confirmed by DNA — 46 Waldrop descendant matches

Read the names carefully. William. Amanda. Harriet. These are Clarissa's children — named in a legal document not to honor them, but to distribute them as property. They were listed alongside their mother and their grandmother and their aunt Rachel as items to be divided.

Clarissa had three confirmed children by 1827. She was approximately twenty years old. She had already built a family inside captivity — and that family had already been named in a deed before any of her children were old enough to understand what that meant.

The deed distributed Clarissa and her children to Claiborne Parrish Waldrop — one of Patty Waldrop's sons, born 1798 in South Carolina. He would hold Clarissa and her family for the next thirty-eight years.

1839 · Rankin County, Mississippi

The Last Mississippi Record

Twelve years after the 1827 distribution deed, Clarissa's name appeared in Mississippi one final time. It was March 25, 1839 — a Rankin County estate transfer, Hezekiah Waldrop settling his affairs before his death.

Rankin County Mississippi · Deed Book 4 Page 435 · March 25, 1839 · Signed E.P. Waldrop
Verbatim transcription — confirmed primary source:

"Whereas Francis DEAVENPORT of the State of South Carolina by deed dated the 4th day of August, 1795, and duly recorded in the clerks office of Newberry County in the State of South Carolina on the 18th of August 1795, gave to my late wife PATTY WALDROP and her heirs in Tail a certain negro woman named Patience with her increase, and whereas since the death of my said wife I have worked said negro slave and her increase in Trust for the use of the children of my said wife without claiming title thereto, now in consideration of the premises and for the consideration of Ten Dollars in hand unto me paid by Claiborne P. WALDROP, Fanny Micholes (Nicholes?) late Fanny Waldrop, James D. WALDROP and Stephen R. WALDROP, Willy B. WALDROP, Patsy Stoddard late Patsy Waldrop, Sarah Robertson late Sarah Waldrop, Selina Robertson late Salena Waldrop, children born of the body of my late wife, PATSEY WALDROP I have bargained and sold given released & confirmed unto them the said children of my said deceased wife the said Negro slave Patience and her increase named Rachel, Clarissa, Tom, Rachel's son, William and Amanda and Harriet children of Clarissa and their future increase free from the claim of myself & of all persons claiming by or under me."

Signed E. P. WALDROP [seal] — Acknowledged before John Tate, Clerk of Court of Probate, Brandon Mississippi — Recorded March 25, 1839.
Rankin County Mississippi Deed Book 4:435 · March 25 1839 · Confirmed primary source · Received August 2026 via researcher Dawn

Critical Clarification · This Is Not A Freedom Document

This deed has sometimes been misread as granting freedom to Patience and her family. It does not. The phrase "free from the claim of myself and of all persons claiming by or under me" means E.P. Waldrop is releasing his personal claim to Patience's family and transferring them as property to Patty Waldrop's eight children. Patience, Clarissa, Rachel, Tom, Amanda, Harriet, and William were transferred as enslaved people — not liberated. Fanny Waldrop Nichols — who had already married John Nichols in Austin County Texas on October 14 1837 — was one of the eight recipients of this transfer. Clarissa's descendants came to Texas as Fanny's portion of this transfer. Joseph Nichols was born enslaved in Bellville because this deed transferred ownership to his mother Fanny — not freedom to the people she received.

Researcher Notes · Typed on the Original Document · Kathryn R. Bonner · Marianna Arkansas

— Typed annotation by Kathryn R. Bonner (Mrs. W.G.), Rt 3 Box 61, Marianna AR 72360. Her ancestor in this deed is Wiley B. Waldrop. This document traces Clarissa's family line all the way back to 1795 — to the deed that gave Patience to Patty Waldrop. Forty-four years of one family holding another family. Named and transferred and named again.

After March 25, 1839, Clarissa does not appear in any Mississippi record. The Waldrop family was moving west. Claiborne Waldrop — who held Clarissa — would leave Mississippi and settle in Sabine Parish, Louisiana by approximately 1841. His youngest child was born in Louisiana in 1841, confirming the family had moved by then.

Clarissa went with them. She had no choice.

1841 — 1865 · Sabine Parish, Louisiana

Twenty-Four Years in Louisiana

Claiborne Parrish Waldrop settled his family near Pleasant Hill — a town on the border between Sabine Parish and DeSoto Parish in northwest Louisiana, roughly twelve miles from the Texas state line. He acquired land. He built a plantation. And by 1850 he held nineteen enslaved people — Clarissa among them.

The 1850 federal census slave schedule recorded her without her name — as it recorded every enslaved person — only by age and gender. But the ages are consistent. And the household is Claiborne Waldrop's.

Ten years later, in 1860, the census taker came again. Claiborne Waldrop's household had grown from six enslaved people to eighteen. Children had been born in Sabine Parish. A decade of life had happened in those cabins near Pleasant Hill — births, deaths, bonds formed and broken — none of it recorded anywhere except in the count of bodies.

She was 51 years old. She had been enslaved for her entire life. She had been moved from South Carolina to Mississippi to Louisiana across five decades. She had watched her children grow up enslaved. She had watched grandchildren born into the same captivity.

And then the war came.

On April 9, 1864 — one year before emancipation — the Battle of Pleasant Hill was fought near the Waldrop plantation. Union troops came through Sabine Parish. The plantation world was disrupted. Claiborne Waldrop held 19 people in 1861. By the time the war ended, everything he had built was in collapse.

On April 9, 1865 — exactly one year after the Battle of Pleasant Hill — the war ended in the east. On June 19, 1865 — Juneteenth — the enslaved people of Texas were freed. Louisiana's liberation had come even earlier. Clarissa was free.

She was approximately fifty-eight years old. She had never been free before in her life.

1870 · Fort Jesup, Ward 4, Sabine Parish, Louisiana

Unknown to Herself

Five years after emancipation, a census taker walked through Ward 4 of Sabine Parish — the area around Fort Jesup, directly on the road between Many and Pleasant Hill. He was recording the first census in which the formerly enslaved people of Louisiana would be listed by name.

He came to household 118. He wrote down four names.

1870 U.S. Federal Census · Ward 4 · Sabine Parish, Louisiana · Household 118 · Rows 33–36
Madison Godwin — 34 — Male — Black — farm laborer — born Louisiana
Peter Godwin — 80 — Male — Black — at home (helpless) — born Georgia
Clarcy Godwin — 60 — Female — Black — keeping house — BIRTHPLACE: UNKNOWN TO HERSELF
Elzena Godwin — 10 — Female — Black — at home — born Louisiana
1870 U.S. Federal Census · Sabine Parish Louisiana Ward 4 · Household 118 · Page 15

Her name — written as the census taker heard it — was Clarcy.

Clarissa. Clarcy. The same name compressed by an ear that wrote what it heard and moved on.

She was sixty years old. She was keeping house. She had taken the surname Godwin — possibly from Peter, possibly from the Godwin family who were slaveholders in the same Sabine Parish community as the Waldrops, possibly from a reason the record never explains. She could not read. She could not write.

And when the census taker asked where she was born — the question that would have placed her in South Carolina, would have traced her back to Newberry County, to the household of Francis Davenport, to the red clay country along the Enoree River where Patience first held her — she said she did not know.

The census taker wrote what she told him: Unknown to herself.

She had been moved from South Carolina to Mississippi to Louisiana
across sixty years.

She had been sold, transferred, distributed, and moved
so many times that she lost her own origin.

The place where she was born
had been taken from her
along with everything else.

She did not know where she came from.
She told him so.
He wrote it down.

Unknown to herself.

After 1870, Clarissa does not appear in any record we have found. She would have been approximately seventy years old in 1880 — if she survived. She does not appear in the 1880 Sabine Parish census. Madison Godwin, who was thirty-four in 1870, also disappears. Peter Godwin, who was eighty, almost certainly died before 1880.

Clarissa died sometime in the early 1870s. In Sabine Parish, Louisiana, near Pleasant Hill — not far from the plantation where she had spent twenty-four years of her life. Her burial place is not recorded anywhere we have found. Her last document is a single line in a census book, written by a man who did not know her name and did not know her story and moved on to the next household before she had finished telling him what she could not remember.

How Clarissa Connects to Easter Bell · Two Lines · One Family

This is the part that confuses people — and it is important to hold clearly. Clarissa is not Easter Bell's grandmother in the direct line. Easter Bell came from a separate enslaved family line. The two lines merged when Joseph Nichols married Easter Bell on December 18, 1865 — six months after Juneteenth.

Line One · The Waldrop-Nichols Line Clarissa's Line

Patience → Clarissa → one of her children (William, Amanda, or Harriet) → Joseph Nichols — born ca. 1847, enslaved by John Nichols in Bellville Texas, DNA-confirmed descendant of Clarissa. Took the Nichols surname at emancipation.

Line Two · The Bell-Blayn Line Easter's Line

Rendy Blayn (born 1817, North Carolina) → Easter Bell (born July 4, 1838, Texas) — enslaved in John Nichols' household in Bellville. Took the Bell surname — likely from Thomas Bell, John Nichols' closest business associate.

December 18, 1865 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Joseph Nichols married Easter Bell.
Clarissa's line and Rendy Blayn's line
became one family.

The exact connection between Clarissa's confirmed children — Amanda and Harriet — and Joseph Nichols remains unresolved. William is Rachel's son per the 1839 primary source deed. The John Nichols 1864 probate record at Austin County Clerk (979-865-5911) is the document most likely to answer this question.

She Did Not Know
Where She Came From.
We Do.

When the census taker asked Clarissa where she was born, she told him she did not know. Sixty years of being moved and sold and transferred had taken that from her. The place where she came from — Newberry County, South Carolina, the household of Patty and Hezekiah Waldrop — had been erased from her own memory by the system that held her.

We know. We found the deeds. We found the slave schedules. We found the census entry that named her Clarcy and wrote down what she said about herself. We traced her from Newberry County South Carolina in 1807 to Fort Jesup Louisiana in 1870. We found her mother and her sister and her children and her grandchildren and the line that runs unbroken from her to the person reading this page.

She spent her whole life without knowing her own origins. This page exists so that her descendants never have to say the same thing about her.
