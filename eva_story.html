June 27, 1900 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Born on the John Nichols Survey

Eva Armstrong was born on June 27, 1900 in Bellville, Austin County, Texas. She was the daughter of Hellen Nichols Armstrong and John Armstrong — and through them the granddaughter of Easter Bell Nichols and Joseph Nichols, the great-granddaughter of Rendy Blayn and of Clarissa's line through whatever generation connected to Joseph.

She was born on the same ground where Easter Bell had been born enslaved sixty-two years earlier. The John Nichols Survey — Abstract 73 — was the land her great-grandparents had acquired after emancipation. It was the land the family had held since 1865. Eva was born free, on land her family owned, in a town that had been built on the grant that once belonged to the man who had held her grandmother in bondage.

Her father John Armstrong was 22 years old. Her mother Hellen was approximately 30. They were renting a home in Bellville in 1900 — confirmed by the census which shows no home ownership for their household. They were working people in a working town.

She was four months old when that census was taken. She would not remember Bellville. But Bellville would always be in her — in the land her family came from, in the name Easter Bell had carried forward from the Bell family whose land built the town, in the 2 acres that would eventually go back to Austin County in 2006 long after Eva was gone.

August 30, 1911 · Fort Worth, Tarrant County, Texas

Eleven Years Old
When Her Father Was Killed

Eva Armstrong was eleven years old when Jack Thompson shot her father dead.

John Armstrong was working as a delivery wagon driver for the Denver News Service in Fort Worth. On the evening of August 30, 1911 — at 6:30 p.m. — he was shot at three feet near the entrance of the Terminal Hotel on East Front Street. The bullet entered his mouth and exited through the top of his head. He was dead within three minutes.

Jack Thompson walked free on a $3,000 bond signed within hours by prominent white Fort Worth citizens including former police commissioner George H. Mulkey. The plea was self-defense. He was never convicted.

Eva was eleven years old. Her mother Hellen was approximately 41. They were alone in Fort Worth. The family had been living at 921 Arizona Avenue. After John's death they stayed — they had nowhere else to go and no resources to go anywhere.

She was eleven years old.
She watched her mother rebuild.
She watched her mother become Helen Gilbert.
She watched her mother keep going.

And then Eva kept going too.

The 1930 census finds Eva as the head of her own household at 1121 Pulaski Avenue in Fort Worth. She is 29 years old. Her mother Helen Gilbert — widowed — is living with her. James Ballard is 3 years old. Margie Ballard is 1 year old. Eva is the head of the household. She is holding everything together the same way her mother had held everything together after 1911.

Fort Worth · San Antonio · 1920s–1950s

James Wilson Ballard Sr.
and the San Antonio Years

Eva Armstrong married James Wilson Ballard Sr. — born August 26, 1897 in Palestine, Texas — the son of O.C. Ballard and Mary Ann Edwards Ballard, grandson of Rev. George W. Ballard, great-grandson of Newton Ballard who had been enslaved by Lewis F. Ballard in Smith County Texas.

Together they had six children — confirmed by birth certificates and death records. They moved from Fort Worth to San Antonio. They built a life on the East Side. James ran operations at 207 N. Center Street. Eva ran the Nip and Sip.

Eva Armstrong Ballard & Minor A. Green Minor Green knew the family since 1922 · later married Eva · witness on 1957 estate affidavit

The Nip and Sip was a bar and social gathering place on San Antonio's East Side. Eva ran it. She knew every family in that community. She knew the police. She knew the politicians. She knew who was honest and who wasn't.

The man in the photograph with Eva and James Wilson Ballard Jr. is Cornelius "Ice Cream" Armstrong Sr. — one of the most well-known figures on San Antonio's East Side. Orphaned at age three and raised by his mother's white employer, he got the nickname Ice Cream as a child because he ate so much of it. At 14 he went to work for Charles Bellinger — the powerful Black political boss of San Antonio's East Side — and spent the rest of his life in the Bellinger family's orbit, continuing under Valmo Bellinger after Charles died. Ice Cream was not a blood relative of Eva's — he shared the Armstrong surname by coincidence. He was a community figure in the same East Side world Eva and James operated in, connected through the Bellinger network that also touched 207 N. Center Street.

James Wilson Ballard Sr. died March 13, 1953 at Austin State Hospital. Eva had already been holding the family together for years before his death. After it, she continued.

A San Antonio Light newspaper article from March 8, 1954 names Eva Ballard in connection with a business license held under "Eva Smith" and names Margie Ballard regarding activity at 207 N. Center Street. Federal Gambling Tax Stamps — IRS Revenue Act 1951 — placed John Armstrong and James Ballard Jr. as Agents at that address with Bellinger as Principal. Eva was operating in a world where survival required navigating systems that were not designed for Black women on the East Side of San Antonio in the 1950s. She navigated them.

1955 · San Antonio, Texas

She Was Subpoenaed.
She Told the Truth.

In 1955 Eva Armstrong Ballard was subpoenaed to testify in a police brutality case in San Antonio. She was a witness. She knew what she had seen. She told the truth.

This is the sentence that has been in the archive from the beginning. It does not have a long explanation because it does not need one. A Black woman in San Antonio Texas in 1955 — one year after Brown v. Board of Education, the same year Emmett Till was murdered — was called to testify against the police. And she told the truth.

Her grandmother Easter Bell was born enslaved on the Fourth of July.
Her grandfather Joseph could not read or write but voted anyway.
Her father was shot dead by a white man who walked free.

Eva Armstrong Ballard stood in a courtroom in San Antonio
in 1955 and told the truth about what the police had done.

She knew exactly what she was doing.
She did it anyway.

The case involved seven witnesses and a grand jury. The newspaper documented it. Eva was named. She testified. The outcome of the case has not been confirmed by primary source — but Eva's presence and testimony is confirmed.

1950s–1960s · San Antonio to Garden Grove, California

California

At some point in the late 1950s or early 1960s Eva Armstrong Ballard moved her family from San Antonio to California. The exact year has not been confirmed by primary source. What is confirmed is that by the mid-1960s the family was in Orange County, California — Garden Grove specifically.

She had six children. She brought them west the same way thousands of Black families from Texas were moving west during the Great Migration — toward California's defense industry jobs, toward less explicit segregation, toward something that might be better.

Betty · Eloise · Eva · Margie · James Jr. The Ballard children with their mother Eva Armstrong Ballard · California

She also married again. Minor A. Green — who had known the family since 1922 — became her husband. He appeared as a witness on her 1957 estate affidavit. He is in the photographs. He was part of the family's California life.

Eva Armstrong Ballard died on November 24, 1977 in Garden Grove, California. She was 77 years old. She had outlived her first husband, her second, most of her siblings, and a century's worth of everything that had been done to her family before she was born.

She was buried in Santa Ana, California.

She Ran a Bar.
She Testified.
She Moved Her Family West.
She Left Six Children Behind.

Eva Armstrong Ballard was born carrying 105 years of her family's history. She did not choose Patience's enslavement or Clarissa's displacement or Rendy's sale or Easter's captivity or her father's murder or the Jim Crow world she grew up in. She was born with all of it already behind her.

What she chose was the Nip and Sip. The testimony. The move to California. The six children. The life she built from Fort Worth to San Antonio to Garden Grove across 77 years of the twentieth century.

Family Memory · Jasmine M. Lee · Approximately 1987 · Recalled 2026

The Dream

For years Jasmine believed she had attended a funeral as a very young child. She remembered it clearly — walking into a room, and a beautiful dark-skinned woman sitting in a dark green velvet chair, almost like a recliner. The woman had a veil over her face. You could see through it. Her skin was chocolate. Her presence was still.

The woman signaled to Jasmine silently — a quiet gesture with her hand — calling her over. Jasmine walked to her. The woman lifted the veil from her face and whispered in a voice soft as breath:

"I love you and everything will be okay."

About fifteen years ago Jasmine went to her mother Hjordis and asked about it — which funeral had they attended, who was the woman. When she described what she remembered, Hjordis went quiet. She said she recognized the woman immediately. She said that was Eva. Eva had been ill with cancer at the end of her life. She had a chair like that. And then Hjordis said something that stopped Jasmine completely.

You weren't at her funeral. I hadn't even had you yet. You weren't even thought about.

Eva Armstrong Ballard died November 24 1977. Jasmine was born January 25 1982. Four years and two months separated them. They never shared the same world. And yet.

People who knew Eva in life — including Hjordis's lifelong friend Barbara Ann Collins who had the opportunity to meet Eva — and family members including Michael Ballard and Ebony Boyd all independently noted that Jasmine is the only one in the family who looks like Eva. Michael Wallace wrote: "I swear I see Jasmine in Eva's face." Hjordis confirmed she has been saying the same thing for years.
