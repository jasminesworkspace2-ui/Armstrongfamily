John Nichols · Enslaved Community

A primary source reconstruction of the enslaved community held by John Nichols in Austin County, Texas — from his 1827 arrival through his 1864 death. This household contained our confirmed ancestors Rendy Blayn and Easter Bell Nichols.

Who Was John Nichols

John Nichols was born in Louisiana ca. 1797 — confirmed by the 1860 Austin County free population census. Before arriving in Texas he was in Williamson County, Tennessee from at least 1828 through 1835 — confirmed by primary source Chancery Court records found in 2026. In 1828 he purchased an enslaved woman named Harriet at public auction for $306 (Nichols vs. Hardeman and West, Williamson County Chancery Court, December 23, 1829). By 1830 the U.S. Federal Census slave schedule records him holding 24 enslaved people in Williamson County. A creditor named Benjamin W. Bedford obtained a legal attachment in 1832 against three people — Milley, Harriet, and their infant daughter Fanny — to satisfy a debt. John Nichols refused to surrender them. When the Williamson County Sheriff came to enforce the attachment in 1835, the return was marked Not Found. He had fled to Texas with those people (Bedford vs. Nichols, Williamson County Chancery Court, 1832–1835, FamilySearch Image Group 008479344). He arrived in Texas in 1827 — confirmed by the Stephen F. Austin Register of Families (Page 80) which records him as: John Nicholls, age 33, wife Fanny age 31, two children, one other dependent, farmer, origin Sabine, arrival 1827. That one dependent is the unnamed enslaved person who became the first member of the Nichols enslaved community in Austin County Texas.

He was granted one league and one labor of land by the Mexican government on October 20 1832. That land grant is not recorded in Austin County deed books — it originates in Mexican colonial records. He subsequently sold portions of his original grant to James and Thomas Bell — the Bell family whose name became attached to the town of Bellville. The 1837–1866 Austin County deed books and the 1825–1835 Spanish deeds are active research targets that may document transfers of enslaved people into and out of the Nichols household.

John Nichols died March 3 1864 — one year before emancipation. His children were Ellen, Sarah Jane, James William, Frances Elizabeth, Samuel Houston, and Caroline Mary. His wife was Frances Fanny Waldrop — daughter of Hezekiah Waldrop — whose family had held Patience and Clarissa in Newberry County South Carolina before migrating to Hinds County Mississippi and eventually to Williamson County Texas. The Waldrop family connection is the most significant finding in this research — documented across three states spanning 1795 to 1863.

His son Joseph Nichols married Easter Bell Nichols after emancipation. Both Joseph and Easter — and all of their children Allen, Josephine, Oscar, and Clara — are consistently recorded as Mulatto in post-emancipation census records. This reflects the documented reality of sexual violence by John Nichols against the enslaved women in his household — the census recorded the result as a Mulatto designation without naming what produced it.

Henry Nichols — who appears in the 1880 census immediately adjacent to Joseph and Easter in Precinct 1 Bellville — is almost certainly Joseph's brother from the same household. Henry was born ca. 1839 in Texas. He married Emmeline Nichols — born ca. 1842 in Shreveport, Louisiana — confirmed by her 1932 death certificate (Texas Death Certificate No. 19489, Austin County). Emmeline was not from the South Carolina or Tennessee chains — she came from Louisiana, confirming that the Nichols household contained people from at least three distinct origins: Tennessee (Milley, Harriet, and Fanny, brought when John Nichols fled Williamson County in 1835), South Carolina through Mississippi (the Waldrop community), and Louisiana (represented by Emmeline). Henry and Emmeline are buried at Jefferson Cemetery in Bellville alongside Easter Bell Nichols.

John Armstrong — who married Hellen Nichols Armstrong, Easter Bell's daughter — was the son of Dave Armstrong (born South Carolina) and Clara Morgan Armstrong (born North Carolina ca. 1848). Dave Armstrong married Clara Morgan in Austin County Texas on August 29, 1868 — confirmed by Austin County marriage record No. 376. Their son John Armstrong's connection to the Nichols community is confirmed by the death certificates of his sister Ida Twiggs (May 1918) and his mother Clara Armstrong (November 1918), both of which list him as informant and record Dave Armstrong's birthplace as South Carolina and Clara Morgan's birthplace as North Carolina. Dave Armstrong coming from South Carolina — the same origin state as the Waldrop-Davenport enslaved community — suggests he too was part of the broader community that traveled from South Carolina through the domestic slave trade corridor to Texas.

The Waldrop Connection · Three States · Seventy Years

Fanny Waldrop Nichols was born in South Carolina ca. 1802 — confirmed by the 1860 Austin County free population census which records her birthplace as South Carolina. This is the most significant finding in this research. Fanny Waldrop was born in South Carolina in 1802 — five years after Hezekiah Waldrop is documented in Newberry County South Carolina in 1797. She was born into the same South Carolina Waldrop household that held Patience and Clarissa. She grew up in that household. She married John Nichols on October 14 1837 in Austin County Texas — confirmed by researcher Kathryn R. Bonner who held the primary source marriage record — and came to Texas in 1827 — bringing enslaved people from that same community with her. The 1839 Rankin County Mississippi deed (Deed Book 4 Page 435) names Fanny Micholes — Fanny Waldrop Nichols — as one of eight recipients of Patience and her increase. That deed transferred Patience's family as property to Patty Waldrop's children. It did not free them. Fanny's share of that transfer is how Rendy Blayn and Easter Bell came to be in the John Nichols household in Bellville. The 1870 census records her birthplace as Tennessee — but the 1860 census is the stronger primary source being closer to the period of documentation. Her connection to the Newberry County South Carolina Waldrop family is now confirmed by primary source. The documented migration of the Waldrop family runs directly from South Carolina to Mississippi to Texas:

1792 — Francis Davenport sells an enslaved girl named Easther to James Davenport in Newberry County SC — witnessed by Richard Henderson Waldrop (Deed Book B: 629). The name Easter appears in the Davenport-Waldrop community 46 years before Easter Bell's birth.

1795 — Francis Davenport Sr. gives enslaved girl Patience to his daughter Patty Waldrop (Deed Book C: 367–368). Three days later he gives enslaved girl Eliza to his daughter Nancy Waldrop (Deed Book C: 369). Both deeds certified by Charles Griffin J.P. and Frederick Nance C.N.C.

1797 — Hezekiah Waldrop witnesses a Davenport deed in Newberry County SC — placing him in the same community as Patience and Clarissa.

1836 — Hezekiah Waldrop executes a Deed of Gift in Hinds County Mississippi — adjacent to Rankin County where the 1839 Waldrop deed distributed Patience and Clarissa.

1857–1863 — Hezekiah Waldrop purchases and sells land in Williamson County Texas — adjacent to Austin County where John Nichols held Rendy Blayn and Easter Bell.

The enslaved community that began with Patience in Newberry County South Carolina in 1795 — held by the Waldrop family — traveled the same migration corridor as the Waldrop family itself: South Carolina → Mississippi → Texas. Rendy Blayn and Easter Bell in the Bellville Nichols household in 1860 are the Texas endpoint of that journey.

How to Read This Reconstruction

Slave schedules did not record names — only age, sex, and color. This table cross-references the 1860 slave schedule with post-emancipation census records to make the best possible identifications. Every row is labeled with a confidence level. Confirmed Hypothesis means multiple primary sources support the identification. Hypothesis means the identification is plausible but not yet confirmed. Unknown means no identification has been made. No identification is presented as certain unless labeled confirmed by a named primary source document.

Historical Timeline · John Nichols Household
1827 — Arrival

John Nichols arrives in Texas from Sabine County

Travels with wife Fannie, two sons, two daughters, and one unnamed enslaved person — described as a "Negro dependent" in historical records. This is the first confirmed enslaved person in the Nichols household in Texas. Who this person was and what became of them has not been confirmed by primary source.

Source: Texas Historical Commission · Bellville townsite history

1832 — Land Grant

Mexican government grants John Nichols one league and one labor of land

Granted October 20 1832 by the Mexican government. This is not an Austin County deed — it originates in Mexican colonial land grant records. John Nichols later sells portions to James and Thomas Bell whose family name becomes attached to Bellville. The 1825–1835 Spanish deeds and 1837–1866 Austin County deed books are active research targets for documenting enslaved people transferred into or out of this household.

Source: Texas Historical Commission · Mexican land grant records · Active research target

1836 — Texas Revolution

John Nichols serves in the Texas Revolution · Captain R.H. McNutt's Company

John Nichols — also called Jack Nichols — served in the Texas Revolution that separated Texas from Mexico. He was in Captain R.H. McNutt's Company. In 1836 he furnished cattle to the Texas Army — an officer refused to give him or Jesse Stoddard receipts for cattle taken. Three witnesses confirmed his service under oath in 1876: James B. Pier, Nathaniel Reed, and S.G. Reams. His widow Fanny Waldrop Nichols received a state pension of $37.50 per quarter beginning in 1876 on the basis of his service.

Source: Texas Comptroller's Office Veteran Claim Files · FamilySearch Image Group 008700661 · Images 482–492 · Sworn affidavits 1876

1860 — Slave Schedule

John Nichols holds 9 enslaved people in Bellville Austin County Texas

The 1860 Federal Slave Schedule records 9 enslaved individuals in the John Nichols household — no names, only age, sex, and color. Cross-referenced with post-emancipation census records two identifications are supported as confirmed hypotheses: the Black female age 43 is consistent with Rendy Blayn, and the Mulatto female age 13 is consistent with Easter Bell Nichols.

Source: 1860 U.S. Federal Census Slave Schedule · Austin County Texas · Bellville · John Nichols household

1864 — Death

John Nichols dies one year before emancipation

John Nichols died in 1864. His probate file was searched at the Austin County Courthouse — the clerk reported no entry found in the probate index under any spelling variant of Nichols. The file may be lost, damaged, or filed under a different heading. His death one year before Juneteenth means the people he held were freed by General Order No. 3 in June 1865 — one year after his death.

Source: Austin County Clerk correspondence · Stephanie Kovar · 2026 · Probate file not located

1865 — Emancipation

Juneteenth · June 19 1865 · Bellville Austin County Texas

General Order No. 3 reaches Texas. The people held in the John Nichols household — including Rendy Blayn and Easter Bell — are freed. Easter Bell was approximately 17–27 years old depending on which birth year is accepted. Rendy Blayn was approximately 48 years old. The Bellville Countryman newspaper recorded in July 1864 that "Mill Creek bottom is said to be full of runaways" — the community was already moving toward freedom before the order arrived.

Source: General Order No. 3 · June 19 1865 · The Bellville Countryman · Vol. 4 No. 41 · July 5 1864

1876 — Fanny Waldrop Nichols · Indigent Widow

The woman who brought Clarissa's community to Texas applies for a soldier's pension — in poverty

Twelve years after her husband died, Fanny Waldrop Nichols — who had been born in South Carolina ca. 1802, who had received Patience's family in the 1839 Mississippi deed, who had brought the South Carolina enslaved community to Texas — was living in Travis County Texas in indigent circumstances. She applied for a pension as the widow of Texas Revolution soldier John Nichols. She signed her name with a mark — she could not write. Three witnesses confirmed her husband's service. The state granted her $37.50 per quarter beginning in 1876. By 1877 Sam H. Nichols — her son — was in Travis County managing her affairs. By 1879 James W. Nichols — another son — was writing to the Comptroller from Coleman County Texas. The family that held Easter Bell Nichols as property had scattered. The land was gone. The pension ran out within three years when the state appropriation was exhausted.

Source: Texas Comptroller's Office Pension File · FamilySearch Image Group 008700661 · Images 482–496 · Travis County Court November Term 1876

John Nichols · Texas Revolution Soldier · Land · Legacy

John Nichols · Texas Revolution · 1836

John Nichols — also known as Jack Nichols — served in Captain R.H. McNutt's Company in the Texas Revolution of 1836. Three independent witnesses confirmed this under oath in August 1876 when his widow Fanny applied for a pension: James B. Pier, Nathaniel Reed, and S.G. Reams all stated they served with him in the same company. A separate 1853 affidavit from Jesse W. Stoddard confirms that Nichols and Stoddard both furnished cattle to the Texas Army in 1836 and that the army officer refused to give receipts for the cattle taken.

Source: Texas Comptroller's Office · FamilySearch Image Group 008700661 · Images 482–492 · Image Group 008986113 · Image 576

John Nichols · Land Holdings · Peak Assessment

At peak John Nichols held 4,000 acres in Austin County — 300 acres from the Stephenson grant and 3,700 acres from the Stephen F. Austin and John Hodge colonial grant — plus Bellville town lots and Hempstead town lots. He held 5 horses, 75 cattle, paid poll tax. J.G. Bell acquired 240 acres from the Nichols original grant — confirmed by tax record as Original Grantee: Nichols, John — the primary source confirmation of the Nichols-to-Bell land transfer that became Bellville. John Nichols signed all documents with an X mark — he could not write. His widow Fanny also signed with a mark.

Source: Austin County Texas Tax Assessment Records · Multiple years

After 1864 · Two Fates · One Land

The White Nichols Family — Left

John Nichols died in 1864 leaving 4,000 acres. Within fifteen years the family had scattered. Sam H. Nichols was in Travis County by 1877 — witnessed his mother's pension transfer. Fanny Waldrop Nichols — the woman who brought Clarissa's community from Mississippi to Texas — was in Travis County by 1876, in indigent circumstances, drawing a $37.50 quarterly state pension as a soldier's widow. Both she and John had been unable to write — they signed everything with X marks. James W. Nichols was in Coleman County Texas by 1879, writing letters to the state Comptroller about his mother's pension. John W. Nichols bought 100 acres in Coleman County in 1891 for $650, paid it off by 1903. The 4,000-acre Austin County grant — the land named after them, the land Easter Bell held legal title to for 140 years — was gone from the family within a generation of John Nichols's death.

Sources: Texas Comptroller's Office pension file 1876–1879 · Coleman County TX Deed Book 29 p.71 · 1903 lien release

The Formerly Enslaved Community — Stayed

Easter Bell Nichols stayed in Bellville. She held legal title to a fractional interest in the John Nichols Survey Abstract 73 for 140 years after emancipation. She was still named on the deed — as Easter Pink — when Austin County foreclosed on her fractional interest in 2006. She died in Bellville in 1935 — 71 years after John Nichols died — on or near the same land she had been born enslaved on in 1838. Henry Nichols farmed beside his probable brother Joseph in Precinct 1 for decades. Emmeline Nichols owned her Bellville home in 1930, worth $300. The tax records show dozens of formerly enslaved community members holding fractional parcels of Nichols Abstract 73 land — small pieces of the same original grant — for generations after emancipation.

Sources: 2006 Tax Foreclosure Cause No. 97V-003 · 1930 Census · 1935 Texas Death Certificate · Austin County Tax Records

Priority 2 · 1837–1866 Austin County Deed Books

Deed records for the period between John Nichols's 1832 Mexican land grant and his 1864 death may document the transfer of enslaved people into or out of the Nichols household — including any bill of sale from a Blain or Bland family to John Nichols that would confirm Rendy's path from the Blain household to the Nichols household.

Priority 3 · 1825–1835 Spanish Deeds · Mexican Land Grant Records

John Nichols's original land grant was issued by the Mexican government on October 20 1832 — not recorded in Austin County deed books. Spanish colonial deed records and Mexican land grant archives may contain additional information about the Nichols family's enslaved community before Texas statehood.

Priority 4 · Freedmen's Bureau · Brenham Subdistrict · Texas

Austin County fell under the Brenham subdistrict of the Freedmen's Bureau. Labor contracts, ration records, and complaint reports from 1865–1870 may name Rendy Blayn and Easter Bell directly — the first post-emancipation records that would confirm their identities by name. Search on Fold3 under Texas Freedmen's Bureau — Brenham subdistrict.

Rendy Blayn · Origin Research Summary

Rendy Blayn's surname traces to a white family named Blain or Bland who held her before she came to the John Nichols household. Her surname is confirmed by Easter Bell's 1935 death certificate — the primary source that names her mother as Rendy Blayn. Easter's 1900 census lists her mother's birthplace as Arkansas. Easter's 1910 census lists her mother's birthplace as North Carolina. The discrepancy between these records has not been resolved.

A John Blain received a Class 3 headright land grant in Fayette County Texas — the county directly adjacent to Austin County — arriving between October 1837 and January 1840. Easter Bell was born in Texas on July 4 1838. A Blain family arriving in adjacent Fayette County in 1837–1838 bringing Rendy from Arkansas is consistent with that timeline.

Critically — John S. Blain also appears in the 1850 census in Milam, Sabine County Texas — and John Nichols arrived in Austin County from Sabine County in 1827. The Blain and Nichols families may have known each other before either arrived in Austin County — both with documented Sabine County connections. A bill of sale from a Blain family to John Nichols — transferring Rendy — may exist in either Sabine County or Austin County deed records.

After emancipation the Bland family — Horace Bland born Kentucky — lived immediately adjacent to Joseph and Easter Nichols in the 1880 and 1900 censuses in Bellville. In 1880 Fayette County Almedia Blain — a Black woman born 1822 in Kentucky — appears with her father's birthplace recorded as Africa. She is the same generation as Rendy and shares the Blain surname — suggesting a shared enslaved community origin.

Emeline Nickols · Last Confirmed Survivor of the Household · Born ca. 1847

In 1930 — 65 years after emancipation — an 83-year-old Black woman named Emeline Nickols was living in Bellville, Austin County, Texas. She owned her home. It was worth $300. She could not read or write. She worked as a laundress. Her granddaughter Zelma Whitmere, age 15, lived with her.

Emeline Nickols was born ca. 1847 in Texas. Her parents were both born in Texas — meaning they were among the earliest generation of enslaved people in Austin County, possibly arriving with John Nichols from Sabine County in 1827. The 1860 John Nichols slave schedule lists a Black female age 11 born ca. 1849 in his household — consistent with Emeline within two years of census age rounding. She took the Nickols name after emancipation because that was the household she was freed from.

She lived in Bellville for 83 years. She outlived John Nichols by 66 years. She outlived the Civil War, Reconstruction, Jim Crow, and into the era of radio — which she did not own. She owned her home in Bellville in 1930 on or near the same land the John Nichols household had occupied since 1827. The unnamed "Negro dependent" who arrived with John Nichols in 1827 per the Stephen F. Austin Register of Families may have been Emeline's mother — the first generation of this family on this land. Emeline was the last confirmed survivor with living memory of that household.

January 27 1868 · Joseph Nichols Registers to Vote · Bellville Austin County Texas

Less than three years after emancipation — Joseph Nichols registered to vote in Bellville, Austin County, Texas on January 27 1868. James W. Nichols registered the following day on January 28 1868. These are primary source documents showing formerly enslaved men in the John Nichols community exercising their rights as citizens during Reconstruction. Joseph Nichols — Easter Bell's husband — born ca. 1843 in Texas — registered as a voter in the same Bellville community where he had been enslaved.

Source: Texas Voter Registration Lists 1867–1869 · Joseph Nichols · January 27 1868 · Bellville Austin County Texas · James W. Nichols · January 28 1868

The Land · 140 Years · Lost in 2006

Primary Source · 2006 Tax Foreclosure · Cause No. 97V-003 · Austin County Texas

The people John Nichols held as property inherited his land after emancipation. The John Nichols Survey — Abstract No. 73 — originally granted by the Mexican government on October 20 1832 — descended in fractional interests to the formerly enslaved community and their descendants. Easter Bell Nichols, her children, and her grandchildren held legal title to a piece of that land for over 140 years.

In 2006 Austin County filed a tax foreclosure suit — Cause No. 97V-003 — in the 155th Judicial District Court of Austin County, Texas. The land sold at foreclosure on August 1 2006. The named defendants — the legal heirs — were the descendants of the people John Nichols had enslaved:

Easter Pink (Easter Bell Nichols) · Allen Nichols · Oscar Nichols · Clara Nazy (Clara Nichols Nagy) · Joseph King · Charley King · Annie King (Annie Nichols King) · Helen Armstrong a.k.a. Helen Gilbert (Hellen Nichols Armstrong) · Joseph Nichols · Willey Nichols · Myrtle T. Everline

The land transferred to the Bellville Independent School District. The title chain traces to Volume 209 Page 228 of the Austin County Deed Records — the fractional 2-acre split from the original 1832 John Nichols Survey master grant.

This foreclosure document also provides the third independent primary source confirmation that Helen Armstrong and Helen Gilbert are the same person — named together in a legal court filing in 2006. Combined with Death Certificate No. 54237 and the Find a Grave memorial, this identification is now confirmed by three independent primary sources.

Source: Cause No. 97V-003 · 155th Judicial District Court of Austin County Texas · Sheriff R. DeWayne Burger grantor · recorded November 1 2006 · John Nichols Survey Abstract No. 73 · ACAD Account No. R20014 · Austin County Deed Records Vol. 209 Page 228

Research Note · Volume 209 Page 228 · Austin County Deed Records

The John Nichols 1864 probate file was not found in the Austin County courthouse index — but the land descended through deed, not probate. Volume 209 Page 228 of the Austin County Deed Records contains the chain of title showing how the Nichols Survey land transferred to Easter Bell and the other heirs. This deed record is an active research target and may document the exact transfer from the Nichols estate to the formerly enslaved community after emancipation.
