Molley

Molley is the oldest confirmed ancestor in this family's documented line. She was enslaved by James Sims Sr. of Union County, South Carolina. On March 22 1793 — when James Sims mortgaged 850 acres of land and 36 enslaved people to Peter Braselman of Newberry County — Molley was listed by name alongside her children.

Her children were: Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, Tom, and Patience.

She kept seven children. In a world that separated families without warning or recourse — in a world where children were collateral, where bodies were currency, where love had no legal standing — Molley raised seven children and held them together long enough for all seven names to appear in the same document. That document is a mortgage. It recorded her family as property. But it also recorded her family as a family. And it survived.

Molley was born approximately 1750 to 1770. Where she was born — whether Virginia, North Carolina, or South Carolina — is not yet known. How she came to be in James Sims's household in Union County is not yet documented. She may have come from an earlier estate, been purchased at sale, or been born into the Sims household. That search continues.

What happened to Molley after 1793 is not yet confirmed by primary source. When Patience was given away to Patty Waldrop in 1795 — two years after the mortgage — Molley and her other children were not named in that transaction. Whether Molley remained in the Sims household, was sold, or was eventually freed is not yet known. What is known is that she existed. She had a name. She had seven children. And one of them — Patience — carried her line forward across two centuries to the present day.

The Document That Named Her

On March 22 1793 James Sims Sr. of Union County South Carolina mortgaged his land and his people to Peter Braselman of Newberry County — a merchant to whom he owed one thousand pounds sterling. The document lists 850 acres of land and 36 enslaved people as collateral.

The 36 people are listed in family groups — couples with their children named together, mothers with their children, individuals listed by name. This organization reflects how the enslaved community itself was structured — and how the people in it were known to those around them. Molley is listed as a mother. Her children are listed with her. That is the primary source record of their relationship.

Deed Book B · Page 467 · March 22 1793 · Union County South Carolina · Recorded June 29 1793
James Sims, Senr. of Union County, State of South Carolina, planter, to Peter Braselman of Newberry County, South Carolina, Merchant. In consideration of £1000 Sterling — one tract of land in Union County containing 600 acres; 150 acres on Tyger River and another 100 for a total of 850 acres; also 36 negroes:

Jack and Doll and their children Ambrose, Paul;
Isaac and Tabby and their children Jack, Emille, Fanny, Robert;
Molley and her children Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, Tom and Patience;
Big Lucy and Daniel, Tabby, Peter, Desly, and Major and Lucy;
Jenny and her children Mary, Henry, Peter;
Delsey and her children Morning and Daniel, Isaac, Lucy, Amy, Frank and Doll;

also ten horses, mares and colts, furniture, hoggs, tools...

Signed James Sims, Sr. Wit: James Sims jr., Robert Powell. Recorded 29 June 1793. Fredk. Nance, DC.

James Sims also gave nine enslaved people to his daughter Drucilla Braselman — Peter's wife — on June 26 1793, recorded just days before the mortgage. The Sims and Braselman families were connected by marriage. The 36 people mortgaged — including Molley and her children — were caught between a debt and a family transaction that would determine their fate.

What happened to the mortgage after 1793 has not yet been documented. Whether Sims discharged the debt and kept his people — or whether Braselman foreclosed and the 36 were sold — is the research question that explains how Patience came to be in Francis Davenport's household by 1795. That document, if it exists, is still to be found.

Primary source · Newberry County South Carolina Deed Book C Pages 367-368 · August 4 1795

Patience

Patience — Molley's daughter — was born in South Carolina approximately 1765 to 1780. She grew up alongside her brothers Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, and Tom. By 1793 she was old enough to be listed as an individual in the mortgage — not as a child within a smaller family unit but as a person in her own right, named beside her brother Tom.

Two years after the mortgage — on August 4 1795 — Francis Davenport Sr. of the District of 96 and Newberry County gave Patience to his daughter Patty Waldrup as a deed of gift. How Patience came from the Sims mortgage in Union County to the Davenport household in Newberry County in those two years has not yet been documented. The transaction that transferred her — whether a sale, a foreclosure, or an inheritance — is the missing link in this chain.

What is confirmed is that her brother Tom came with her. Tom is listed in the 1793 mortgage alongside Patience. Tom appears in the 1839 Mississippi deed alongside Patience. Across forty-six years — two states — multiple households — Tom and Patience stayed together. Brother and sister. They did not lose each other.

Deed Book C · Pages 367-368 · August 4 1795 · Newberry County South Carolina
Deed of Gift. Francis Davenport, Sr. of the District of 96 and Newberry County to his beloved daughter Patty Waldrop — transferring one negro girl named Patience.

Witnessed by Isaac Davenport, Edward Turner, and Elizabeth Towling.
Proved before Charles Griffin, J.P.
Recorded in Deed Book C, Pages 367-368.

Francis Davenport Sr. was born in Virginia in 1718 and died in Newberry District South Carolina on July 23 1798 — three years after giving Patience to his daughter. He was 77 years old when he made that deed of gift. Patience was one of the last things he gave away before he died.

Patty Waldrop — Patience's new enslaver — was the daughter of Francis Davenport. The Waldrop family had been in Laurens County South Carolina since at least 1772 when James Waldrop Sr. received a land grant on the Enoree River. The Davenport and Waldrop families were neighbors in the same Upcountry South Carolina planter community — Laurens and Newberry counties sharing a border, the same rivers, the same Baptist churches, the same network of land and labor and debt.

In that household Patience had a daughter. Her name was Clarissa.

Primary source · August 11 1827 · Yazoo County Mississippi · March 25 1839 · Rankin County Mississippi

The Long Road West

The Waldrop family moved west. This was the pattern of the era — white planter families following land prices and opportunity from the exhausted soils of the Carolinas to the rich cotton fields of Mississippi, carrying their enslaved people with them. Patience had no choice in the matter. Neither did Tom. Neither did Clarissa.

On August 11 1827 Hezekiah Waldrop — Patty's husband — transferred Patience, Rachael, and Thomas to his son Stephen R. Waldrop of Yazoo County Mississippi. The family was moving. Patience was going with them. Tom — listed as Thomas — was going too.

Patty Waldrop died at some point before 1839. When she died, everything she owned — including Patience and all of Patience's descendants — passed to her children. On March 25 1839 Hezekiah Parrish Waldrop executed a formal declaration in Rankin County Mississippi confirming that ownership for his and Patty's children. He cited the original 1795 Davenport deed as his legal foundation.

That 1839 declaration names: Patience and her increase — Rachol, Clarissa, Tom — and Clarissa's children — William, Amanda, and Harriet.

Three generations. One document. Forty-four years after Francis Davenport gave Patience to his daughter — the same Patience, the same family, named together in Mississippi. They had not been separated. They had not lost each other. Molley's daughter and Molley's son and Patience's own daughter and grandchildren — all still together.

The Unbroken Line

Most African American families searching their history hit a wall at 1870 — the first census that recorded Black Americans by name. Some break through to 1865. A very few find documents from before the Civil War.

This family's confirmed documented line goes back to 1793. To a woman named Molley in Union County South Carolina. To her daughter Patience. To Patience's daughter Clarissa. To Clarissa's descendant Easter Bell — who was born July 4 1838 and lived to age 97 and died free and remembered. To Hellen. To Eva. To Margie. To Hjordis. To Jasmine.

Nine generations. Two hundred and thirty years. One family. Every generation documented by primary source.

A note from the compiler

What It Means to Find Them

Most African American families cannot get past 1870. The records before that year were not kept for Black people — they were kept for the people who owned them. Finding your ancestors in those records means searching the documents of the people who enslaved them, reading your family's names in ledgers of property and debt, finding them in the margins of other people's lives.

That is what this research required. And that is what it found.

Molley's name is in a mortgage document from 1793 because James Sims needed money and used his people as collateral. That is a brutal fact. But her name is there. Her children's names are there. And because those names are there — because someone wrote them down in a deed book in Union County South Carolina on a March day in 1793 — we know them now. Two hundred and thirty years later. We know Molley had seven children. We know Patience was one of them. We know Tom was another and that he stayed with his sister across two states and half a century.

They did not know anyone would look for them. They did not know their names would survive. They survived anyway. That is not a small thing.

This archive exists because Jasmine M. Lee looked for them. Seven years of research. Primary source by primary source. County courthouse by county courthouse. Census record by census record. DNA match by DNA match. She found them.

Molley. Patience. Tom. Clarissa. Easter Bell. Hellen. Eva. Margie. Hjordis. Jasmine.

The line is unbroken.

"I did something most Black people aren't able to do. I'm hurt to know what they went through — but at peace knowing they had each other."
