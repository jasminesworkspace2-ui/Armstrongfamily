her story ↓

ca. 1817 · North Carolina

Before Any Document Named Her

She was born approximately two hundred and nine years ago. The year was approximately 1817. The place was North Carolina — confirmed by the one document that mentions her, which records her daughter's birthplace as Texas and her own birthplace as unknown.

North Carolina in 1817 was a state in the full machinery of slavery. The domestic slave trade — the buying and selling of people within the United States after the international trade was banned in 1808 — was at the height of its cruelty. Families were separated. People were sold south. Children were taken from mothers. Rendy was born into this world.

What family she was born into — who her mother was, who her father was, what name they gave her before she was given the name Rendy — the documents do not say. They were never meant to say. The records that survive from this period name enslaved people when they were transferred. They did not record births into bondage as anything other than an increase in property value.

At some point — perhaps as a child, perhaps as a young woman — Rendy came into the household of the Blane family in Halifax County, Virginia. Whether she was born there, sold there, or brought there as a child is not recorded. What we know is that by 1850, she was in Halifax County, Virginia. And she had a daughter named Easter.

ca. 1838 · Halifax County, Virginia

Easter

Easter Bell was born approximately 1838. Her birthplace — confirmed by her death certificate — was Texas. But the 1850 census places a woman of Rendy's age and a girl of Easter's age in Jacob Blane's household in Halifax County, Virginia.

This is the only window we have into their life together before Texas. Two women — a mother approximately 33 years old and a daughter approximately 11 — living in a household of more than twenty enslaved people in the Banister district of Halifax County, Virginia. The land around them was tobacco country. The Blane family had a mill and farming operations near Cedar Grove. Rendy and Easter worked in that world for years.

The other people in that household — the ones who stayed — were almost certainly family. Stephen Blain, born ca. 1835 in Virginia, would go on to head his own household in Halifax County after emancipation. Amanda Blain, born ca. 1844, lived in the same household as Stephen in 1870 at Mount Carmel. They took the Blane surname — the name of the family that held them — as many formerly enslaved people did.

Stephen and Amanda stayed. Rendy and Easter did not.

Between 1850 and 1860
Rendy and Easter disappeared from Jacob Blane's household.

They did not leave.
They were sold.

The specific record of that sale —
the deed or the bill of sale or the trader's manifest —
has not been found.

What we know is only this:
they were in Virginia in 1850.
They were in Texas by 1860.
Everything in between is the silence of the domestic slave trade.
By 1860 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Texas

By 1860, Rendy and Easter were in Bellville, Austin County, Texas — in the household of John Nichols, one of the earliest Anglo settlers of Austin County, who had received a massive land grant from the Mexican government in 1832. The town of Bellville was built on land he had sold to the Bell brothers. Thomas Bell — the man whose name Easter would eventually carry — had been John Nichols' trusted legal witness as far back as 1845.

She was approximately 43 years old. She had traveled — whether she walked or was transported by wagon or by river barge, whether she was separated from Easter during the journey or kept with her, whether she knew where she was going — from Virginia to Texas. From the tobacco country of Halifax County to the cotton and cattle country of Austin County. A journey of more than a thousand miles that no one recorded and that she had no choice in making.

Easter was 22 years old in 1860. John Nichols was 63 years old. His wife Fanny Waldrop Nichols — the daughter of Hezekiah Waldrop who had held Patience and Clarissa — was 58 years old. The two enslaved family lines that would merge in 1865 were now living under the same roof. Clarissa's descendants were in that household. Rendy's daughter Easter was in that household. They did not yet know what the next five years would bring.

John Nichols died on March 3, 1864 — one year before emancipation. Rendy was approximately 47 years old when he died. The household she had been sold into was dissolving. The war was in its final year. Everything was about to change.

June 19, 1865 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Free

Juneteenth. June 19, 1865. General Gordon Granger arrived in Galveston, Texas and read General Order No. 3. The people enslaved in Texas were free.

Rendy was approximately 48 years old. She had been enslaved her entire life. She had been born in North Carolina, held in Virginia, sold to Texas. She had watched her daughter grow up in bondage. She had survived the domestic slave trade, the Civil War, and forty-eight years of a world that treated her as property.

She was free.

Six months later — on December 18, 1865 — Easter Bell married Joseph Nichols in Bellville. Easter had taken the surname Bell — almost certainly from Thomas Bell, John Nichols' closest business associate, whose family's name was on the town itself. Easter did not take the Blayn surname. She did not take the Nichols surname. She took Bell — the name of the land, the name of the community, the name that was everywhere around her in Bellville.

But she remembered her mother's name. She kept it. Not as a surname — as a piece of information she carried forward.

After 1865, Rendy does not appear in any census record we have found. She does not appear in the 1870 Austin County census. She may have lived with Easter in the years after emancipation. She may have died before 1870. She was approximately 53 years old in 1870 — not old, but the years of bondage had taken their toll on every formerly enslaved person who survived emancipation.

The record goes silent. The woman who had survived everything — the sale from Virginia, the journey to Texas, forty-eight years of captivity — disappears from the documentary record after 1865.

The Only Document That Names Her

Seventy Years Later,
Her Granddaughter Wrote Her Name Down

Clara Nezy was Easter's daughter. She had never known her grandmother Rendy — Rendy almost certainly died before Clara was born. What Clara knew was a name she had heard her mother say. She wrote it down the way it sounded: Candy Blayn. The census taker's ear, the phonetic spelling, the name compressed by time and distance — Candy for Rendy, Blayn for Blain. This is the only place in any official record where Rendy's name appears.

What the Research Found

The People She Left Behind —
And the Trail That Led Back to Her

In Virginia · The Family Who Stayed

Stephen Blain — born ca. 1835 Virginia — head of household · Mount Carmel, Halifax County · 1870. Almost certainly Rendy's brother or another close family member. He took the Blane surname and stayed in the community where he had always lived.

Amanda Blain — born ca. 1844 Virginia — living with Stephen in 1870. Age 26. She was approximately 6 years old when Rendy was sold away. She grew up in the same Halifax County community without her.

In Texas · The Family Who Carried Her Name

Easter Bell — born July 4 1838 — Rendy's daughter — married Joseph Nichols December 18 1865 — had nine children — lived 97 years — died December 17 1935 in Bellville Texas on the same land where she was born enslaved. She never forgot her mother's name.

Clara Nezy — born Clara Nichols 1875 — Easter's daughter — Rendy's granddaughter — the woman who wrote the name down on the death certificate in 1935. She is the reason Rendy's name survived at all.

One Name.
Written Phonetically.
By a Granddaughter Who Never Met Her.

Clara Nezy was born in 1875. Rendy Blayn almost certainly died before Clara was born. What Clara knew about her grandmother was a name she had heard her mother Easter say out loud — the way Easter said it, the way it sounded in the Bell household in Bellville, Texas, in the decades after emancipation. When the death certificate was placed in front of her in 1935, Clara wrote what she had heard: Candy Blayn.

The census taker's spelling became the archive's only record. A phonetic compression of a name that had already survived the domestic slave trade, a journey of a thousand miles, and the death of the woman who carried it.

Rendy survived long enough for Easter to remember her name. Easter survived long enough to say it to her children. Clara survived long enough to write it down. And now it is here — spelled correctly, sourced, placed in the archive where it belongs, alongside every other name this family kept alive through everything that was done to erase them.
