Austin County, Texas · Why We Started Looking

The Bells of Bellville.

Easter Bell took that name at freedom. Somewhere in Bellville, a white Bell household held her before 1865 — the same way John Nichols held Joseph. But which Bell? Thomas and Abigail's? John G.'s hotel? A cousin's farm across town? The town had more than one Bell family in it, all landed, all related by name if not always by document, and Easter's own record never says which one raised her. That question is what sent this research into every Bell household Austin County left behind — and it kept turning up more Bells than we expected, some connecting cleanly, some not connecting at all yet, and at least one — a Black father and son named Bell, born right in the middle of slavery — that we haven't been able to let go of. This page follows that search all the way through, in the order it actually happened, not cleaned up after the fact.

Why This Page Exists

People who share a surname, settle the same small town, and name that town after themselves did not arrive as strangers to one another. When a Black or mixed-race child is born in that same town, during or immediately after slavery, carrying that same surname — that is not coincidence requiring a separate document to "prove." It is the expected, ordinary outcome of what slavery actually was. This page treats every Bell found in Austin County research as part of one connected story, whether or not a will or deed spells out the exact relationship between any two individuals. Austin County's own records are thinner and harder to access than many neighboring counties' — a gap that is itself worth noting, not explaining away.

Starting Where Easter Started — Thomas & Abigail's House

This is the household the search began with, because it's the one Easter's own age most cleanly fits into.

Founding Family Thomas Bell b. ca. 1802, North Carolina · d. January–May 1858, Bellville · Texas Revolution veteran

Co-donor of the Bellville townsite, land purchased directly from John Nichols. Married Abigail Grimes (b. 1810, Sumner Co., TN — d. 1860, Bellville), daughter of George Grimes and Eurydice Gardner. Adopted John Gideon Slade (age 33, from Florida) as legal heir, January 4, 1854 — a documented legal adoption, not a blood relation, per the Texas State Historical Association's own record.

His January 1858 will named two enslaved men directly: Henry — willed to Abigail for four months, then to John G. Bell, with a clause allowing him to "choose his master" if dissatisfied. Mat — willed to Abigail for her life, then to grandson Thomas J. Bell. The will was contested by Abigail herself, who argued she and Thomas had already executed a full community-property partition in 1856. The contest was ongoing as of the will's probate in May 1858.

One household wasn't the whole town, though. Bellville had Bells everywhere — different farms, different generations, not all of them obviously related to Thomas and Abigail on paper. Ruling each one out, or in, meant checking all of them.

Married Calpernia Shellbourne (native of Alabama). Served in the Mexican War, 1846–48. Four children: a daughter (Mrs. R. Wit—), a daughter (Mrs. C.H. Bethany), John P. No enslaved people found under A.J. Bell's name in the 1850 slave schedule index — either he held none, or his household is recorded under a name variant not yet located. This remains an open, unresolved gap given his documented position and long tenure in the county.

Named in the 1869 Freedmen's Bureau report on racial violence in Bellville as one of the men "whispering John" (J.G. Bell) and this family held long, entrenched political control over — Austin County's treasury key, sheriff's office, and county judgeship all passing through this same extended white Bell network for decades after emancipation.

Then We Started Looking for Them, Not Just the Bells Who Owned Them

Every white Bell household is only half the question. The other half is who came out of it carrying the name. Easter's own trail is documented in full elsewhere on this site — the rest of what turned up while looking is here.

Direct Ancestor Easter Bell (Nichols, later Pink) b. July 4, 1838 · d. December 17, 1935 · Bellville

Fully documented elsewhere on this site. Traced through the Thomas/Abigail/John G. Bell household by consistent age progression: age 12 (Bell hotel, ~1850), age 12 (A.G. Bell's 1850 slave schedule), age 19 (J.G. Bell's 1860 schedule). Mother: Rendy Blayn. Took the Bell surname at emancipation. Married Joseph Nichols, December 18, 1865.

Confirmed, Community Ned Bell b. ca. 1835/1843 (records vary), South Carolina · d. May 20, 1912, Bellville · Fisherman

Documented across 1880, 1900, and 1910 census records. In 1900, listed alongside Jefferson Nagy (b. 1811, Louisiana), no stated blood relation. Parents both South Carolina-born per 1900 census.

Married Into Nichols Family Edward Bell Married Eliza Nichols, Austin County Freedmen's Marriage Register, Entry No. 7 · December 10–11, 1865

Married the same week as Joseph Nichols and Easter Bell (Entry No. 8). Eliza's surname was Nichols before marriage — very likely enslaved in the same John Nichols household as Joseph. By 1870: Edward Bell (24), Eliza Bell (22), son Charles Bell (5), Dwelling 430, Bellville.

The Reason This Page Exists John Bell & William Bell John Bell: b. ca. 1845, Texas, Black · William Bell: b. ca. 1863, Texas, Mulatto, son of John Bell

1870 U.S. Federal Census, Beat 1, Bellville, Dwelling 592: a 25-year-old Black man named John Bell, living in the household of Moses and Ellen Miller, with his own 7-year-old son beside him — William Bell, recorded as Mulatto.

Run the year back. William was born around 1863. Slavery in Texas didn't end until June 19, 1865. That means William was conceived and born enslaved, two years before freedom, and recorded as Mulatto — meaning one of his parents was white. His father carries the Bell surname. So did the family that founded this town, named it, and held Easter Bell in the same years, a few miles from wherever John and William actually lived.

No will or deed says which white Bell held John. It could be Thomas and Abigail's household. It could be A.J. Bell's, the sheriff's, whose own son was nearly the same age as John. It could be Robert E. Bell's, out at Pine Grove. Nothing found yet closes that gap. But a mixed-race child, born enslaved, carrying the Bell name, in the same small town as every other Bell already on this page — that is not a coincidence this research is willing to file away. It's the reason the search widened to every Bell household in the first place, and it's still open.

Checked and Ruled Out Lottie B. (Garnett) Bell/Beal Fletcher b. April 25, 1911 · d. February 19, 1971, Bellville

Appeared in 1940 census indexing as "Fletcher Lottie Bell." Her own 1971 death certificate names her parents as Robert Garnett and Eliza Boldan — neither a Bell. A separate 1920 census record shows a "Lottie B. Beal" in a large Beal family household in Justice Precinct 8 — "Beal" and "Bell" are easily confused in transcription and indexing. Recorded here for completeness; not currently supported as a Bell descendant by blood or marriage.

Also checked, not part of this family's confirmed line: the white John P. Bell household (1850, 1900) — son of A.J. Bell, unconnected by any document to the Black John Bell above beyond shared name and geography. Robert E. Bell's wife Elizabeth Winters and her own Winters family — Virginia/Tennessee origin, unconnected to the Bell bloodline itself.
