Research Methodology

How this research was conducted, what standards it follows, and why those standards matter for African American genealogy in particular.

For Family Members · Plain Language

This page explains how the research was done — what counts as evidence and what does not. If you are a family member just reading the family story, you do not need to read this page. If you are a researcher who wants to verify or build on this work, this page tells you exactly how every conclusion was reached and why some things are labeled confirmed while others are labeled hypothesis.

The Standard

This research follows the Reasonably Exhaustive Search standard — the professional benchmark established by the Board for Certification of Genealogists and the standard required for publication in peer-reviewed genealogical journals. It means searching every record type that could reasonably be expected to contain relevant information, not stopping at the first record that seems to answer a question, and continuing to search even when early results are negative.

For African American genealogy — particularly for families whose ancestors were enslaved — this standard is not optional. It is the minimum required to do this work honestly. The records that document enslaved ancestors are scattered, inconsistently preserved, filed under enslavers' names, and often indexed under misspellings. A surface-level search almost always fails. Every confirmed ancestor in this archive was found only because the search went deeper than the obvious sources.

Newton Ballard was found in the Texas GLO records — not because his name appeared in a standard census search, but because a researcher recognized that the misspelled name "Newton Bullard" in an old land file jacket was the same person as the unnamed 20-year-old male on a Smith County slave schedule. That connection required cross-referencing five independent record types. That is what a reasonably exhaustive search looks like in practice.

Core Principles
Primary Sources Only

Every fact stated as confirmed in this archive is supported by at least one primary source — a document created at or near the time of the event by someone with direct knowledge of it. Death certificates, deeds, census records, court orders, church records, probate inventories, slave schedules, land patents, voter registration rolls, military records, and newspaper accounts are all primary sources used in this research. Information copied from other family trees, unverified transcriptions, and secondhand accounts are never stated as fact. When a claim is based on inference or pattern-matching rather than a direct document, it is clearly labeled as a hypothesis or a working conclusion.

Hypothesis Discipline

Unconfirmed connections are always labeled as hypotheses — never stated as facts, never silently incorporated into the confirmed line. The Louisa Hypothesis, which proposes a community connection between Newton Ballard and the John Bullard estate enslaved people in Louisiana, is supported by three primary source documents and significant geographic evidence. It is still labeled a hypothesis because no document directly names Newton in relation to that community. This discipline protects the integrity of the confirmed line and ensures that future researchers can clearly see what is proven and what is still being investigated.

Conflation Risk Awareness

Multiple white Bullard and Ballard families with overlapping geographies and similar names appear throughout this research. Early in the project, a land claim belonging to a white Newton Bullard — son of John William Bullard and Rebecca Fortenberry Bumgarner, born 1832 Tennessee — was incorrectly associated with our Newton Ballard. That error was caught and corrected when five independent record types failed to support the connection. Every new record is verified against the established generational timeline before being incorporated into the archive.

Census Birthplace Data Caution

For formerly enslaved individuals, census birthplace data is treated with particular caution. Rev. George W. Ballard's parental birthplace data varied across four different census years — not because the data was wrong, but because he did not know his parents' exact origins. This is expected and common for people born into enslavement. When census data conflicts with primary source documents — as Easter Bell Nichols's birth year did across multiple sources — the primary source closest to the birth event takes precedence.

Geography Filtering

Nationwide or state-level record searches without county-level filtering have repeatedly returned unusable data in this project. Every census search, deed search, and tax record search is scoped to the specific county identified by prior research before being expanded. This principle was established after early searches returned hundreds of results across multiple states, none of which connected to the confirmed family line.

Generational Math

Every new record is cross-checked against the established generational timeline before it is accepted. A record for a person with the right name in the right county is not automatically the right person — the birth year implied by the record must be consistent with what the confirmed documents already show. The 1952 Tulsa marriage record for a James Wilson Ballard was flagged as likely belonging to a different individual precisely because the birth year implied by the record created a generational impossibility.

What This Research Cannot Confirm

Open Research Questions

Newton Ballard's birth mother has not been identified by primary source. His death date and burial location have not been confirmed. The connection between the Anson County North Carolina Bullard family and the Louisiana John Bullard estate has been established but the connection to Lewis F. Ballard Sr.'s Nash County North Carolina family line has not been confirmed. Clarissa's death date and post-1839 record have not been found. Hellen Nichols Armstrong's death certificate has not been located — the TX State File No. 54237 belongs to Helen Nichols Reese, a different individual. These gaps are documented throughout the archive and do not affect the confirmed primary source chain from Molley through Jasmine M. Lee.

About the Researcher

This research was conducted by Jasmine M. Lee, founder and CEO of the Dock Ellis Foundation, and a direct descendant of every confirmed ancestor documented in this archive. The research began as a personal family history project and grew into a multi-year archive spanning primary sources across eight states and spanning 1778 to the present. Jasmine reviewed original documents directly — voter registers, deed books, tax rolls, probate records, slave schedules, land patents, and notarial records — and uploaded her findings directly into this research archive.
