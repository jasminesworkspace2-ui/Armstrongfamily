Primary Source Documents · 1793 – 1839 · Newberry County, South Carolina

The Paper Trail

We found her. Not in a family story passed down through generations. Not in a photograph. Not in a letter she wrote.

We found her in a debt record. In a merchant's ledger. In deeds signed by men who believed they owned her.

Her name was Patience. She was approximately thirty years old. And what follows is every document that ever wrote her name down.

1848 Bellville, Austin County, Texas — Easter Bell Nichols is born July 4, 1848 — in the household of John and Fanny Nichols, the same family that received Patience's children in 1827. She is Patience's great-granddaughter.
I
March 22, 1793

Deed Book B · Page 467 · Union County, South Carolina

The Mortgage

The first time Patience's name appears in any document, she is collateral.

On March 22, 1793, a planter named James Sims Sr. of Union County, South Carolina, needed money. He needed £1000 sterling — an enormous sum. To secure the debt, he pledged everything he had to Peter Braselman, a merchant in neighboring Newberry County: 850 acres of land, ten horses, furniture, hogs, tools — and thirty-six enslaved people.

Among those thirty-six people, the deed names Molley and her children: Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, Tom — and Patience.

Patience is listed as one of Molley's children. But she is not a child. She is approximately twenty-eight years old.

Primary Source · Deed Book B:467 · Newberry County SC · March 22, 1793
The deed book containing Patience's first recorded appearance · Newberry County, South Carolina
Deed Book B:467 · The Sims Mortgage · Names of Enslaved People
The 1793 mortgage deed · Molley and her children including Patience are named among 36 enslaved people pledged as collateral
Transcription · Deed Book B:467 · March 22, 1793
Mortgage. James Sims, Senr. Of Union County, State of South Carolina, planter to Peter Braselman of Newberry County, South Carolina, Merchant. In consideration of £1000 Sterling — one tract of land in Union County containing 850 acres; also 36 negroes: Jack and Doll and their children Ambrose, Paul; Isaac and Tabby and their children Jack, Emille, Fanny, Robert; Molley and her children Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, Tom and Patience, Big Lucy and Daniel, Tabby, Peter, Desly, and Major and Lucy; Jenny and her children Mary, Henry, Peter; Delsey and her children Morning and Daniel, Isaac, Lucy, Amy, Frank and Doll; also ten horses, mares and colts, furniture, hoggs, tools... Signed James Sims, Sr. Recorded 29 June 1793. Fredk. Nance, DC.

Thirty-six people. Named in a debt document as security for a loan. The deed groups them by family unit — Jack and Doll with their children, Isaac and Tabby with theirs, Molley with hers. The families knew each other. They had probably lived together for years. They were not abstractions in a ledger. They were people whose names James Sims knew well enough to write down.

The mortgage was recorded June 29, 1793 by Frederick Nance, the Clerk of Newberry County — the same man who would record Patience's transfer to Patty Waldrop two years later. Newberry County was a small world. Everyone in it knew each other. Patience did not know yet that the debt that pledged her would not be paid.

II
June 26, 1793

Deed Book B · Page 466 · Three Months Later

The Gift That Excluded Them

Three months after the mortgage, James Sims Sr. made a different transaction. On June 26, 1793, he gave nine enslaved people as a gift to his daughter — Drucilla Braselman. The same Drucilla who was married to Peter Braselman. The same Peter Braselman to whom Sims had pledged Molley and Patience as collateral.

The nine people given to Drucilla were: Phillis, Sarah, Ithea, Stephen, Benjamin, Abina, Barlet, Susahan, and Henry.

Molley was not among them. Patience was not among them.

James Sims was giving his daughter assets while his debt to her husband remained unpaid. The people he chose to keep as collateral — the people he did not give away — were Molley and her family. Whether this was because Molley's family was considered more valuable, or because Sims still intended to repay the debt and reclaim them, or for reasons the record does not say — we cannot know. What we know is that Molley and Patience stayed pledged. And Sims would not repay the debt.

The nine people given to Drucilla — Phillis appears here and again in Drucilla's 1836 will, confirming this is the same family network

Phillis, who appears in this 1793 deed, appears again in Drucilla Braselman's 1836 will — 43 years later, still with the same family. Tom, listed as Molley's child in the mortgage, also appears in Drucilla's will in 1836. These are the same people, documented across five decades. The records confirm it.

But Molley and Patience are not in the 1836 will. They left before then. They were sold.

III
January 1, 1795

Deed Book C · Page 124 · Newberry County · The Transfer

Sold in Open Market

The debt was not repaid. On January 1, 1795 — one and a half years after the mortgage came due — seven people were sold to satisfy it.

The buyers were Wadsworth and Turpin, merchants of Newberry County. The sale secured payment of £87 12 shillings 2 pence. The phrase the deed uses is precise and cold: sold in open market.

Primary Source · The Sale to Wadsworth & Turpin · Deed Book C:124 · January 1, 1795
The document that records the sale of Moll and Patience in open market · January 1, 1795 · Newberry County, South Carolina · Frederick Nance, Clerk
Transcription · Deed Book C:124 · January 1, 1795
...sold in open market to Wadsworth and Turpin, Merchants of the aforesaid County and State of South Carolina — one Negro wench named Moll about 30 years of age, Patience about 30 years old, Hannah about 12 years of age, Lin about 11 years of age, Violet 9 years of age, Esther 7 years of age and George 3 years of age — sold in open market to secure payment of £87 12 shillings 2p as by bond bearing date the 1 January 1795. Fredk. Nance, C.N.C.

Read that again: Moll about 30 years of age. Patience about 30 years old.

They are the same age. They are not mother and daughter. The 1793 mortgage grouped Patience under Molley's family unit — but Patience was not Molley's child. She was approximately Molley's contemporary. They may have been sisters. They may have been close kin. They may have been two women who had been together long enough that the deed writer listed them as family because they were family — just not by blood.

With them: Hannah (12), Lin (11), Violet (9), Esther (7), George (3). Seven people, sold together in open market on New Year's Day, 1795.

Moll about 30 years
Patience about 30 years
Hannah about 12 years
Lin about 11 years
Violet about 9 years
Esther about 7 years

Esther — seven years old in January 1795, born approximately 1788 in South Carolina. The name Easter — Esther — in this family network is confirmed as early as 1788. Easter Bell Nichols, your ancestor, was born July 4, 1848 in Bellville, Texas — sixty years later. The name traveled. It survived the Middle Passage, South Carolina, Mississippi, and arrived in Texas, where it became the name of the woman whose line runs directly to Jasmine M. Lee.

"Sold in open market." Seven words. Seven people. Moll and Patience are thirty years old. They have been alive for three decades. They have children or kin beside them. They are sold on New Year's Day to settle a debt of £87.

Wadsworth and Turpin acquired them in January. By August, Patience would be gone — transferred again, this time to a new family, leaving Moll and the children behind. We do not know what they said to each other when Patience left. The record does not preserve that.

IV
August 4, 1795

Deed Book C · Pages 367–368 · Newberry County · The Transfer That Changed Everything

One Negro Girl Named Patience

Seven months after the sale to Wadsworth and Turpin, on August 4, 1795, Francis Davenport Sr. — a planter of the District of 96 and County of Newberry, South Carolina — gave one enslaved woman to his daughter.

Not Moll. Not Hannah. Not Lin. Not Violet. Not Esther. Not George.

Patience.

The deed calls her "one Negro girl named Patience" — she was approximately thirty years old. The language of the law did not change with age. She was given to Patty Waldrop, Francis Davenport's beloved daughter, the wife of Hezekiah Waldrop.

The full deed page · Note Deed Book C:369 immediately follows — Francis Davenport gave another woman named Eliza to a second daughter Nancy Waldrop on August 1, 1795 · He was distributing enslaved people to multiple daughters at once
Transcription · Deed Book C:367-368 · August 4, 1795
Deed of Gift. Francis Davenport, Sr., of the District of 96 and County of Newberry, and State of South Carolina, planter, to Patty Waldrop, beloved daughter, of the State and County aforesaid — one Negro girl named Patience. Signed Francis Davenport. Wit. Isaac Davenport, Edward Turner, Elizabeth Towling. Proved by Isaac Davenport before Charles Griffin, J.P. August 5, 1795. Recorded August 18, 1795. Fredk. Nance, C.N.C.

Three days earlier — August 1, 1795 — Francis Davenport had given another woman, Eliza, to his daughter Nancy Waldrop. On the same day he gave Patience to Patty, a deed also records Francis selling an enslaved man named Anthony to his son Francis Jr. He was conducting multiple transactions at once, distributing his estate among his children while he was still living.

Why Patience specifically? Why her and not Moll or one of the others? The record does not say. What we know is that Patience was singled out — separated from Moll and the children — and given to a woman named Patty Waldrop, who took her west. First to Mississippi. Then her descendants made their way to Texas.

Isaac Davenport — Francis's son, born April 3, 1753 — witnessed the deed. The same Isaac Davenport who appears elsewhere in the Newberry County records buying and selling enslaved people. He signed his name on the document that transferred Patience to the Waldrop family and thereby set in motion the chain of events that would eventually produce Jasmine M. Lee.

"One Negro girl named Patience."
She was thirty years old.
She was given away like property
by a man to his daughter.
Everything that came after this — came after this.
V
1827 · Mississippi

Luke Waldrop Sr. Distribution Deed · 1827 · The Transfer That Connects South Carolina to Texas

Patience and Her Increase

Patty Waldrop — the woman Francis Davenport gave Patience to in 1795 — died sometime before 1827. After her death, her husband Luke Waldrop Sr. executed a distribution deed that divided Patty's estate among their children. In that deed, he names exactly what Patty had been given thirty-two years earlier in South Carolina.

He calls them "the said negro slave, Patience and her increase."

Transcription · Luke Waldrop Sr. Distribution Deed · 1827
...paid by Claiborne P. Waldrop, Fanny Nichols (late Fanny Waldrop), James D. Waldrop and Stephen R. Waldrop, Willey B. Waldrop, Patsy Stoddard (late Patsy Waldrop), Sarah Robertson... children born of the body of my late wife, Patsey Waldrop... I have bargained and sold, given, released and confirmed unto them... the said negro slave, Patience and her increase named Rachel, Clarissa, Tom (Rachel's son), William, Amanda, and Harriet [children of Clarissa]...

Read the names of the recipients. Fanny Nichols, late Fanny Waldrop. She is listed second. She has already married John Nichols by the time this deed is executed. The same John Nichols who would bring his family to Bellville, Austin County, Texas — where Easter Bell Nichols was born on July 4, 1848, in that exact household.

This deed is the document that connects everything. It names Patience. It names her children and grandchildren. And it names the woman who carried them to Texas.

Fanny Waldrop Nichols was born in Newberry, South Carolina — the same district where Peter and Druscila Braselman held Molley and Patience after the 1793 mortgage. She grew up knowing this family. When she married John Nichols and moved to Texas, she did not leave them behind. The 1827 deed confirms it: Patience's children and grandchildren traveled west with Fanny. And in Bellville, Austin County, Texas, in 1848, Easter Bell Nichols was born — into that exact household — carrying the name that had first appeared in Newberry County, South Carolina sixty years earlier, when a seven-year-old girl named Esther was sold alongside Patience in open market on New Year's Day 1795.

The family that traded Patience among themselves in South Carolina
moved to Mississippi together.
Then to Texas together.
And they brought her children with them.

Easter Bell Nichols was born in their house.
She was Patience's great-granddaughter.
She did not know it.
Now we do.

The Network · 1770s–1848

Geographic & Community Context · How These Families Moved Together

The Community That Carried Her

This was not a series of random transactions between strangers. The Sims, Braselman, Waldrop, and Davenport families were a community — one that had been neighbors in North Carolina before moving to South Carolina together, and that eventually migrated to Mississippi and Texas together, carrying the same enslaved families with them across a thousand miles and six decades.

The Migration Route · 1770s to 1848 · Sims · Braselman · Waldrop · Davenport · Nichols
The Sims and Waldrop families originated in Granville County, North Carolina before migrating south to Union and Newberry Counties, South Carolina in the 1770s–1780s. The Waldrops moved to Mississippi (Rankin County) bringing Patience and her family. After Patty Waldrop's death in the 1820s, Fanny Waldrop Nichols carried Patience's children to Bellville, Austin County, Texas — where Easter Bell Nichols was born in 1848.

The Documented Connections Between These Families

NORTH CAROLINA ORIGIN

Luke Waldrop Sr. and his sons sold parcels of land directly to William Sims (Drusilla's relative) in Granville County, North Carolina before either family migrated south. They were neighbors before South Carolina. They were neighbors after.

THE ENOREE RIVER COMMUNITY

Around 1772, the Waldrops settled on the Enoree River and Duncan's Creek in Laurens and Newberry counties — the same rivers where Peter and Druscila Braselman operated. Land grants show John Waldrop owning land directly adjacent to the Braselman community networks. These families were literal neighbors on the same waterway.

NEWBERRY COUNTY COURTS

Both the Braselmans and the Waldrops were active in the Newberry District court system simultaneously. Guardianship records place the Braselman estate business — including minor children Nathan Sims Braselman and Sarah S. Braselman — right alongside Artemus Waldrop and Samuel Waldrop, handling estate filings in the same local district courts.

INTERMARRIAGE

The Waldrop and Sims families eventually intermarried. Nancy Waldrop (born ca. 1799, South Carolina) married into the Sims family line — legally cementing the financial and familial ties between the two clans that had already been transacting with each other for decades.

THE MIGRATION TO TEXAS

Fanny Waldrop — born in Newberry, South Carolina — married John Nichols and moved to Bellville, Austin County, Texas. The Sims family also migrated to Austin County. Pioneer surveyor Bartlett Samuel Sims arrived in Stephen F. Austin's colony early. When these families arrived in Austin County, they continued to do business near one another — mirroring the community structure they had maintained in South Carolina for three generations. Easter Bell Nichols was born into this same network in 1848. She was not born into a stranger's household. She was born into the household of the family that had held her great-grandmother Patience since 1795.

Frederick Nance — the Newberry County Clerk who recorded the Sims mortgage in 1793, the Wadsworth and Turpin sale in 1795, and the Davenport deed that gave Patience to Patty Waldrop in 1795 — signed his name on all three documents. One man. Three transactions. One community. The same handwriting appears on the first record of Patience's name and the last record of her in South Carolina.

VI
August 1, 1795

Deed Book C · Page 369 · Three Days Before

The Other Daughter

Three days before giving Patience to Patty Waldrop, Francis Davenport gave another enslaved woman to another daughter. On August 1, 1795, he gave one negro girl named Eliza to Nancy Waldrop — "in consideration of the Natural Love and Affection."

Deed Book C:369 · August 1, 1795 · Francis Davenport gives Eliza to Nancy Waldrop
Deed Book C:369 · August 1, 1795 · The deed immediately preceding the Patience transfer · Francis Davenport was distributing people to multiple Waldrop daughters in the same week

In one week in August 1795, Francis Davenport Sr. transferred at least two enslaved women — Patience and Eliza — to two of his daughters, both married into the Waldrop family. He was an old man settling his affairs. The phrase "Natural Love and Affection" appears in the deed for Eliza. It does not appear in Patience's deed. Patience's deed simply names her and records the transfer.

Patience did not leave South Carolina voluntarily. She did not choose Patty Waldrop. She did not choose Mississippi or the life that waited. She was transferred the way land is transferred — in a document, by a signature, witnessed by men who went home afterward to their families.

What she carried with her out of South Carolina — what she held inside her that no deed could transfer or take — is what this archive exists to honor.

VII
1839 · Mississippi

Rankin County, Mississippi · Deed Book 4 · Page 435 · The Last Record

The Last Time Her Name Appears

Forty-four years pass between the deed that gave Patience to Patty Waldrop and the next document that names her. In that time, she traveled from South Carolina to Mississippi with the Waldrop family. She had children. One of those children was Clarissa — born approximately 1807.

Patience is Jasmine's 5th great-grandmother. Easter Bell Nichols is Jasmine's great-great-grandmother. Every person reading this page who is a descendant of Margie Nell Ballard Corbin descends directly from Patience.

In 1839, in Rankin County, Mississippi, an estate transfer records Patience for the last time. E.P. Waldrop transfers Patience, Rachel, Clarissa, Tom, and Clarissa's children — William, Amanda, Harriet — to Stephen R. Waldrop.

Patience is approximately 74 years old in 1839. She has survived everything. The mortgage. The debt sale. The transfer to the Waldrops. The journey to Mississippi. She is still alive. She is still named.

After 1839, she does not appear in any record we have found. She likely died before 1860. Her burial place is unknown. The name she was called before she was called Patience — whatever name her people gave her in whatever language they spoke before all of this — is also unknown.

Four documents. Forty-six years. South Carolina to Mississippi.

She was property in every one of them.
She was a person through all of them.
Those two things existed simultaneously
and the second one outlasted the first
by generations.
VIII
The Index · 1785–1827

Blacks Found in the Deeds of Laurens & Newberry Counties SC · Name Index

Found in the Index

The collection "Blacks Found in the Deeds of Laurens & Newberry Counties, SC: 1785–1827" is a scholarly index of every enslaved person named in the deed books of these two South Carolina counties. Someone spent years going through those old deed books, page by page, and writing down every Black name they found.

Page 201 of the name index · Patience appears twice — once as "child of Molley" at page 118 (the Sims mortgage) and once independently at page 124 (the Wadsworth and Turpin sale) · This is how the research confirmed the chain of custody

Patience appears twice in this index. Once as "child of Molley, 118" — the 1793 Sims mortgage. Once as "Patience, 124" — the 1795 Wadsworth and Turpin sale. Two separate deed entries. Two separate moments in her life when someone thought to write her name down.

That index — compiled by a researcher who never knew this family existed — is what made it possible to find page 124. Which led to the discovery that Patience was approximately thirty years old in 1795. Which rewrote the entire timeline. Which changed what we know about who she was.

Research builds on research. Records survive because someone preserved them. Names survive because someone wrote them down. Patience survived because she was real, and because she was strong enough to build a line of descendants that ended up searching for her 230 years later.

We Are the Fifth Time Her Name Is Written Down

She appears in exactly four documents across forty-six years. A mortgage. A debt sale. A deed of gift. An estate transfer. Four times her name was written down by men who considered her property. Four times she survived it.

They did not know her name would survive them. They did not know that the line they were moving across South Carolina and Mississippi would eventually produce a woman named Jasmine M. Lee who would spend seven years finding every document they ever signed, every deed they ever recorded, every name they wrote down in ink — and reading it back differently.

Not as property. As a person. As the beginning. As proof that we did not come from nothing. We came from her.

Patience.
South Carolina · ca. 1765 · Mississippi · ca. 1839
