Reference · Plain Language

Glossary

Every term used in this research, defined in plain language for family members who are encountering genealogy for the first time. No background required.

Census Records
Slave Schedule

A separate section of the U.S. Census taken in 1850 and 1860 that listed enslaved people — but not by name. They appear only as age, sex, and sometimes a physical description, listed under their enslaver's name. This is why Newton Ballard appears in the 1850 Smith County Texas census only as "unnamed male, age 20" — the law did not require their names to be recorded. Finding an ancestor in a slave schedule requires knowing who enslaved them.

In this research: Newton Ballard · 1850 U.S. Federal Slave Schedule · Smith County Texas · Page 459 · unnamed male age 20 under Lewis F. Ballard
The 1870 Wall

A term genealogists use for the research barrier that exists before the 1870 census — the first census after emancipation in which formerly enslaved people were listed by name. Before 1870, African American ancestors appear only in slave schedules (unnamed), plantation records, deeds, estate inventories, and church records. Breaking through the 1870 Wall requires identifying who enslaved your ancestor and searching that person's records.

In this research: Every ancestor above Easter Bell Nichols required breaking through this wall. Newton Ballard was found because his enslaver Lewis F. Ballard Sr. was identified in the 1850 slave schedule.
Free Inhabitants Schedule

The main population census — the one that lists everyone by name. Before 1870 this only included free people. After 1870 it included all residents. When genealogists say they found someone "in the census," this is usually what they mean.

Land Records
GLO · General Land Office

The Texas General Land Office — the state agency that managed public land grants and homestead claims. When Newton Ballard filed his 160-acre preemption claim in 1856, the paperwork went into a GLO file jacket. That file — GLO File No. 3378 — is how researchers confirmed he claimed that land before the Civil War. The GLO archive is searchable online and is one of the most valuable tools for Texas genealogy.

In this research: Texas GLO Preemption File · File No. 3378 · Survey No. 124 · Abstract 131 · Certificate No. 131262 · Newton Ballard · Upshur County Texas · August 26 1856
Abstract Number

A unique number assigned to a specific tract of Texas land — it stays with that piece of land forever regardless of who owns it. In Upshur County Texas, Newton Ballard's 160-acre preemption claim was assigned Abstract 131. Even after he sold the land in 1873 and it was absorbed by tax sale in the 1890s, the land records continued to reference it as Abstract 131. This number allowed researchers to track the land across multiple owners and multiple decades.

In this research: Abstract 131 · Upshur County Texas · Newton Ballard's preemption claim 1856–1894
Preemption Claim

A legal process that allowed settlers to claim unoccupied public land by living on it and improving it — clearing timber, building a structure, farming it. The claimant filed a sworn affidavit stating they were the bona fide occupant. Newton Ballard used this process in 1856 — signing his own sworn statement that he was living on and working the land. This makes his claim one of the earliest documented examples of a Black man asserting independent land ownership in that part of Texas, years before emancipation.

Deed of Trust

A legal document where a borrower pledges property — including, in the antebellum South, enslaved people — as collateral for a loan. If the borrower defaults, the trustee named in the document can sell the pledged property to satisfy the debt. The 1840 Grand Gulf Bank Deed of Trust is how five of the eight enslaved people in Newton Ballard's community came to be mortgaged — and why two members of that community were likely lost when Lewis F. Ballard Sr. defaulted.

Government Records
Freedmen's Bureau

The Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands — a federal agency established in 1865 to help formerly enslaved people transition to freedom. It provided food, medical care, education, and legal assistance, and created labor contracts between formerly enslaved people and employers. Freedmen's Bureau records — which include labor contracts, ration records, marriage registers, and complaint records — are one of the most important sources for tracing African American ancestors in the years immediately after emancipation. Records are available on FamilySearch and Ancestry organized by state and county.

Probate Record · Succession Record

The legal process of settling a deceased person's estate — distributing their property to heirs and paying their debts. Probate records often name enslaved people individually, list their ages, and show who inherited them. This makes them one of the most valuable sources for pre-emancipation African American research. In Louisiana, the same process is called a succession record. The Ouachita Parish succession record for John Bullard — which this research has not yet found — is one of the highest-priority open targets for the Louisa Hypothesis.

Voter Registration Roll

During Reconstruction (1865–1877), federal officials registered Black men to vote for the first time. These rolls — organized by county and state — are some of the earliest records listing formerly enslaved Black men by name after emancipation. In this research, the 1867 Texas Voter Registration Rolls confirmed Adam Ballard and C.H. Ballard registering in Upshur County — almost certainly Newton's brothers or close relatives.

DNA Terms
cM · Centimorgans

The unit used to measure how much DNA two people share. The more centimorgans two people share, the more closely related they are. A parent and child share about 3,400–3,800 cM. First cousins share about 1,100–2,000 cM. Third cousins share about 90–200 cM. DNA matches of 20–50 cM are typically third to fifth cousins — useful for confirming family lines but requiring additional documentary research to identify exactly how the relationship connects.

In this research: Gwen Reese — 53.5 cM match to kit SQ8499767 — confirmed segments on chromosomes 8, 11, and 17 — Nichols-Waldrop family line
ThruLines

An AncestryDNA feature that identifies which of your DNA matches share a documented common ancestor with you through their family trees. When ThruLines shows 46 matches connecting through Clarissa as the common ancestor, it means 46 living people's DNA and family trees all point to Clarissa as a shared great-great-grandparent or similar. ThruLines is a powerful confirmation tool — but it depends on the accuracy of the family trees involved, so it should be confirmed by documentary research.

In this research: 46 ThruLines matches through Clarissa · confirmed via AncestryDNA · kit SQ8499767 · Joseph Nichols as the documented pathway
Triangulation

A DNA analysis technique where you confirm that three or more people share the same segment of DNA on the same chromosome — proving the DNA they share came from a common ancestor rather than by chance. When Gwen Reese's match was confirmed on specific chromosomes 8, 11, and 17, that is the beginning of triangulation — showing the shared DNA is real and traceable, not statistical noise.

GEDmatch

A free third-party website where people who have tested at different DNA companies (AncestryDNA, 23andMe, MyHeritage) can upload their raw DNA data and compare results with each other. Because it accepts data from any company, it dramatically expands the pool of potential matches. Kit QW8418781 is Jasmine M. Lee's GEDmatch kit. Kit SQ8499767 is Hjordis L. Corbin's AncestryDNA kit.

Research Terms
Primary Source

A document created at or near the time of the event it records, by someone with direct knowledge of that event. A death certificate, a deed, a census record, a church register, a court order — these are primary sources. Family stories, compiled genealogies, and information copied from other trees are not primary sources. This research relies exclusively on primary sources — every fact in this archive can be traced to a specific document with a specific citation.

Reasonably Exhaustive Search

The standard used by professional genealogists and recognized by the Board for Certification of Genealogists — it means searching all records that could reasonably be expected to contain relevant information, not just the first record that seems to answer the question. For African American genealogy, this means searching not just census records but slave schedules, Freedmen's Bureau records, deed books, probate records, church histories, newspaper archives, voter registration rolls, tax records, and DNA evidence. This research follows that standard throughout.

Hypothesis · Working Hypothesis

A proposed connection that is supported by evidence but has not yet been confirmed by a primary source that states it directly. In this research, the Louisa Hypothesis proposes a connection between Newton Ballard and the John Bullard estate community in Louisiana — supported by geographic convergence, naming patterns, and three primary source documents, but not yet confirmed by a document that directly names Newton in relation to that community. Hypotheses in this archive are always clearly labeled as such and never stated as facts.

Dower Release

A legal document in which a married woman formally gives up her claim to property her husband is selling or mortgaging. In the 19th century, a wife had legal rights to a portion of her husband's estate — her dower interest — and her consent was required before property could be transferred. Dower releases appear frequently in deed books and are useful for confirming marriages and placing families in a specific county at a specific time.

Increase

A legal term used in antebellum deeds and wills to mean the children born to an enslaved woman after she was given, sold, or bequeathed. When the 1795 South Carolina deed gave Patience "and her increase" to Patty Waldrop, it meant that any children Patience bore — including Clarissa — automatically became the property of the Waldrop family under the terms of that document. Clarissa was Patience's increase — born into bondage without any separate transaction.

In this research: "Patience, a negro woman, and her increase" · Francis Davenport Sr. deed · Newberry County South Carolina · August 4 1795
