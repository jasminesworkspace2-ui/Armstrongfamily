Where We Come From

DNA is another way into the archive. The paper trail identifies people, places, estates and transactions; genetic evidence can reveal relationships and communities that surviving records do not always name.

The rule of this page: DNA evidence is preserved at the strength it actually supports. A genetic signal can open a door without pretending to be the document that is still missing.

01 · Read this first

What the DNA page is for

Family + researcher edition

For our family

This page records what the DNA can tell us about biological connections, ancestral populations, and communities connected to the family story. It also shows where the paper trail still has work to do.

For other researchers

The goal is reproducibility. Kit identifiers are masked where appropriate; methods, match sizes, segment information, calculator settings, and research status are shown where available.

DNA does not replace the paper trail. It can point toward a family, place, community, or biological relationship that can then be tested against deeds, probate, census records, wills, land records, death certificates, and other primary sources.
02 · Evidence framework

How genetic evidence is labeled

DocumentedPrimary record or original image directly establishes the historical fact.
DNA-supportedGenetic evidence and documentary evidence point in the same direction.
Genetic network signalClusters, shared matches, geography, surnames, or segments create a research direction.
HypothesisA plausible ancestral or historical explanation still requiring a documentary bridge.
UnresolvedThe genetic relationship may be real while the exact historical ancestor remains unknown.

Who are the Fulani?

One people, many communities

The Fulani, also called Fula or Fulbe, are a large and geographically dispersed people whose communities extend across a broad belt of West and Central Africa. Fulfulde and related varieties are spoken across this region, with major concentrations from Senegal and Guinea through Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger and northern Nigeria, and farther east into Cameroon and Chad. Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History

Fulani communities have never been a single uniform population. Many have historically practiced pastoralism and seasonal livestock movement, while others have lived as farmers, combined agriculture with livestock keeping, or settled in towns and cities. Their histories also differ by region, language variety, lineage, religion, political history and migration. National Geographic

Why this matters here: the EthioHelix/Oracle results can point toward reference populations associated with broad African regions. A Fulani model result is therefore a population-level research signal. It does not by itself establish that a specific historical ancestor was Fulani, nor does it identify where in the Fulani world that ancestry entered the family.
Historical research questionIf the Fulani signal persists across independent genetic analyses, the next step is to investigate the documented routes, communities and Atlantic-world populations through which West African ancestry could have entered the family—not to assign the result to a named ancestor without a bridge.

10 · African population results

What the current models can—and cannot—say

Can say

Population similarity

The tested DNA resembles the calculator's reference populations in the proportions and combinations reported.

Can suggest

Research directions

Broad West African, Sahelian, Central African, East African, or other population signals can help determine which historical communities deserve deeper investigation.

Cannot establish alone

A named ancestor

An admixture component cannot, by itself, identify Patience, Easter, Rendy, or another specific ancestor—or prove a specific ethnic identity.

Why the EthioHelix chart remains important: it preserves the actual computational result instead of hiding it behind a narrative. Future tools, better reference panels, or new DNA matches can be compared against this recorded baseline.
11 · Research corrections

The original record outranks the database

Campbell indexing correction

The 1870 Austin County Campbell household was misindexed as White in major genealogy databases. The archive records the original-sheet examination showing M/B designations.

The research page also preserves the Hannah Campbell inferred-mother problem and the 1880 birthplace discrepancy rather than silently reconciling them.

Why this matters to DNA work

Bad indexing can erase a family from search results. A DNA match may point toward a community that the indexed record makes difficult to find. Original images are therefore part of the genetic-genealogy workflow, not a separate activity.
