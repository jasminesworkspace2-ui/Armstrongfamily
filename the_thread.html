The Full Thesis · 1793–1865 · South Carolina to Texas

The Thread.

Molley to Patience to Clarissa. Rendy Blayn to Easter Bell. One story, laid out start to finish.

This page exists to do one thing: show, in order, with sources, how every piece of this research fits together — what is confirmed by a document that names the person directly, what is reasoned from household structure, age, and location because no enslaved person's own words or name were ever recorded stating it, and what remains genuinely open. Nothing here is invented. Where the paper trail stops, that stop is marked plainly, and the reasoning that fills the gap is shown as reasoning — not dressed up as proof it isn't.

01
The Beginning
Molley b. ca. 1750s–1770s (estimated) · Newberry/Union County, South Carolina Earliest documented, name only — not independently verified

Molley is the earliest name in this family's documented history. She appears in a March 22, 1793 Union County, South Carolina mortgage — James Sims Sr. to Peter Braselman — naming "Molley and her children Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, Tom and Patience." This is the only document found naming a Molley with a daughter named Patience.

Tested and Held Open

"Molley and her children Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, Tom and Patience"

Deed Book B: 467 · March 22, 1793 · James Sims Sr., Union County, SC, to Peter Braselman

This is marked open, not confirmed, for a precise reason. A separate document — the January 1, 1795 Wadsworth & Turpin debt sale, naming "Moll about 30 years of age, Patience about 30 years old" as two separate adult women, not mother and child — conflicts directly with the Union County record. The Patience who enters this family's confirmed line seven months later, in the August 1795 Davenport-to-Waldrop deed, is best explained by the January 1795 sale, not the 1793 Union County mortgage. Molley may be a real ancestor whose line simply predates what any surviving document can verify — or the "Patience" in the Union County mortgage may be a different girl who shared the name, the same way "Easter," "Clary," and "Eliza" all recur independently throughout this research. Molley's place in this family is honored here as likely, but not proven.

02
Newberry County, South Carolina
Patience b. ca. 1780s–1790s (estimated) · d. Sabine Parish, Louisiana, likely between 1850–1860 Confirmed by two independent, dated primary sources

Patience is the true root of this family's documented line — the earliest ancestor named across multiple independent documents spanning 65 years and three states.

January 1, 1795 — Debt Sale

"...one Negro wench named Moll about 30 years of age, Patience about 30 years old, Hannah about 12 years of age, Lin about 11 years of age, Violet 9 years of age, Esther 7 years of age and George 3 years of age, sold in open market to secure payment of £87 12 shillings 2p... to Wadsworth and Turpin, Merchants."

Deed Book C, page 124 · Newberry County, South Carolina

August 4, 1795 — Deed of Gift

"Francis Davenport, Senr... to Patty Waldrop, beloved daughter... one negro girl named Patience."

Deed Book C: 367–368 · Witnessed by Isaac Davenport, Edward Turner, Elizabeth Towling · Proved before Charles Griffin, J.P. · Recorded August 18, 1795

Seven months separate these two documents — Patience sold to the merchant firm of Wadsworth & Turpin in January, then given by Francis Davenport to his daughter Patty in August, both in Newberry County. The most economical reading is that Wadsworth & Turpin transferred or sold Patience to Francis Davenport sometime in that window, and Davenport then gave her to his daughter — a plausible, if not fully documented, bridge between two real transactions in the same small county the same year.

03
The Foundational Discovery
Patty Waldrop Is Martha "Patsy" Davenport Confirmed — closes the 44-year chain

For years, "Patty Waldrop" was simply a name on the 1795 deed. Today's research confirmed she was Martha "Patsy" Davenport — Francis Davenport's own daughter, who married Hezekiah Parrish Waldrop around 1796 in Newberry County. Patience was not given to a stranger. She was given by a father to his own daughter, as the daughter began her married life.

1820 Federal Census — Lawrence County, Mississippi

Hezekiah Waldrop's household: 1 enslaved female age 45+, 2 enslaved females age 14–25, 1 enslaved male age 14–25.

1820 U.S. Federal Census, Lawrence County, Mississippi

Read as a family, not four isolated numbers: the 45+ woman is Patience herself (a real, close age match if she was born in the 1770s–1780s). The two females aged 14–25 are her daughters — Rachel and Clarissa — already born, already growing up in this household by 1820. This is reasoned from age and household structure, not a document naming them individually — but it is the single most internally consistent reading of the numbers.

Patsy Davenport Waldrop died in 1829, in Hinds County, Mississippi. For the next ten years, Hezekiah held Patience and her family in trust for his late wife's children — legally his to manage, not his to own outright.

04
Brandon, Rankin County, Mississippi
March 25, 1839 — The Distribution The single most important document in this entire archive

Ten years after Patsy's death, Hezekiah formally transferred title to Patience and her descendants to his eight surviving children.

The Deed, In Full

"...I have worked said negro slave and her increase in trust for the use of the children of my said wife without claiming title thereto... I have bargained and sold, given, released & confirmed unto them, the said children of my said deceased wife, the said negro slave Patience and her increase named Rachel, Clarissa, Tom — Rachel's son, William and Amanda and Harriet, children of Clarissa..."

Rankin County, Mississippi · Deed Book 4, p. 435 · Signed E.P. (Hezekiah) Waldrop · Acknowledged before Clerk John Tate, Brandon, Mississippi

The eight children named as grantees: Claiborne P. Waldrop, Fanny Nichols (late Fanny Waldrop), James D. Waldrop, Stephen R. Waldrop, Willey B. Waldrop, Patsy Stoddard, Sarah Robertson, and Selina Robertson.

The Family Splits Here

One of the eight grantees, Fanny Nichols, late Fanny Waldrop, was already married to John Nichols Jr. of Austin County, Texas — two years before this deed was even signed. She is the thread connecting this Mississippi family to the Texas story. But Fanny received no direct share of Patience's own children in this deed. What happened to Patience, Rachel, and Clarissa's family from this point split into two separate, traceable paths.

05
Holmes County, Mississippi → Louisiana
Rachel's Branch — Sold Within Five Months Confirmed by an actual bill of sale

Rachel and her son went to Stephen R. Waldrop. He did not keep them.

August 19, 1839 (signed July 5, 1839)

"I Stephen R. Waldrop... do sell alien and convey unto Howell Prestridge... One Negro woman named Rachel for Eight hundred and fifty dollars, one boy named Briant for two hundred dollars..."

Holmes County, Mississippi · Witness Wm. Prestridge · Acknowledged before John S. Ellis, J.P.

Just five months after receiving her, Stephen sold Rachel and her son to Howell Prestridge — his own father-in-law. Stephen had married Prestridge's daughter Matilda around 1833; their son Howell Waldrop (b. ~1836) was named for this same grandfather. The sale kept Rachel inside the extended family network, even as it separated her permanently from her mother and sister.

Prestridge's own household moved from Attala County, Mississippi (1840) to Jackson Parish, Louisiana (by 1850), where he died in January 1860. Rachel's own trail does not track forward cleanly by age into either his 1840 or 1850 household — the closest candidate, a 70-year-old woman in his 1850 slave schedule, fits Patience's likely age range far better than Rachel's. Rachel's specific fate after 1839 is not confirmed by any document found in this research.

06
Sabine Parish, Louisiana
Clarissa's Branch — Held Together for at Least 20 Years Reasoned from two matching census-year household structures

Claiborne Parrish Waldrop — Hezekiah's eldest son, who had lived alongside Patience's family in his own father's household as a young man in 1820 — is the strongest candidate for who kept custody of Patience and Clarissa going forward. He relocated his own family from Mississippi to Sabine Parish, Louisiana sometime between 1838 and 1841.

Read his 1850 household as one family, not seven isolated ages:

Working conclusion: Patience most likely died in her seventies sometime between 1850 and 1860, still with this family. Clarissa lived on, and the family she and Patience founded appears to have remained intact, together, growing across at least three generations under Claiborne Waldrop's household in Sabine Parish, Louisiana — a genuinely different outcome from Rachel's line, which was broken apart within months of the 1839 deed.

She received no direct share of Patience's descendants in that deed. Nothing found in this research shows Patience, Clarissa, Rachel, or any of Clarissa's children ever physically reaching Texas.

08
Bellville, Austin County, Texas
Rendy Blayn & Joseph Nichols Reasoned from one household, read as one family

This is where the documented Mississippi/Louisiana line and the Texas line become two separate stories. No document found in this research connects Patience's descendants to Rendy Blayn. Rendy's own family must be read from the Texas records alone.

The Only Document Naming Rendy Directly

Rendy Blayn, listed as mother, on Easter Bell Nichols's own death certificate, December 17, 1935 — informant Katie Thorton.

Texas, U.S., Death Certificates, 1903–1982 · Birth year given as 1817

That single line is the entire documentary record of Rendy's existence. She never appears, by name, in any slave schedule, deed, or Freedmen's Bureau record found in this research. But the 1860 slave schedule for John Nichols — the same man Fanny Waldrop married — contains a household whose ages align with her almost exactly.

Read as a household, not nine isolated numbers: this is a plausible, coherent, three-generation family, with Rendy at its head, Joseph growing up inside it as a young man, and a wider circle of siblings, nieces, and nephews around him. No document names any of these nine people. This reading is built from age, family structure, and common sense about how enslaved households were actually organized — not from a record that states the relationships directly.

The John Nichols estate partition following his 1864 death names five people by name: Fanny (55), Jack (53), Rhoda (33), Mingo (27), Amanda (21), and Warren. None of their ages cleanly match the 43-year-old woman from 1860 — she does not clearly survive into this later, named document, meaning she most likely died, was sold, or was transferred elsewhere in the intervening four years.

Fanny's own trail continues forward with real strength. A "Fannie George" appears in this exact precinct in 1880, age 70 (b. ~1810) and again in 1900, age 90 — matching the partition's Fanny (b. ~1809) almost exactly, in the same Bellville community, having taken the surname George sometime after emancipation, the same way Easter took Bell and Joseph took Nichols. Her own father is recorded as born in Africa. This is treated here as a strong, reasoned identification — the tightest age match of any of the five 1864 partition names against a later, named record.

09
Bellville, Austin County, Texas
Easter Bell b. July 4, 1838 · d. December 17, 1935 One girl, aging consistently across three independent sources

Easter was enslaved separately from Joseph — under the extended Bell family (Thomas Bell, his wife Abigail Grimes Bell, and their adopted son John G. Bell), the same family whose land purchases from John Nichols created the town of Bellville itself.

These are not three different candidates. They are the same girl, tracked at three points in her own life. The hotel record and the 1850 slave schedule both place a 12-year-old Easter in the Bell family's world at essentially the same moment — not two separate Easters, but the same child, confirmed twice, independently, the same year. The 1860 figure of 19 sits four years off her exact expected age of 22, well inside the ordinary drift seen throughout every record in this era for people whose birth was never formally documented. Taken together, this is a single, continuous, internally consistent trail from age 12 to age 19 (actually 22), across three separate sources, naming one household the entire time.

Thomas Bell's Will, Signed January 1858

"...It is my wish and desire that Henry shall have one day in every two weeks and that when old to be well taken care of and kindly treated and that if Henry or Mat are not satisfied to dispose of them allowing them to choose their masters..."

Austin County Probate Records · Probated May 31, 1858 · Contested on appeal by Abigail Bell

This will names Henry and Mat directly — two enslaved men held by this same family — and shows the household was, at least once, willing to record an unusually direct concern for two individuals by name. Easter is not named in it. She remains a woman whose presence in this family's story is built from where she stood, and from watching her age forward consistently across records that were never designed to track her as an individual at all.

10
Juneteenth Forward
1865 — Freedom, and the Names That Followed Confirmed by the Austin County Freedmen's Marriage Register

Six months after Juneteenth, in the same week, two couples formalized their marriages before the Austin County clerk — the first legal recognition either had ever been permitted.

Entry No. 8 — December 18, 1865

"Joseph Nichols freeman and Easter Bell freedwoman... joined in Marriage as husband and wife..."

Austin County, Texas Freedmen's Marriage Register · Officiant Benj. L. Cheek, J.P. · Clerk John R. Campbell

The entry immediately before theirs, Entry No. 7, records Edward Bell and Eliza Nichols marrying the same week — a Bell man marrying a Nichols woman, in the same register, in the same room, as a Nichols man married a Bell woman. This is the moment the two separate enslaved family threads — the Nichols household where Joseph and Rendy's family lived, and the Bell household where Easter lived — visibly cross, in the historical record, for the first time.

Joseph took the surname Nichols, from the family he'd been enslaved by. Easter took the surname Bell, from hers. Their children — Allen, Josephine, Oscar, Hellen, Clarar, Willie — carried the Nichols name forward. It is Hellen's line, through Eva Armstrong Ballard and Margie Nell Ballard Corbin, that reaches this archive's own compiler today.

11
South Carolina to Texas · Two Generations, One Family Practice
The Names That Repeat: Eliza, and the Pattern Itself Two women, fifty years apart, sharing one name on purpose

Two women named Eliza appear in this research, separated by roughly fifty years and two states. They are not the same person — the age gap makes that plain. But read together, they show something real about how this family held itself together across the very separations meant to break it apart.

Eliza — Newberry County, South Carolina, August 1, 1795

"Francis Davenport, Senr... to Nancy Waldrop... one negro girl named Eliza."

Deed Book C: 369 · Three days before Patience was given to Nancy's sister Patty · Likely a sibling or cousin of Patience, distributed to a different Davenport daughter the very same week

Eliza Nichols, later Eliza Bell — Austin County, Texas, December 11, 1865

Married Edward Bell, one entry before Joseph Nichols and Easter Bell's own marriage in the same Freedmen's Register. By 1870: age 22, with husband Edward (24) and son Charles (5).

Austin County Freedmen's Marriage Register, Entry No. 7 · 1870 U.S. Federal Census, Beat 1, Bellville

Her name before marriage was Nichols — meaning she was very likely enslaved inside the same John Nichols household as Joseph, quite possibly a sister or cousin to him, the same way the 26-year-old woman in the 1860 Nichols schedule is read as Rendy's daughter. Read the two Elizas side by side, not as one trail but as one practice: a name carried forward, generation after generation, through a family that was sold, gifted, and scattered across South Carolina, Georgia, Tennessee, Mississippi, Louisiana, and Texas — surviving every separation even when the people carrying it never met each other, never knew of each other, and left no document connecting them at all. The name is the thread. It is the one thing slavery's paperwork couldn't fully erase.

Held Honestly Open

This research does not connect everything, and it should not pretend to. Stated plainly, what remains unresolved:

The Missing Bridge

No document found connects Patience, Rachel, Clarissa, William, Amanda, or Harriet — the well-documented Mississippi/Louisiana branch — to Rendy Blayn or anyone in the Texas Bellville household. These may be the same extended family, scattered by sale and distribution until the paper trail simply ran out. They may also be two entirely separate enslaved families who happened to pass through the same Waldrop-Nichols network at different points. Both remain genuinely possible.

Rendy's Origin

Whether Rendy Blayn was born in Texas, Virginia, or South Carolina remains unresolved. The Blain/Blayn surname is documented in Virginia's Halifax County and separately in Laurens District, South Carolina, in the right era — but no single document has confirmed which, if either, is hers.

Where Amanda, William, and Harriet Went

Clarissa's three children are named in 1839 and plausibly present, as adults, in Claiborne Waldrop's 1860 Sabine Parish household — but none has been individually identified by name in any later record.

This Is the Whole Trail,
Walked Once, Start to Finish.

Every name on this page was real. Most were never asked their own name by anyone who wrote it down. Where this page says confirmed, a document says so in its own words. Where it says reasoned, it is because the people who lived this history were never given the dignity of being recorded — and reconstructing their lives from the shape of the households they were forced into is the closest this record can come to giving it back. This is not the end of the search. It is what the search has found so far, told honestly, in one place.
