The People Who Walked This Land.

A family story reconstructed from the records, the places, the fragments, and the questions that survived.

Prologue

I Did Not Begin With a Story.

I began with names. That is how this research started.

A name here.

A census there.

A family tree that seemed to connect one person to another.

A DNA match that raised another question.

A record that contradicted something I had been told. Then another record. And another.

The deeper I went, the more I understood that genealogy is not always the process of finding a clean line from one generation to the next. Sometimes it is the process of finding a person who was never supposed to be easy to find.

A person whose birth was never recorded.

A mother whose name appears only because someone sold her child.

A child who appears in a deed because someone considered him property.

A family relationship preserved accidentally in a will.

And sometimes — a single phrase in an old document changes everything. That is what happened to me.

Chapter One · South Carolina, 1793

Where the Paper Trail Begins

The family did not begin in 1793. But this is where one of my earliest surviving documentary trails begins.

The South Carolina upcountry was a landscape of rivers, farms, roads, settlements, and expanding agriculture. Newberry County had been created in 1785. This was also a society in which slavery was deeply embedded. But I am not beginning this story with cotton. I am beginning with a woman. Molley. And with a girl named Patience.

On March 22, 1793, James Sims Sr. mortgaged land and enslaved people to Peter Braselman. It was a financial document, not a family record. But inside it are names. Patience appears within a group that includes Isaac, Tabby, Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, and Tom. Molley appears with seven children.

The record gives me something slavery tried very hard to prevent descendants from having: a glimpse of people existing together.

But I have to be careful. The record does not prove that Molley was Patience's mother. It does not prove that the Patience in this mortgage is the same person who appears in every later record. It gives me a beginning. And sometimes a beginning is all a researcher gets.

Chapter Two · South Carolina, 1795

When a Person Becomes a Transaction

Documented

Two years later, another document brings Patience into view. August 4, 1795. Francis Davenport Sr. Patty Waldrop. One enslaved girl: Patience.

The record does not tell me what Patience thought. It does not tell me what she wanted. But it tells me something I cannot ignore: Patience changed hands. The witnesses were Isaac Davenport and Edward Turner. The transaction was recorded in Newberry Deed Book C.

The paper is precise. The human reality behind it is not.

Chapter Three

Patience and the Family Hidden Inside the Paperwork

The deeper I went, the more Patience stopped looking like a single name. She began looking like the center of a family. Later records connect Patience with people including Rachel, Clarissa, Tom, William, Amanda, and Harriet.

But the records do not preserve this family the way a modern family tree would. Their relationships appear in fragments. A deed. A transfer. An estate. A later recital of an earlier transaction. A child named here. A mother named there. A person disappearing from one household and appearing in another.

This is what reconstruction means. I take the fragments and ask: what is the strongest story these records allow me to tell? Not: what story do I wish they told?

Chapter Four

The Family Was Not the Same Thing as the Ownership

This became one of the most important lessons in my research. The legal records created one network. The family created another. The two networks overlapped. But they were not the same.

A person could belong legally to one household while their mother, sister, child, or grandchild belonged to someone else. A person could be transferred without the family relationship disappearing. That is why I now keep two maps.

Who held whom. Who transferred whom. Who inherited whom.

Chapter Five · 1839

A Family Is Divided on Paper

Strongly Supported / Research Reconstruction

By 1839, the family appears more clearly in the record.

Patience's daughter Rachel appears with her son, Tom.

Patience's daughter Clarissa appears with her children, William, Amanda, and Harriet.

The names are no longer just isolated entries in old documents. They begin to reveal a family structure:

Patience→Rachel→Tom
Patience→Clarissa→William, Amanda, Harriet

This is one of the most important family relationships preserved in the surviving records. The document was created to transfer people as property. But within that legal transaction, it also preserved pieces of a family.

The original 1839 deed still needs direct verification. Until then, this reconstruction is labeled strongly supported, not fully documented.

Chapter Seven

Easter: Two Women, Two Stories

Documented — Confirmed Separate

One of the most important things genealogy has taught me is that a repeated name is not automatically the same person. There are at least two different women named Easter in the records I am following.

One Easter was emancipated by John Pringle in 1817 — the record describes her as about forty, with him roughly twenty-eight years. Another Easter appears in the Davenport/Burton documentary network, first in 1792, then again decades later in the Burton estate records.

I do not merge these women simply because the name is the same. That is not a limitation of the story. It is part of the story.

Chapter Eight

The Sentence That Changed the Research

Documented

Jonathan Davenport's 1842 will contains one of the most important documentary relationships in this research. Bluford is identified as:

Son of Easter.

Not a guess. Not a DNA prediction. Not a family-tree algorithm. A written statement.

Sometimes one sentence survives that tells us what generations of silence tried to erase.

Chapter Nine

I Learned Not to Confuse Roles With Ownership

A recurring problem in historical records is assuming everyone named in an estate or transaction had the same role. A witness is not automatically an owner. An appraiser is not automatically an owner. An administrator is not automatically an owner. An executor is not automatically an owner. And a person with the same name is not automatically the same person.

This became more than a technical rule for me. It became a way of respecting the people whose identities I am trying to recover. I will not merge two people simply because doing so makes the family tree prettier.

Chapter Ten

Westward

The family story does not stay in one place. The map begins widening. South Carolina. Mississippi. Louisiana. Texas. The distances become enormous. But people moved. Owners moved. Families moved. Land changed hands. New settlements developed. And slavery moved with them.

The records place different generations and branches of this family in these regions. The exact routes taken by individual ancestors are still being reconstructed.

Chapter Eleven

Texas

Texas changes the landscape again. The geography is different. The political world is different. The records are different. Eventually the research reaches Austin County and Bellville.

Broader history of settlement, slavery, emancipation, and land provides real context — but it does not automatically prove that a specific event happened to a specific ancestor. This is where I have learned to distinguish the world around my ancestors from the things I can prove about my ancestors.

Chapter Twelve

The Nichols Family

John Nichols. Fanny. The Nichols name. The Bell connection. The enslaved people appearing in the Texas records. These form another layer of the family story.

But Texas also introduces a problem that follows African-American genealogy into the nineteenth century: many records were created primarily to document the interests of property holders, governments, churches, and employers — not to preserve complete biographies of the people being researched.

So I have to read the records sideways. Repeated names. Age patterns. Locations. Estate records. Neighbors. Witnesses. Later marriages. Children. Freedpeople's records. Death certificates. DNA. And the places where independent clues point in the same direction.

Chapter Thirteen

Rendy

Preserved Variants — Not Yet Resolved

One of the names I refuse to let disappear is Rendy. The surname appears in different forms, including Blayn or Bland, across the research trail. If I normalize everything too quickly, I may erase the evidence that something changed. So I preserve the variants.

I look at the original record. Who was around the person. The place. The age. Later descendants. And I ask: could these records belong to the same person? Sometimes the answer is yes. Sometimes it remains: not yet proven.

The goal is not to make the past cooperate with my tree. The goal is to recover the past as honestly as I can.

Chapter Fourteen

Easter Bell

Easter Bell was born July 4, 1838. I cannot know what her mother knew about the date. I cannot know who chose the name Easter. I cannot know what the Fourth of July meant to a Black child born enslaved in Texas.

But I know what that date means to me now — because she lived long enough for the world around her to change.

Chapter Fifteen

June 19, 1865

On June 19, 1865, General Gordon Granger's General Order Number 3 announced emancipation in Texas at Galveston. The news did not instantly erase the conditions created by generations of slavery, and the transition to freedom unfolded unevenly.

For people like Easter, freedom was not an abstract historical event. It changed what another human being was legally allowed to do to your body. It changed the meaning of movement. Of marriage. Of children. Of your own name. And the meaning of the word: family.

Chapter Sixteen

After Freedom

Freedom did not erase the past. It created a new problem: what do you do with freedom after generations of having almost none? Where do you live? How do you earn money? How do you keep your children together? How do you prove who you are? How do you marry? How do you claim land?

How do you build a family when the previous system spent generations breaking families apart? These are questions I think about when I look at the generations that followed.

Chapter Seventeen

Love After Ownership

I cannot prove everything about the emotional lives of my ancestors. I cannot know every relationship, every act of resistance, every moment of tenderness. But freedom changed the legal possibilities surrounding Black family life.

Marriage could become a legal institution entered by choice rather than an arrangement controlled through ownership. Children could be recognized as members of families rather than property. A household could become something a family built, rather than something assigned. That is why the generations after emancipation matter so much to me.

Chapter Nineteen

What Was Inherited

I used to think inheritance meant property. Land. Money. A house. A surname. Now I think inheritance is more complicated.

Some things are inherited through documents. Some through DNA. Some through stories. Some through silence. Some through the way a grandmother insists everyone stays close. Some through the fear of losing people. Some through the instinct to protect the family. Some through the questions nobody thought to ask.

I cannot scientifically prove every emotional connection I feel to these women. But I can say this: when I look at the generations of women who kept finding ways to carry family forward, I recognize something. I recognize the work.

Chapter Twenty-One

What I Believe May Have Happened

My Reconstruction

This is where I allow myself to tell the story the documents suggest — while remaining honest about where I cannot prove it.

I believe the story began long before the first document I have found.

I believe Molley may have been part of a family already living within the South Carolina enslaved community before Patience appears in the 1793 Sims mortgage.

I believe Patience became connected to the Davenport and Waldrop families through the 1795 transaction.

I believe the later records reveal that Patience was part of a family whose members included Rachel, Clarissa, and their children.

I believe slavery repeatedly interrupted that family without necessarily destroying the relationships themselves.

I believe some people whose names appear in separate households may have continued to know exactly who they belonged to, even when the law said otherwise.

I believe the westward movement of different family branches carried parts of the story into Mississippi, Louisiana, and eventually Texas.

I believe the Texas records contain descendants and family connections we are still learning how to reconstruct.

I believe Easter Bell stands at one of the most important thresholds in the story: the point where a woman born into slavery lived long enough to see the legal world around her change.

And I believe the generations after her carried more forward than names. They carried survival. They carried family. They carried memory. And eventually, they carried me.

Chapter Twenty-Two

What I Still Do Not Know

I do not know whether Molley was Patience's mother.

I do not know exactly what happened to Patience after every documented transaction.

I do not know whether every later woman named Patience belongs to the same documentary line.

I do not know the identity of every person in the 1839 family group.

I do not know whether every recurring name belongs to the same family.

I do not know whether every DNA connection points to the exact relationship I initially imagined.

I do not know the original African names of most of these ancestors.

I do not know every road they traveled, every place they slept, every person they loved.

I do not know how many children were born and never recorded, or how many names disappeared completely.

And I will not invent those answers.

Chapter Twenty-Four

The Documents Were Never Meant for Me

The people who created many of these records were documenting property, debt, land, inheritance, taxes, sales, estates, legal disputes, and ownership. They were not trying to preserve the memories of the people I am descended from.

And yet — their names survived.

Patience survived in a mortgage.

Patience survived in a deed.

Easter survived in an estate.

Bluford survived because someone wrote: son of Easter.

Rendy survived in a later record.

The family survived in fragments. And those fragments eventually reached me.

Final Chapter

I Am the Descendant Looking Back

I used to think the purpose of genealogy was to complete a tree. Now I think it is something else. It is to return humanity to people whose records often reduced them to names, ages, prices, and property descriptions.

I cannot undo what happened. I cannot give Patience her childhood back. I cannot tell Molley what happened to her children. I cannot reunite every family that slavery separated. I cannot recover every name that disappeared.

But I can refuse to let the surviving names disappear again. So I keep looking.

In deeds. In probate. In wills. In census records. In marriage records. In death certificates. In land records. In DNA. In maps. In places. In names that were misspelled. In names that changed. In names that appeared only once.

And now — I look at the land.

They walked this land before I knew their names.

They survived a world that tried to make them disappear.

And now I am walking backward to find them.

I am still looking.

Jasmine M. Lee

The research continues. Because there are still names waiting to be found.
