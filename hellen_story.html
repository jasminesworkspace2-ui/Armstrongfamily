September 1870 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Born Free

Hellen Nichols was born in September 1870 in Bellville, Austin County, Texas. She was the fifth child of Joseph Nichols and Easter Bell Nichols — born five years after Juneteenth, five years after her parents were freed, five years after her father walked into the voter registration office and put his name on the list.

She was born into a family that had survived everything. Her mother Easter had been born enslaved on the Fourth of July 1838 and freed at 27. Her father Joseph had been born enslaved in February 1845 and freed at 20. They had married four and a half years after Juneteenth. They had already had three children before Hellen arrived — Allen, Annie, and Oscar. Hellen was the first generation of her family to be born into freedom from the very first day of her life.

The world she was born into was not safe. Reconstruction was collapsing. The 1870 census that recorded her birth also recorded her father as a farm laborer who could not read or write. The community around her in Bellville was a formerly enslaved community trying to build lives on the same land where they had been held in bondage five years earlier.

But she was free. And that was the difference her parents had fought for.

1870 — 1898 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Growing Up on Piney Creek

The 1880 census confirms Hellen's presence in the Nichols household — though she does not appear by name in the indexed results. She would have been approximately 10 years old in 1880, one of six children in the household with Joseph age 37 and Easter age 33.

She grew up on the same land where her parents had been enslaved. The 2 acres that Easter and Joseph had acquired from the John Nichols Survey — Abstract 73 — was the family ground. The town of Bellville around them had been built on land that John Nichols had owned. The Bell family whose name was on the town was the same Bell family whose name Easter had taken as her own at emancipation.

Hellen's world was Bellville. She knew every family in that formerly enslaved community — the Armstrongs who had come from Upshur County, the Morgans who had come from Virginia through Austin County, the Nichols families who had been enslaved alongside her parents. The community she grew up in was built by people who had survived the same thing her family had survived.

She grew up and she stayed. She was approximately 28 years old when she married.

November 27, 1898 · Austin County, Texas

John Armstrong

On November 27, 1898 — Hellen Nichols married John Armstrong in Austin County, Texas. He was born in 1879 in Bellville — the son of David Armstrong and Clarissa Ann Morgan. David Armstrong had been enslaved by the Armstrong family in Western District, Upshur County, Texas and had come to Austin County after emancipation. John's mother Clarissa Ann Morgan was the daughter of Louisa Morgan — a formerly enslaved woman born ca. 1810 in Virginia who had come to Austin County Texas.

Two formerly enslaved families from the same Austin County community. John Armstrong's parents had been enslaved in Upshur County and Austin County. Hellen's parents had been enslaved on the Nichols plantation half a mile away. They had grown up in the same Bellville community — children of the same generation of formerly enslaved people — and they married.

By June 1900 — the first census after their marriage — the household shows John Armstrong age 22, Hellen Armstrong age 23, and infant Eva Armstrong age 4 months. Eva Armstrong was born June 27, 1900 — Hellen and John's daughter — the woman who would become Eva Armstrong Ballard.

John Armstrong ran his own transfer line — a truck driving and delivery operation — in Bellville. He was 22 years old, a business owner, a husband, a new father. The family was building something.

They had two children. One died before 1910 — unnamed in any surviving record. Eva survived.

August 30, 1911 · Fort Worth, Tarrant County, Texas

The Night Jack Thompson
Shot Her Husband Dead

By 1911 the family had moved from Bellville to Fort Worth. John Armstrong was working as a delivery wagon driver for the Denver News Service — headquartered inside the Terminal Hotel. He was 32 years old. Hellen was approximately 41. Eva was 11.

On the evening of August 30, 1911 — at 6:30 p.m. — John Armstrong was shot and killed near the East Front Street entrance to the Terminal Hotel in Fort Worth.

Fort Worth Daily Newspaper · August 31, 1911

NEGRO TRUCK DRIVER IS SHOT AND KILLED
JACK THOMPSON, WHITE MAN, SURRENDERS TO OFFICERS AT HEADQUARTERS
$3,000 BOND FURNISHED

Jack Thompson — who operated a transfer line business at 15th and Commerce Streets — shot and killed John Armstrong, a negro truck driver, near the East Front Street entrance to the Terminal Hotel at 6:30 o'clock Wednesday evening.

Thompson drove around the corner to Houston Street, met former police commissioner George H. Mulkey, and went to police headquarters to surrender. Justice Tom Maben fixed Thompson's bond at $3,000, which was promptly signed by several white bondsmen — Clarence Parker, George W. Mulkey, W.P. McLean Jr.

John Armstrong lived at 921 Arizona Avenue in Fort Worth with his family. He was employed to drive a delivery wagon for the Denver News Service.

Thompson's defense was self-defense. A white witness named Lon Sullins testified that John Armstrong had advanced on Thompson's buggy with a knife. The outcome of the hearing is not confirmed by primary source. But the pattern is familiar — a Black man dead at three feet, a white man walking free on bond signed by the city's most prominent white citizens within hours of the shooting.

John Armstrong was buried in Fort Worth. The death certificate lists the place of burial as blank.

1911 — 1930 · Fort Worth, Texas

After

Hellen Armstrong was widowed at approximately 41 years old with an 11-year-old daughter. She had no husband. She had no confirmed income. She was a Black woman in Fort Worth in 1911 — in a city where her husband had just been shot dead in the street and his killer had walked free on a $3,000 bond within hours.

At some point after John Armstrong's death, Hellen remarried. She took the surname Gilbert — becoming Helen Gilbert. When she remarried and who she married has not yet been confirmed by primary source. What is confirmed is that she appears in later records as Helen Gilbert rather than Helen Armstrong.

The 2006 Austin County tax suit — Cause No. 97V-003 — lists her as a named defendant: "Helen Armstrong, Also Known As: Helen Gilbert." This is the legal document that confirms her identity as Easter Bell and Joseph Nichols' daughter — named as a co-heir to the 2 acres of the John Nichols Survey alongside her siblings Allen, Oscar, Clara, and Willey. Even after death, her name remained on the land.

Eva Armstrong — Hellen's daughter — grew up in Fort Worth after her father's death. By 1920 Eva was approximately 20 years old. By 1929 Eva had married James Wilson Ballard Sr. and had children of her own.

In April 1930 — the last confirmed record of Hellen Nichols Armstrong — she is living with Eva at 1121 Pulaski Avenue in Fort Worth. She is listed as Helen Gilbert, widowed, age 59. Eva is the head of household. James Ballard is 3 years old. Margie Ballard is 1 year old.

Hellen Nichols Armstrong was living with her daughter and her grandchildren. James Ballard — who would grow up to become James Wilson Ballard Jr. She was present for the beginning of the next generation.

After April 1930, Hellen disappears from the documentary record. Her death certificate has not been located. Her burial place is unknown. The date and circumstances of her death have not been confirmed by primary source.

She Was Born Free.
She Outlived a Murdered Husband.
She Raised Eva.
She Disappears from the Record in 1930.

Hellen Nichols Armstrong was the first person in her direct line born free. Her mother Easter Bell was enslaved for twenty-seven years. Her father Joseph Nichols was enslaved for twenty. Hellen was born in September 1870 — five years after Juneteenth — into a world that was still dangerous for Black families in Texas, but into a world where she had rights her parents had never had.

She married. She had a daughter. Her husband was shot dead by a white man who walked free. She rebuilt. She appeared in the 1930 census living with Eva and her grandchildren — James and Margie — the next generation taking shape around her.

After April 1930 the record goes silent. Her death date is not confirmed. Her burial place is not known. What is known is that she raised Eva Armstrong Ballard — and Eva raised Margie Nell Ballard Corbin — and Margie raised the daughters who became the family that eventually produced Jasmine M. Lee.

The line held. Through enslavement and freedom and a murdered husband and Jim Crow Texas and the Great Migration to California — the line held. It held because Hellen held it.
