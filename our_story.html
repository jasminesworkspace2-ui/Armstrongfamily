A Personal Reflection · Seven Years of Research

What I've
Learned.

I thought I was looking for a family tree. I was really looking for the people who came before me — people whose names my family had lost, whose stories were broken apart, and whose surviving records became the puzzle pieces I had left.

By Jasmine M. Lee · Armstrong Family Archive

Where I Started

I Did Not Begin With a Story.

I began with names. And even that is more generous than it sounds.

Margie was the name I truly knew. She was real to me because she was part of my living memory. But before her, the family history became blurry. Texas was something I knew existed in our family's past, but for a long time it felt almost like a place without a story attached to it.

My mother did not know these names. The elders who might have known more were gone. There was no neatly preserved family Bible waiting for me. No complete list of ancestors. No one who could simply sit down and tell me where everybody came from.

So I started looking.

A name. A census. A document. A question.

Then another document that changed the question.

Then another.

The Search

I Had to Learn How to Look Backward.

At first I thought genealogy was mostly about finding the next parent and moving backward one generation at a time.

That works differently when slavery is part of the story.

There are generations where a surname disappears. Children appear in estate records instead of birth records. A mother may be named because somebody was documenting property. A family relationship can survive accidentally in a will. A person can appear once, disappear, and leave you searching for them for years.

I learned that I could not demand that the records tell the story in the order I wanted.

I had to follow the pieces wherever they led.

The records were never created to make it easy for my descendants to find me.

That changed how I read every document.
The Names

Then the Names Started Coming Back.

There is something difficult to explain about seeing an ancestor's name in an old document for the first time.

It is just ink. A line in a deed. A name in a will. A census entry. A marriage record. A death certificate.

But when you have spent years knowing almost nothing, the name feels enormous.

Patience was no longer just a name I had heard in a database. Easter was no longer just a woman in the distant past. Joseph was no longer just another ancestor to put in a box on a tree.

They became people I was responsible for remembering correctly.

The Puzzle

All I Had Were Puzzle Pieces.

That is the best way I know how to describe it.

The records are pieces of a puzzle. Some fit together beautifully. Some appear to belong together but need more proof. Some pieces are damaged. Some are missing completely.

And the hardest part is understanding why so many pieces are missing.

This was not simply a family that forgot to write things down. Slavery created systems in which Black people were documented primarily when somebody else needed to document property, labor, debt, inheritance, taxes, sales, estates, or legal status.

Families could be separated. Names could change. Children could be listed without parents. People could be moved from one state to another. A relationship that was obvious to the people living it might leave almost no surviving record for the descendant trying to reconstruct it generations later.

So when I cannot find an answer, I have learned not to automatically assume there was never an answer.

Sometimes the answer existed. The record simply did not survive.

What Slavery Changed

The Family Was Broken Apart Before I Ever Got Here.

I have had to sit with what that means.

My ancestors lived through a system that could treat a mother, her children, her husband, her siblings, and her parents as separate property interests. People could be inherited, sold, transferred, mortgaged, moved, or separated.

I cannot know what every one of those moments felt like. I cannot pretend to know the private grief of people whose voices did not survive.

But I can understand the structural reality: the family lines I am trying to reconstruct were sometimes being broken at the same time the records were being created.

That is why this research can take a lifetime to get right.

I am not only searching for ancestors. I am searching through the damage that was done to the record of their lives.

What the Records Taught Me

I Learned to Slow Down.

The longer I researched, the more I realized that being careful was not the opposite of finding answers. It was how I protected the truth of the answers I did find.

01

A Name Is a Clue.

A shared name can open a door. It cannot, by itself, prove that two records describe the same person.

02

A Record Has a Role.

A witness is not automatically an owner. An appraiser is not automatically an owner. A person named in a transaction may have a very different role from the person who created it.

03

DNA Is Evidence.

DNA can support a family connection and open new research paths. It does not give permission to skip the documentary work.

04

A Gap Is Still a Gap.

I would rather say “I don't know yet” than turn a reasonable possibility into a fact because I want the tree to be complete.

The Meaning of Freedom

I Understand Today's Freedom Differently Now.

Researching these generations changed the way I look at ordinary life.

Being able to choose where I live. To move. To marry. To raise children in my own household. To own property. To know my name. To search for my ancestors. To sit down with a document and ask questions about the people who came before me.

These things can feel ordinary when you have always had them.

They do not feel ordinary to me anymore.

When I read about people who lived under slavery, I think about the future they could not yet see. They made choices, raised children, protected relationships, survived, and sometimes left wishes for the generations that would come after them.

They could not know exactly what freedom would look like for us.

But I am living in a world they helped make possible.

They looked forward

They imagined a future.

They could hope that their children and grandchildren might live differently, even when their own lives were constrained by a system they did not control.

I look backward

I can finally see the distance.

I can look back across generations and understand that some of the freedoms I take for granted were once things my ancestors could only hope would exist.

The Honor of Finding Them

Sometimes I Look at a Document and Feel Honored.

That may be one of the biggest things I have learned.

There are moments when I am looking at a document and I stop thinking about the research problem. I stop thinking about whether a connection is going to fit into the tree.

I just think:

They made it this far.

The piece of paper survived. Their name survived. The relationship survived in some form. And now, generations later, I am holding the evidence.

That feels like an honor.

Not because everything was easy to find. It wasn't.

Because so much was lost, and something still made it through.

What I Inherited

I Used to Think Inheritance Meant Things.

Land. Money. A house. A surname.

Now I think inheritance is much larger than that.

Some things are inherited through documents. Some through DNA. Some through stories. Some through silence. Some through the way a family protects one another. Some through resilience. Some through questions that refuse to go away.

I inherited a life that my ancestors could not have fully imagined.

And the more I learn about them, the less I take that life for granted.

What I Cannot Recover

There Are Things I May Never Know.

I may never recover every name.

I may never know every mother, every father, every child, every sibling, every separation, every reunion, every road they traveled.

I may never find the document that closes a particular gap.

And there are pieces of the story that were destroyed long before I knew there was a puzzle.

That hurts.

But it has also taught me something important: an incomplete history is not a worthless history.

The surviving pieces still matter.

Why I Keep Looking

I Am Not Trying to Make the Past Perfect.

I am trying to make it visible.

I want to know who these people were. I want to understand what the records can actually tell me. I want to separate what is documented from what I believe may have happened. And when the evidence is not enough, I want to be honest about that too.

Because these people deserve more than being turned into a neat family tree just because a neat family tree feels satisfying.

They deserve to be remembered as people who lived through something real.

People who loved people.

People who lost people.

People who survived.

People who made choices with whatever freedom they had.

People who left pieces behind.

Looking Back

I Am the Descendant Looking Back.

I started this research because I wanted to know where I came from.

I did not realize how much learning where I came from would change the way I see where I am.

Texas is no longer just a vague place in the family past.

Margie is no longer the beginning of the story.

And the names I did not know before this research are no longer strangers to me.

I have learned that the distance between me and my ancestors is not only measured in years. It is measured in missing records, broken families, changing names, state lines, laws, and systems that were never built to preserve their stories.

But somehow, through all of that, pieces survived.

I am still looking for them. And now I understand why the search matters.

They were looking forward.

I am looking backward.

Somewhere between those two directions is the life I get to live.

Jasmine M. Lee · Armstrong Family Archive
