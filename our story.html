One Story, Start to Finish

Our Story.

Everything else on this site is organized by document, by household, by search. This page is organized the way a story is — one thing leading to the next. Read it straight through.

In 1793, in Union County, South Carolina, a woman named Molley was listed on a mortgage alongside her seven children — Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, Tom, and a daughter named Patience. The document that names them was written to secure a debt. James Sims, the man who owned them, needed money, and he put his own people up as the collateral that would guarantee it. That is the first time anyone in this family appears in writing. Not a birth record. Not a name given with love. A list of property, attached to a loan.

Two years later, in January 1795, a "Patience about 30 years old" was sold in open market in Newberry County to a merchant firm called Wadsworth and Turpin, alongside a woman named Moll and five others — Hannah, Lin, Violet, Esther, George. Seven months after that, in August 1795, a planter named Francis Davenport gave "one negro girl named Patience" to his own daughter, Patty, as she began her married life. Patty's married name was Waldrop. She had married a man named Hezekiah.

Patience spent the rest of her life inside that one family's orbit — Newberry County, South Carolina, then Georgia, then Tennessee, then Mississippi, as Hezekiah Waldrop's own household moved west across three decades. She had at least two daughters there: Rachel and Clarissa. Hezekiah's wife Patty died in 1825. For the next fourteen years, Hezekiah held Patience's family "in trust" — his to manage, not his to keep — until, on March 25, 1839, in Brandon, Mississippi, he finally divided them among his eight surviving children.

The document he signed that day is the single most important piece of paper in this whole story. It names Patience "and her increase — Rachel, Clarissa, Tom, Rachel's son, William and Amanda and Harriet, children of Clarissa." Three generations, one family, still together on paper after forty-four years.

They did not stay together after that.

Two Roads West

Rachel went to Hezekiah's son Stephen. Stephen sold her within five months — to his own father-in-law, a man named Howell Prestridge, for eight hundred and fifty dollars, along with a boy named Bryant. She was gone from this family's story almost as soon as the ink dried on the deed that was supposed to protect her.

Clarissa went, most likely, to Hezekiah's eldest son Claiborne — a man who had grown up alongside Patience's own family as a boy, back when they all lived under his father's roof in Mississippi in 1820. Claiborne moved his household to Sabine Parish, Louisiana. A census taken there in 1850 shows a woman aged sixty-three — almost certainly Patience herself, near the end of her life — and a woman aged forty-six who fits Clarissa exactly. Ten years later, in 1860, that same household had grown to eighteen people: children, grandchildren, a family that had not been broken apart, even if it had been carried far from where it started.

One of Hezekiah's other children took a different road entirely. His daughter Fanny had already left the family years before the 1839 deed was ever signed. She married young — first in Tennessee in 1817, then in Mississippi in 1821, and finally, in October 1837, in Austin County, Texas, to a man named John Nichols. Fanny received no direct share of Patience's family in the 1839 document. She was already gone, already building a different life, in a place none of the rest of them would ever see.

The Other Family

John Nichols had come to Texas in 1834. As a young man, before any of this, he had fought under Andrew Jackson at the Battle of New Orleans in 1815 — a soldier before he was ever a planter. By the time Fanny married him, he already held land and people on Piney Creek, in what would soon become the town of Bellville.

Somewhere in that household was a woman named Rendy Blayn, born around 1817. Almost nothing was ever written down about her directly — only a single line, on her own daughter's death certificate decades later, naming her as the mother. But a census taken in 1860 shows a forty-three-year-old woman in John Nichols's household — the exact age Rendy would have been that year — living alongside a household that reads, when you lay all nine names side by side, like a family: an older woman at the center, a younger woman likely her daughter, a cluster of children, and an eighteen-year-old young man who was very probably her son, Joseph.

A cousin who shares Jasmine's own DNA — she signs her research "Poppy," after her cat — believes something more specific than this site had ever put into words before her. She believes John Nichols was not only Joseph's enslaver, but his father. And she believes, going back one more generation, that Clarissa herself was not only Patience's daughter, but the daughter of Hezekiah Waldrop — fathered by the very man who held her mother. If she is right, then the men whose names appear again and again in this story as enslavers were, at the same time, the fathers of the children they enslaved. That is not a footnote. It may be close to the center of everything.

Easter

Across town, in the household of a family named Bell — the same family the town itself was named for — a girl named Easter was growing up. She was twelve years old in 1850, already working as a domestic servant. By 1860 she was nineteen, still in that same household, still enslaved. Her own birth date, July 4, 1838, is one of the very few facts about her early life that every record agrees on.

Whether Easter and Joseph already knew each other before freedom came — whether they had already found each other, the way two people in the same small town so often do — is something no document will ever answer. What is certain is that Easter had a daughter, Katie, born March 25, 1860, five years before slavery ended in Texas. Katie's own father is listed on every record as "don't know." She grew up carrying the surname Thornton, a name that matches a real man — Ely Thornton, enslaved in that same small community, in the very household of John Nichols's own widow. If Ely was her father, then it means an enslaver decided who Easter would have a child with, the same way an enslaver had decided everything else about her life. It does not mean Easter and Joseph did not love each other. It may mean exactly the opposite — that they loved each other before they were ever allowed to choose it for themselves.

"Joseph Nichols freeman and Easter Bell freedwoman... joined in Marriage as husband and wife."

On December 18, 1865 — six months after Juneteenth reached Texas — Joseph and Easter stood before the Austin County clerk and made it official. Their marriage was recorded in the same register, the same week, as another couple: Edward Bell and Eliza Nichols — a Bell man marrying a Nichols woman, the same week a Nichols man married a Bell woman. Two enslaved households, crossing into one family, in the very same room.

Joseph took the surname of the man who had held him. Easter took the surname of the family that had held her. Between them, they raised at least nine children — Allen, Annie, Josephine, Oscar, Henry, Hellen, Clara, Willie — and somewhere in that same generation, a young man named Alfred, four years older than Joseph, who research and a confirmed DNA match both now suggest was Joseph's own brother, carrying the Nichols name into his own family and his own line of descendants.

⁂

Hellen

Their daughter Hellen was born in September 1870 — five years into freedom, the first generation of this family born with the law, at least on paper, no longer able to sell her away from her mother. She married John Armstrong in 1898. John's own parents, Dave Armstrong and Clara Morgan, had come from South Carolina and North Carolina — the same two states, the same general region, where Patience's whole story had begun a full century earlier, on the other side of the country, in a family Hellen would never have known she was distantly, invisibly connected to.

John Armstrong was murdered in August 1911. Hellen's own later life scatters across the records the way so much of this family's history does — recorded once as Hellen Armstrong, once as Helen Gilbert, once, on a death certificate that may or may not belong to her at all, as Helen Reese. Whichever records are truly hers, one fact holds steady through all of them: she had a daughter, Eva, born June 1899, who carried this family forward into the twentieth century.

Eva, Margie, Hjordis

Eva Armstrong grew up without her father, in Fort Worth, and eventually made her way to San Antonio. There, she ran a business of her own — a cafe called The Spot, at the corner of Nolan and North New Braunfels — and by 1944 she had bought land of her own, on her own terms, the only property in this entire story that was purchased freely and sold freely, never taken by force or by tax sale. Her daughter Margie Nell Ballard Corbin carried the family forward again. Margie's daughter, Hjordis, is Jasmine's own mother.

Which means every name in this story — Molley, Patience, Rachel, Clarissa, Fanny, Rendy, Joseph, Easter, Katie, Hellen, Eva, Margie — belongs to the same unbroken line of women and men who came before Jasmine, across two hundred and thirty years, five states, and a distance that once would have been impossible to imagine closing.

⁂

Some of what's written here is confirmed by a signed document — a deed, a marriage license, a death certificate, a will. Some of it is the best reading of a household laid out on a census page, reasoned out the way you'd reason out any family too poor, too enslaved, too unwritten-about to leave behind anything cleaner. And some of it — Poppy's own theory about who fathered Clarissa and Joseph, the question of Katie's own father, exactly where Rendy came from before Texas — is still genuinely open, still being searched for, one document at a time, right now.

That's not a weakness in this story. It's the truth of what it actually takes to find a family that was never supposed to be found at all.
