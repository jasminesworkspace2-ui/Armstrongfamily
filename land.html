Three Properties · Three Stories · 1856 to 2017

The Land.

What was claimed. What was held. What was lost.

This family tried to own land three separate times, in three separate counties, across three separate generations. One tract was sold legally, on fair terms, before anyone in this family had ever heard the word "heir." Two others were taken by tax sale — one in 2006, one in 2017 — sold at the courthouse steps for a fraction of their worth, decades after the people whose names were still on the title had died. This page tells all three stories, together, for the first time.

Property One · Lost 2006
Bellville, Austin County, Texas The Bellville Land 2 acres · J. Nichols Survey · Abstract No. 73 Lost — Tax Sale — August 1, 2006

Sometime after emancipation in 1865, Easter Bell's family acquired two acres inside the same J. Nichols Survey where she had been born enslaved. They held that land — through Reconstruction, through Jim Crow, through the Great Migration that carried so many of their relatives to Fort Worth and California — for 141 years.

Easter Bell Nichols died December 17, 1935, at age 97. Her name stayed on the title. So did the names of her children — Allen, Oscar, Clara, Willie, Hellen — and their children after them, as the fractional ownership split further with every generation and no single administrator ever consolidated it.

In 1997, Austin County and the Bellville Independent School District filed a delinquent tax suit — Cause No. 97V-003 — naming every heir the county could identify, living and dead. Easter Pink (her name by her second marriage, to Hannibal Pink) was listed as a defendant sixty-two years after her death, because her name was still legally attached to the land.

Judgment was rendered December 14, 1999. The Sheriff's order of sale was issued June 23, 2006. On August 1, 2006 — the first Tuesday of the month, as Texas law requires for tax foreclosure sales — Sheriff R. DeWayne Burger sold the land at the courthouse. It was conveyed to Austin County for the benefit of Bellville ISD.

Property Two · Lost 2017 · Most Actionable
Waco, McLennan County, Texas The Waco Land 724 East Johnson Street Lost — Tax Sale — November 7, 2017

Rev. George W. Ballard — son of Newton Ballard and Rebecca Ballard, ordained minister, husband of Mary Ann Edwards Ballard — owned this property in Waco, recorded in McLennan County Deed Records, Volume 289, Page 655.

He died October 24, 1937, at age 76. His death certificate, McLennan County Volume 4, Page 483, formally names Newton Ballard as his father — the document that closes the century-long gap between an unnamed enslaved child on a Mississippi plantation and the family that carries his name today.

The property outlived him by eighty years before it was lost. Austin County tax suit Cause No. 2014-3167-4 led to a sheriff's sale on November 7, 2017 — the land sold to the McQuade family for $1,600.

Property Three · Sold Legally · 1873
Upshur County, Texas Newton Ballard's 160 Acres Preemption Survey No. 124 · Abstract 131 · Caney Creek Legal Sale — No Recourse

In August 1856 — six years before emancipation, while still legally enslaved — Newton Ballard walked into the Upshur County land office and filed a 160-acre preemption homestead claim, Texas General Land Office File No. 3378. In February 1857 he signed a sworn Settler's Affidavit stating he was the bona fide occupant, clearing the timber, working the land himself.

The white county clerk filed the jacket under the misspelled name Newton Bullard — a vowel shift that caused this record to disappear from search for over 150 years. But the 1859 tax roll tells the truth: in the column for who rendered the property, the clerk wrote Ballard, with an A, exactly as the family carried the name forward.

By 1870 Newton is absent from the Upshur County census. His wife Rebecca Ballard heads the household alone, raising Silla, Sandy, and George Washington Ballard. In August 1873, Newton reappears one final time — signing a deed transferring his 160-acre survey to E.G. Horn for $250 in gold, notarized not in Upshur County but in neighboring Rains County. He walks away with the money and disappears from the primary source record. His death date and burial location have never been found.

This sale was legal, fairly conducted, and fully documented by Newton's own signature. There is nothing to contest and no recourse to pursue — it is included here because it is part of the same story: a man who claimed land before he was even legally free, and chose, on his own terms, when to let it go.

The Pattern · Same Survey, Different Decades

The John Nichols Survey — the same 4,428-acre grant where Easter Bell was born enslaved in 1848 — was actively being bought and sold around your family's two acres for the entire 20th century. "J. Nichols league," "Jno. Nichols league," and "John Nichols survey" remained the standard legal description used in dozens of Bellville real estate transactions, decades after emancipation, while your family held onto their own small piece of the same ground.

The Austin County Jail · Built on John Nichols's Original Land

The Austin County Jail in Bellville — the building where Joseph Nichols, Oscar Nichols, Hellen Nichols Armstrong, John Armstrong, and four-month-old Eva Armstrong were all counted in the June 1900 census — was built in 1896 on land originally granted to John Nichols by the Mexican government on October 20, 1832.

Nichols sold portions of that original grant to James and Thomas Bell in the 1830s. That land became the town of Bellville — named for the Bell family — sitting on the same survey where your ancestors were enslaved. John Nichols's estate in 1864 listed ten enslaved people. He died before emancipation reached Texas. His widow and estate held Easter Bell and Rendy Blayn until June 19, 1865.

Source · National Register of Historic Places · Austin County Jail, Bellville TX

"This land had been granted to John Nichols on October 20, 1832 by the Mexican Government. Nichols over the next several years sold portions of his original grant to James and Thomas Bell. In March of 1846, Austin County purchased a total of 145½ acres of land from the Bells for a sum of $2.00." The jail was built on Lot 6 of Block F of this original Nichols land grant.

Two Acres. Twenty-Four Words.
One Hundred Forty-One Years.

Cause No. 97V-003. Cause No. 2014-3167-4. Two case numbers, two counties, two tax sales, eleven years apart, taking land from the same family. Land that had been worked without pay is not the same as land that was later owned outright — but owning it, holding it, passing it down for generations, was still real. It mattered. Losing it mattered too. This page exists so that the loss is recorded the same way the survival was: with names, with dates, with sources, in full.
