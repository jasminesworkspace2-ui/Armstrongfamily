Where He Actually Came From

The 1860 free population census — the same schedule that lists his wife and children by name — records John Nichols's own birthplace as Louisiana, ca. 1797. This is the single strongest primary source statement of his origin found anywhere in this research.

Beyond that one fact, his own parents, siblings, and childhood remain unidentified. No will, no probate, no earlier census has surfaced a father's name, a mother's name, or a sibling for John Nichols. This is recorded here honestly, the same way this archive holds every unresolved origin — not filled in with a guess, left open until a document says otherwise.

The Marriage That Bound Two Enslaving Families

On October 14, 1837, John Nichols married Frances "Fanny" Waldrop in Austin County, Texas. She was the daughter of Ezekiah Parrish Waldrop and Martha "Patsy" Davenport Waldrop — the same Ezekiah Waldrop already documented on this archive's Waldrop enslaver page as the man who held Patience for over thirty years, and the same Patsy Davenport who received Patience as a gift from her own father, Francis Davenport, in 1795.

Fanny was born in South Carolina around 1802 — five years after her own father is first documented in Newberry County. She grew up inside the same household that held Patience and, later, Clarissa. She left that household for Texas well before her father ever executed the March 25, 1839 deed dividing Patience's family among his eight children — which is why she received no direct share of Patience's own children in that document. She was already building a separate life a thousand miles away.

1839 Rankin County, Mississippi Deed — Deed Book 4, Page 435
"...Fanny Micholes..." — one of eight children of Hezekiah Parrish Waldrop and the late Martha "Patsy" Davenport Waldrop named as a recipient of Patience's family. Fanny received no direct share; she is named as an heir of record, already resident in Texas.

Researcher note, typed directly on the original document by Kathryn R. Bonner (Mrs. W.G.), Marianna, AR — descendant of Wiley B. Waldrop: "FANNY MICHOLES is really NICHOLS. FANNY WALDROP married in Austin Co., TEXAS, 14 Oct., 1837. JOHN NICHOLS — I have had this mar. rec. for many years… krb"

Fanny's own seven confirmed siblings — Claiborne Parrish Waldrop, James D. Waldrop, Stephen R. Waldrop, Wiley B. Waldrop, Patsy Stoddard, Sarah Robertson, and Selina Robertson — are the same eight people (with Fanny) who divided Patience, Rachel, Clarissa, Tom, William, Amanda, and Harriet among themselves in 1839. John Nichols married directly into the family that had held this archive's oldest confirmed ancestors for over four decades before he ever met Fanny.

What the Law Actually Paid Him

The Bellville reconstruction page already documents John Nichols's land at its peak — 4,000 acres, the Stephenson grant, the Austin-Hodge grant, the sale to the Bell brothers that founded the town. What it does not walk through is why the colonization system handed land out in the specific amounts it did.

Stephen F. Austin's original 1821 colonization terms paid settlers directly, in acreage, for the people in their household — including the people they enslaved: 640 acres for a single man, 320 more for a wife, 160 for each child, and 80 acres for every person he held in bondage. Read against the Stephen F. Austin Register entry already confirmed on the community page — "John Nicholls, age 33, wife Fanny age 31, two children, one other dependent" — that formula accounts for a real, calculable share of his earliest Texas landholding before his 1832 grant was even issued: 640 for himself, 320 for Fanny, 320 for two children, and 80 more for the one enslaved dependent he already held. Eight hundred acres of that earliest calculation existed because of the people in his household. Eighty of it existed because of one person he owned.

640 acres for a man. 320 for his wife. 80 for every person he enslaved.

— the ratio Mexican colonial law assigned to a human being held in bondage, one quarter the value of a wife, half the value of a child

This is not a claim that his full 4,000-acre estate was built entirely on this formula — his 1832 grant of one league and one labor came through a separate, later colonization process. It is a claim that the earliest, foundational logic of Texas land distribution treated an enslaved human being as a unit of value the government paid for, the same as it paid for a wife or a child — and John Nichols's own household, on the record his own arrival register shows, was compensated on exactly that basis from his very first year in Texas.

Confirmed by primary source · Austin County Deed Records, Vol. L No. 220, Pages 238–244

Ten People, Named at His Death

John Nichols died March 3, 1864. The Bellville community page confirms his own probate file has never been located in the Austin County courthouse index — searched under every spelling variant, with no entry found. But a separate deed record, filed the same year, survives.

Austin County Deed Records, Vol. L No. 220, Pages 238–244 · Signed March 21, 1864 · Filed September 2, 1864
"There were ten negroes in the possession of the said John Nichols deceased at the time of his death."

Ten people, not the nine recorded on the 1860 slave schedule four years earlier — a real, unreconciled discrepancy worth naming plainly rather than smoothing over. Either a birth added a tenth person to the household between 1860 and 1864, or the 1860 schedule itself undercounted by one. This document names the property as belonging to Fanny Nichols, his widow, not to a probate estate for John himself — meaning ownership transferred to her directly rather than through the missing probate process, which may be exactly why no probate file was ever indexed under his own name at all.
