Newton Ballard

ca. 1830 Mississippi · Copiah Co. MS → Smith Co. TX → Upshur Co. TX → Rains Co. TX · ca. 1880

Born into enslavement. Survived a financial crisis that scattered two members of his community forever. Traveled from Mississippi to Texas in 1850 as an unnamed 20-year-old on a slave schedule. Filed his own 160-acre land claim in 1856 — before emancipation — and signed his own sworn affidavit. Father of Rev. George Washington Ballard. The Story

Newton Ballard was born around 1830 in Copiah County, Mississippi — held by a man named Lewis F. Ballard Sr., who had been born in Pensacola, Florida around 1792, the son of a Revolutionary War veteran from Nash County, North Carolina named Francis Ballard. Lewis F. Ballard Sr. came to Mississippi with his family, acquired land, and built what appeared to be a stable plantation life. By 1840 he held 517 acres and eight enslaved people. Newton was one of them — approximately ten years old.

That stability was already crumbling. Lewis F. Ballard Sr. had taken out three overlapping bank debts: a Union Bank Stock Mortgage in May 1839, a $3,060 Deed of Trust to the Grand Gulf Railroad and Banking Company in April 1840, and a default judgment from the Bank of Port Gibson of $476.41 in March 1840. The Panic of 1837 had done its work. In April 1840 five of the eight enslaved people in the household were mortgaged as collateral — Judy, Scylla, Aaron, Emaline, and Rebecca. Newton, approximately ten years old, was not among those mortgaged.

In September 1845 the foreclosure came. Trustee William Barnes — the same William Barnes who had witnessed a dower release in this county in 1832 — put the plantation up for auction at the courthouse door in Gallatin, Mississippi. It was Mary Ballard, Lewis's wife, who stepped forward and purchased the entire 517-acre estate back for $917. That act kept Newton and the surviving members of his community intact. At least two of the adult men in the household — unnamed, unrecorded beyond the bracket of the 1840 census — were almost certainly lost in the years of financial crisis. They do not appear in Texas.

In 1850 the family sold their Mississippi land and joined the westward migration to East Texas, settling in Smith County. Newton appears on the September 25 1850 slave schedule as an unnamed male age 20. The other four enslaved people in the household match the age profiles of Scylla (now approximately 36), Emaline (approximately 16), and Rebecca (approximately 11) — the women and children who had survived the Mississippi crisis alongside him.

Then Newton did something remarkable. In August 1856 — six years before emancipation, while still legally enslaved — he walked into the Upshur County land office and filed a 160-acre preemption homestead claim. In February 1857 he signed a sworn Settler's Affidavit stating he was the bona fide occupant, clearing the timber, working the land. The white county clerk spelled his name Newton Bullard in the file jacket — a vowel shift that would cause modern search engines to miss him entirely. But the 1859 tax roll tells the truth: in the column for who rendered the property, the clerk wrote Ballard, with an A, exactly as the family carried the name forward.

By 1870 Newton is absent from the Upshur County census. His wife Rebecca Ballard heads the household — age 35, born Mississippi — raising their three children: Silla age 13, Sandy age 10, and George Washington Ballard age 8. The name Silla is not accidental. It carries Scylla forward — the woman from the 1840 Mississippi deed, the elder of Newton's plantation community, honored in the naming of his granddaughter two generations later.

In August 1873 Newton comes forward one last time. He signs a deed transferring his 160-acre preemption survey to E.G. Horn for 250 gold dollars. He does not sign it in Gilmer — he signs it before a notary in Rains County, the newly formed territory to the west. He walks away with 250 gold dollars and disappears from the primary source record. The Upshur County assessor moves Abstract 131 to the unrendered roll, marks the owner as Unknown, and alternates between the two spellings — Ballard and Bullard — until the land is absorbed by the state. Newton's death date and burial location have not been found.

On September 4 1937, his son George Washington Ballard died in Waco, McLennan County, Texas at age 76. His death certificate — McLennan County Volume 4, Page 483 — formally names Newton Ballard as his father. That document closes a century of distance between an unnamed child on a Mississippi plantation and the family that carries his name today.

The Louisa Hypothesis · Research Status
Substantially Supported · Not Yet Confirmed by Primary Source

Three primary source documents place the John Bullard estate enslaved community in Upshur County Texas at the same time Newton filed his land claim — the 1854 Faulk Donation Deed (Ouachita Parish, Louisiana) transferring Louiza and her family from the John Bullard estate to Texas; the July 30 1856 Louisiana Sheriff's Deed explicitly confirming this family descended from the estate of her father John Bullard, executed 27 days before Newton's land claim; and the 1860 Upshur County slave schedule showing William Shepperd holding a family group consistent with Louiza's children's ages. Newton's land bordered the John Bullard survey. The Anson County North Carolina Bullard family has been documented continuously from 1778 through 1893 — with consistent Bullard/Ballard surname fluidity matching what Newton's records show in Texas. However, the connection between Newton's enslaver Lewis F. Ballard Sr. — whose family originated in Nash County North Carolina, not Anson County — and the John Bullard family has not been confirmed by primary source. The geographic convergence is documented. The biological or community origin connection remains an open research question.

Open Research Questions
Active Research Targets
Smith County Texas probate records 1850–1860 — Lewis F. Ballard Sr. estate — would name Newton individually and document the transfer of enslaved people after his death.
Copiah County Deed Book G Pages 385–386 — the 1839 Union Bank Stock Mortgage front pages — highest probability location to find Newton's birth mother or his name recorded as an infant.
Copiah County Deed Book B Page 117 — Smith neighbor indemnity bond — may name the two unnamed adult males lost to the bank crisis.
Rains County Grantee/Grantor Index 1873–1885 — where Newton went after selling his land for 250 gold dollars — search under both Ballard and Bullard spellings.
Newton Ballard death record — date and location of death unconfirmed. No death certificate or burial record found in any Texas county.
Judy's fate — named in 1840 Grand Gulf Bank deed, not traced to Texas. Courthouse auction purchaser records September 15 1845 may identify who acquired her.
Scylla / 1840 census discrepancy — deed names both Judy and Scylla as females age 24–35 but census shows only one female in that bracket. Requires resolution.
Ouachita Parish Louisiana succession records for John Bullard — would confirm his origin and connect the Louisiana estate to Anson County North Carolina.
