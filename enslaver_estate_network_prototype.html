Enslaver & Estate Network

Follow the families, children, marriages, estates, land and legal records that controlled the movement of enslaved people. This is not the Armstrong family tree. It is a documentary research network built to help recover the lives of the people those records tried to reduce to property.

IMPORTANT: A family connection does not automatically make someone a documented enslaver. Status badges below reflect the evidence currently attached to that individual or household.

How to read the research status

Documented identityThe person or relationship is directly named in a cited record.
Recipient path documentedA record identifies the person or household receiving an assignment or transfer. This does not automatically establish kinship or every later ownership claim.
Continuity openThe historical person is documented, but a later or earlier identity bridge has not yet been proved.
Original-image reviewA transcription, name, lot, date or wording must be checked against the underlying record before a stronger conclusion is made.
UnresolvedThe archive preserves the question or candidate path but does not present it as fact.

Fanny / Nancy Jane Waldrop ↗
Mississippi → Texas
Bridge person · status requires record-by-record precision
Established sequence: James Hillin (1817, Maury County, TN) → Anthony Hutchins (12 Jul 1821, Lawrence County, MS) → John/Martha Hutchins → John Nichols household in Texas by Mar 1827 → legal John/Fanny marriage (14 Oct 1837, Austin County, TX). Identity control · do not merge
Recurring names are separate research identities unless a continuity record connects them.
Fanny (1864 Nichols valuation, born ca. 1809) ≠ Fanny Waldrop Nichols (born ca. 1802) ≠ Fanny, infant daughter of Milley in the 1832–1835 Tennessee litigation.
Amanda (1864 Nichols valuation, born ca. 1843) ≠ Amanda Brown (born ca. 1830) ≠ Clarissa’s daughter Amanda named in the 1839 Waldrop instrument.
Solomon (1864 Nichols estate, born ca. 1857) ≠ Solomon, son of Sarah, named in Jonathan Davenport’s 1842 will.
Ned (1864 Nichols valuation, born ca. 1845) ≠ earlier Tennessee Nichols-network Ned unless continuity is proved.
Mary, Isaac and Martha each recur elsewhere in the archive; no same-name record is merged across estates or generations without a documentary bridge.
Thomas (1864 Nichols estate, born ca. 1858) remains separate from the later Thomas Nichols freedman unless post-emancipation continuity is established.

AUG. 4, 1795
Francis Davenport Sr. → Patty Waldrop → Patience

Newberry County deed places Patience in Patty's legal property history. This is the opening anchor for the later Waldrop chain.

JUL. 30, 1827
Ezekiah Waldrop → Stephen R. Waldrop

Hinds County deed names Patience, Rachel, Clara and Tom/Thomas. Clara → Clarissa is treated as a supported identification because the later family cluster aligns.

MAR. 25, 1839
The eight Waldrop children and Patience's “increase”

Rankin County Deed Book 4, p. 435 recites the 1795 gift and preserves Patience → Rachel → Tom and Patience → Clarissa → William, Amanda, Harriet. The eight children are named together as joint grantees; the instrument does not divide the named enslaved family into eight individual allotments.

1839 → TEXAS
The missing allocation / movement record

Fanny/Nancy Jane Waldrop is one of the eight children named jointly as grantees in the March 25, 1839 instrument and is independently documented in Texas with John Nicholls by 1827. The unresolved question is which enslaved family members, if any, became part of Fanny's individual interest or household.

PRE-1865
John Nichols household becomes a documented slaveholding site

A tax transcription records John with four enslaved people; the 1860 slave schedule records nine unnamed enslaved people. These records establish slaveholding but do not individually identify Joseph, Henry, Easter, Rendy or another person.

1866–1870
Black Nichols community becomes visible by name

Joe Nichols appears in the 1866 tax index; Joe and Henry Nichols appear on the 1868 Austin County Freedmen's poll-tax list; Henry Nicholas appears in Bellville in 1870 and operates a farm. This is post-emancipation continuity evidence, not a standalone proof of a specific 1860 schedule identity.

Working reconstruction: the combined surname, county, immediate post-emancipation presence, documented Nichols slaveholding, land/community continuity and Fanny's Waldrop inheritance position make the Davenport/Waldrop → Nichols pathway a serious reconstruction to test. The research task is now to identify the mechanism and individuals — not to pretend the networks are unrelated until a single perfect sentence is found.
