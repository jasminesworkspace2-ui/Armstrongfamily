To every member of this family —

Seven years ago I started asking questions. Simple ones at first. Where did we come from? Who were the people before Grandma Margie? What were their names? Where did they live? What happened to them?

What I found changed how I understand myself, this family, and what it means to be who we are. I found Easter Bell Nichols — born enslaved on July 4, 1848, in Bellville, Texas. I found her mother Clarissa, and Clarissa's mother Patience, who was born in South Carolina in the 1780s. I found a great-grandmother named Eva Armstrong Ballard who ran a café on San Antonio's East Side and was subpoenaed to testify in a police brutality case in 1955 — and told the truth. I found a great-uncle named James Wilson Ballard Jr. who made the front page of the San Antonio Light for helping break a cover-up case. I found ministers and porters and waitresses and gamblers and community organizers. I found people who built things under impossible conditions and kept going.

The more I searched, the more I realized I didn't know much. You can live your entire life believing things were seamless or perfect because of the love you share for one another. But the more you learn, you find out your loved ones were human — their past wasn't easy or smooth. And you start to understand your own defaults, your blessings, and the generational curses that were passed down without anyone meaning to pass them. You realize you are not starting from scratch. You are starting from somewhere. That somewhere is what this history is about.

My mother is Hjordis L. Corbin — born January 20, 1960, the daughter of Margie Nell Ballard Corbin. She grew up at 622 South Townsend Street in Santa Ana and later at 12182 Candy Lane in Garden Grove — the home where I have my earliest memories of NaNa. Hjordis carried the weight of losing her mother at 28 years old, navigated a probate process no one explained to her, and kept going. She is the reason I know this family at all. She is the thread that connects me to everything in this history.

"Teach your children who they are. Tell them Easter Bell's name. Tell them that Margie Nell Ballard made it from Fort Worth to California with seven children and kept going. Tell them they come from people who kept going."

I built this website so that what I found does not disappear. So that the names don't get lost again. So that the next generation doesn't have to start from zero the way I did.

This research is ongoing. Every generation adds to the story. What matters is that the foundation is here, sourced to primary documents, and honest about what we know and what we don't. If you have family photographs, documents, stories, or information about any family member — please reach out. Every piece helps.

A Personal Memory · Jasmine M. Lee

“Growing up on Candy Lane was love — real, warm, full love — mixed with the complications of generational things none of us had names for yet.”

Losing a grandmother at a young age does something to a family that is hard to put into words. It leaves a shape in the room where someone used to be. And the strange thing is — some of us were old enough to remember, and some of us were not. I sit somewhere in between. Old enough to carry the feeling, young enough that I have spent years reaching back through records and photographs trying to fill in what memory couldn't hold. I hear about the love she had for people. The parties. The holiday celebrations. The way her house was never empty and never quiet. I try to live vicariously through the records and imagine what that must have felt like — to walk into that house on a Christmas morning, to smell whatever she was cooking, to hear the noise of a full family that had no idea what was coming.

What I do remember, I hold onto tightly.

The house was a wrap-around — you could come in through the front door or through another entrance that led straight into the pool table room, which sat right in the middle of everything like the heart of the house. Uncle Ronnie had made himself a room in the garage. Aunt Se'Taun's room was the first one on the right — she drove a yellow Bug. NaNa's room was at the very back. Five bedrooms. Pictures on the walls. A home that looked like somebody actually lived there and loved it.

She ran a licensed board and care home — adults with mental or developmental disabilities who lived with the family as part of the family. Her house was always full. The clients were not separate from us. They were just there, part of the rhythm of the house, part of what normal looked like on Candy Lane. That was her calling — caring for people who needed a home when the world wasn't built to hold them. She built one herself and opened the door.

I remember Christmas there. I remember the sibling arguments — the kind that are loud and fast and forgotten by dinner. I remember Jackson, the family pet turtle, slow and unbothered by all of it. I remember NaNa driving a station wagon, wearing a reddish-colored wig — though her own hair underneath was long and beautiful and naturally reddish-brown. She always smelled amazing. She made pudding for dinner like it was the most natural thing in the world. One year she gave us Nintendos. We didn't live far from Disneyland.

She made sure we grew up without having to feel the weight of the things so many Black families carry — the worry, the scarcity, the instability. We didn't know we were being shielded. We just accepted what was there, and loved what we had. That is what she gave us. And losing her — at 59, too soon, before most of us were old enough to ask her the questions that matter — is something this family is still reckoning with. This website is part of that reckoning. This is me trying to give back what time took.

Primary Source · 1795 SC Deed · Confirmed

"Patience, a negro woman, and her increase" — given as property at the marriage of Patty Waldrop · Newberry County, South Carolina · 1795

This document names your 5th great-grandmother. She was a person. The deed treated her as property. Her daughter Clarissa was "the increase" — already born or yet to be born, also property under this document.

Primary Source · 1827 Bill of Sale · Confirmed

Clarissa is sold — separated from Patience — by Ezekiah Waldrop to his son Stephen R. Waldrop · Hinds County, Mississippi · 1827

Mother and daughter separated by a bill of sale. Clarissa was approximately 20 years old. This is the last document to place them in the same household.

Primary Source · 1864 John Nichols Estate · Confirmed

"There were ten negroes in the possession of the said John Nichols deceased at the time of his death" — Austin County Deed Records, Vol. L No. 220, Pages 238–244

Signed March 21, 1864 · Filed September 2, 1864 · This document establishes the John Nichols estate on Piney Creek, Bellville, Texas — where Easter Bell was born in 1848. The ten people named as Fanny Nichols's property almost certainly include Easter Bell and her children.

June 19, 1865 · Juneteenth

Easter Bell is freed at 17. She does not leave Bellville. She stays on the same Piney Creek land — and makes it hers.

December 18, 1865 — six months after Juneteenth — Easter Bell marries Joseph Nichols in Austin County, Texas. She is 17. They will have eight children together. She will outlive him and outlive her second husband. She dies in 1935 at 87 years old — free, on Texas soil, surrounded by her descendants.

A Note on DNA Matches to Waldrop and Nichols Descendants

The Waldrop and Nichols families who enslaved your ancestors are not your ancestors by descent. They are the enslaving families. DNA connections to their descendants appear in ThruLines because enslaved people had children with or were assaulted by enslavers — that DNA persists in your genome. But the Waldrops and Nicholses are not your family in the chosen sense. They are part of the historical record.

Eldest · Michael E. Corbin Sr.

Michael E. Corbin Sr.

August 26, 1951 – November 1, 2009 · Las Vegas, NV

Michael spent the majority of his life in Las Vegas, Nevada, raising his two daughters and a son — Jamie, and Tierra, whom he raised as his own even though DNA later revealed she was not his biological child. He loved her like she was. Michael worked for the telephone company for many years. He experienced alcoholism throughout his life and passed away from a rare disease. He died not long after Dock Ellis, and Shirley — his ex-wife — passed away shortly after him. Deserae speaks often about her dad and her memories of him. Dominique stayed with him in his last days. San Antonio · 1954 · Police Brutality

The Caldwell Case

The night a man was killed in Eva's back office — and your great-grandmother told the truth when she didn't have to.

What Happened · San Antonio, Texas · 1954–1955

Johnie W. Caldwell was shot and killed by San Antonio police officer O.R. Graves in the back office of Eva Armstrong Ballard's establishment on the East Side. The officers who responded claimed Caldwell had been armed and threatening. Eva gave investigators the key to the office. She cooperated fully. She told the truth about what she saw.

The Cover-Up · What the Officers Did

Two officers later confessed to planting a knife on Caldwell's body after the shooting. The knife was used to justify the killing — planted evidence to make a murdered man look dangerous. When that came out, the case became front-page news.

No charges were filed against Eva. She had done nothing wrong. But being a Black woman who ran a business on San Antonio's East Side and was subpoenaed to testify in a police brutality case in 1955 — and who told the truth on that stand — was not nothing. That took something.

James Wilson Ballard Jr. · Front Page · San Antonio Light

Eva's son James Wilson Ballard Jr. was also connected to the case — he lived with Eva at 150 Paul Street and was listed on the federal gambling tax stamp records at the establishment at 207 N. Center Street. He made the front page of the San Antonio Light in 1954. He was part of breaking open the cover-up.

Note on the gambling tax stamp: This was an IRS tax under the Revenue Act of 1951 on what were effectively illegal gambling operations. It was not a voting record or a criminal conviction — it was a federal tax stamp required to operate. Many Black-owned East Side businesses held these. It reflects the economic reality of Black entrepreneurship in 1950s San Antonio, not a moral failing.

January 22, 1957 · Eva Leaves San Antonio

On January 22, 1957 — two years after the trial — Eva Armstrong Ballard sold 150 Paul Street in San Antonio and left Texas forever. She moved west. She settled in Garden Grove, California. She never looked back. Whether the Caldwell case was part of why she left, we cannot say for certain. San Antonio, Texas · 1930s–1957

The East Side

Before California, this family built something in San Antonio. The East Side was the center of Black life in that city — and Eva Armstrong Ballard was in the middle of it.

Eva's Business · The Nip and Sip Cafe

Eva ran the Nip and Sip Cafe on San Antonio's East Side. She was named proprietor of record in the 1954 San Antonio Light coverage. Her establishment was at 1501 Burnet Street, at Nolan and N. New Braunfels Avenue. She also had the property at 150 Paul Street — purchased April 22, 1944 for $3,600 and sold January 22, 1957 when she left Texas.

The 1954 Beer License · Eva Smith

A March 8, 1954 San Antonio Light article places Eva operating under the name "Eva Smith" for a beer license in Bexar County. Witness Siebrecht named Margie Ballard directly in connection with bootlegging activity at the time. This document expanded the known family geography and confirmed that Margie was in San Antonio alongside her mother in 1954.

James Ballard Jr. · Federal Tax Stamp · 207 N. Center St.

James Wilson Ballard Jr. served as Agent on a federal gambling tax stamp at 207 N. Center Street. Bellinger served as Principal. James Wilson Ballard Jr. and his brother were listed on these records. Under the IRS Revenue Act of 1951, these stamps were required taxes on gambling operations — not indictments but business records. They place the Ballard family at the center of Black economic life on San Antonio's East Side during the 1950s.

1957 Estate Affidavit · Eva's Five Children Sign

On January 16, 1957 — six days before Eva sold 150 Paul Street — all five of her surviving children signed a Bexar County estate affidavit (Instrument 86820), transferring the property to Eva. This document confirms Eva's children: James Jr., Margie Nell, Lois, Betty, and Charles Ray. It is one of the most important family documents in the collection.

San Antonio · East Side · Armstrong · Bellinger

The Bellinger Connection

Who Bellinger was to San Antonio, who Leroy Armstrong was to Eva, and how these two family threads cross in the photograph and the records.

Who Bellinger Was · San Antonio East Side

Bellinger served as the Principal on the federal gambling tax stamp registered at 207 N. Center Street in San Antonio. James Wilson Ballard Jr. and his brother served as Agents on the same stamp. Under the IRS Revenue Act of 1951, these stamps were required taxes — not criminal records — on operations that ran alongside the legitimate Black business community on the East Side.

The Bellinger name carries weight on San Antonio's East Side. This was a Black-owned business environment during the 1950s — an era when the East Side was the center of African American commercial and social life in that city. Eva's cafe, the tax stamps, the Caldwell case — they all exist within that same community ecosystem. Bellinger was part of that world, and so was Eva's family.

Leroy Armstrong Sr. · "Icecream"

Leroy Armstrong Sr. — known in family memory as "Icecream" — was connected to Eva Armstrong Ballard and the San Antonio chapter of this family. He appears in the 1954 San Antonio Light article that identified family members in Bexar County. The name "Icecream" is the way this person has been remembered across generations — a nickname that stuck in family oral history even as the formal records remained incomplete.

The photograph of Eva with James Wilson Ballard and Leroy — the one that carries an expiring URL — shows Leroy alongside the family in what appears to be a San Antonio-era image. This is one of the most important unresolved research threads: confirming Leroy Armstrong Sr.'s exact relationship to Eva and to the Armstrong line.

Research Status

The connection between Leroy Armstrong Sr. and the Armstrong line (John Armstrong's family) has not been confirmed by primary source documents. The hypothesis that Leroy is from the same Armstrong family that includes John Armstrong (Eva's father) remains active but unproven. DNA evidence would be the most direct path to confirmation.

Leroy Armstrong Jr. · Ronnie's Father · Hypothesis

Leroy Armstrong Jr. has been identified in research as the likely biological father of Ronnie Leroy Ballard — born November 11, 1953 in Bexar County, Texas. Ronnie carries the Ballard surname from his mother Margie but his biological father is believed to be Leroy Armstrong Jr., son of Leroy "Icecream" Armstrong Sr.

This remains a hypothesis. It has not been confirmed by DNA testing or a primary source document naming Leroy Jr. as Ronnie's father. What it does do is explain why a photograph of Eva, James Ballard, and Leroy exists — Leroy was not a stranger. He was connected to this family during the San Antonio years in a way that produced a child: Ronnie Leroy Ballard, named Leroy as a middle name.

Why This Matters

Ronnie holds the most memories of San Antonio in the living family. If Leroy Armstrong Jr. is his biological father, that connects Ronnie to the Armstrong line — the same line as Eva herself. It would mean Ronnie is connected to the family on both sides of his parentage. Uncle Ronnie is the person to speak with about this. He was there.

DNA Made Simple

How to Read a DNA Match

Most of what we proved about this family came from DNA. Here is what it actually means — explained for someone who has never thought about it before.

What is a DNA Test?

You spit in a tube, mail it to AncestryDNA, and they analyze your genetic material. They compare it to millions of other people who have tested. When you share a significant amount of DNA with someone, it means you and that person have a common ancestor. The more DNA you share, the more closely related you are.

What is a centiMorgan (cM)?

The number you see next to a DNA match — like "41 cM" — is called a centiMorgan. Think of it as a unit of genetic closeness. The higher the number, the more closely related you are.

3,400+ cMParent or child — you share about half your DNA
1,700 cMGrandparent or grandchild
850 cMAunt, uncle, or half-sibling
425 cMFirst cousin
106 cMSecond cousin
41 cMThird cousin range — like Jahna Bradshaw, who confirmed Lois Ballard's place in the tree

What is ThruLines?

ThruLines is Ancestry's tool that connects your DNA matches back to a specific ancestor you both share. When it says "46 ThruLines matches through Clarissa" — that means 46 living people have tested their DNA and they all trace back to the same ancestor: Clarissa, your 4th great-grandmother, born enslaved around 1807. That many independent matches pointing to the same person is about as close to proof as genealogy gets.

What Did DNA Actually Prove for This Family?

It confirmed Easter Bell's line goes back to Clarissa. 46 living people match through Joseph Nichols as the common ancestor. This connects to the 1839 Waldrop estate deed that names Clarissa as a daughter of Patience.

It found Lois Ballard. A match of 41 cM to Jahna Bradshaw led us to Jahna's grandmother — Lois Bradshaw, born Elois Maud Ballard — the last living child of James Wilson Ballard Sr. and Eva Armstrong Ballard. She is 93 years old and confirmed living in 2023.

It confirmed Hellen Nichols Armstrong and Helen Reese are the same person. A 53.5 cM match to the Helen Reese connection supported the documentary evidence and the profiles were merged in July 2026.
