March 13, 1929 — October 15, 1988 · Garden Grove, California

She was born on a Tuesday in the middle of the Great Depression, the daughter of a sick man and a woman who had survived everything. She carried one hundred and sixty years of this family's story without knowing she was carrying it. She held the line. She held it until you could hold it yourself.

What the records confirm

March 13, 1929 · Fort Worth, Texas

Margie Nell Ballard was born on March 13 1929 in Fort Worth, Texas — the third child of James Wilson Ballard Sr. and Eva Armstrong Ballard. The birth certificate places the family at 306½ East Annie Street, Fort Worth. File No. 26658.

She was born in the first months of the Great Depression. Her father had been born in Palestine, Anderson County, Texas in 1897 — the grandson of Newton Ballard, a formerly enslaved man who had claimed 160 acres in Upshur County Texas in 1856. Her mother Eva had been born in Bellville, Austin County, Texas in 1900 — the granddaughter of Easter Bell Nichols, who had lived through enslavement and emancipation and had died at age 97 in December 1935, when Margie was six years old.

Two lines of this family — the Ballard line from Smith County and Upshur County Texas and the Nichols-Armstrong line from Bellville Austin County Texas — came together in Margie Nell Ballard.

Where she came from

Two Lines Meeting in Fort Worth

Her father James Wilson Ballard Sr. was a sick man for much of Margie's childhood. He suffered from tuberculosis and the complications that came with it. By 1950 he was a patient at Austin State Hospital in Austin, Texas — confined there in the years when Margie was becoming an adult. He died on March 13 1953 — her twenty-fourth birthday. The date is its own kind of record.

Her mother Eva Armstrong Ballard was the survivor. Eva had grown up in the shadow of her own father's violent death — John Armstrong, shot by a white man in Fort Worth in 1911 when Eva was eleven years old and the killer walked free. Eva raised her children in Fort Worth and then in San Antonio. She outlived her husband by twenty-four years and eventually moved to California to be near her children. She died November 24 1977, the grandmother of fifteen.

The 1930 census shows the family at 1121 Pulaski Avenue in Fort Worth — Eva as head of household with her mother Helen Gilbert living with them and the children James Jr. age 3 and Margie age 1. James Wilson Ballard Sr. is present but Eva is listed as head — a detail that tells its own story about who was holding the family together.

"She was born on her father's twenty-fourth birthday. He died on her twenty-fourth birthday. Between those two dates was a family."

The family moved to San Antonio, Texas. In December 1938 Eva and James had another child — Thelma Louise Ballard — who died on March 16 1939 at three months old of malnutrition and dehydration. Texas death certificate No. 11120. She was buried at EastView Cemetery in San Antonio. James Ballard filed the death certificate from 107 Earl Street San Antonio. Margie was nine years old when her infant sister died.

Her own story

Norman Corbin · Santa Ana · A Man the Community Mourned

Margie Nell Ballard married Norman Caswell Corbin — born 1921 in Cleveland, Ohio. He was a non-denominational minister, a newspaper publisher, a school district liaison, an NAACP vice president, a city council candidate, and the man 1,500 people came to mourn when he died. He was fifty-three years old.

Norman and Margie lived in Santa Ana, California — in the Black community of southwest Santa Ana that Norman served for fourteen years. Their address at his death was 622 S. Townsend, Santa Ana. Before that they lived at 617 Shelley Street, also in Santa Ana.

Norman published the Orange County Star Review — a weekly newspaper. He served as vice chairman of the Orange County Human Relations Commission and as intercultural specialist for the Santa Ana Unified School District — a liaison between the school district and the community. He was vice president and board member of the Orange County Chapter of the NAACP. He spearheaded the formation of the Sunset Little League and the Senior Citizens Southwest Community Center. He ran for Santa Ana City Council Ward 2 in the April 1971 election as a Democrat.

In 1974 Norman traveled to Kentucky to visit a sick aunt. He became ill there and died on April 21 at Riverview Hospital. He carried no life insurance. His riches — as the newspaper noted — were not in the form of money.

The Orange County newspaper published this about him after he died: "People with problems came to him seven days a week at all hours of the day and night. He gave them the feeling that everything would be well, a feeling of security. He didn't stop until he'd done his best to solve their problems."

Fifteen hundred people came to his funeral.

"If he had a dollar in his pocket and somebody's welfare check was late, he gave away the dollar. If somebody was hungry, he fed them."
— Margie Nell Corbin · quoted in Orange County newspaper · 1974

Norman Corbin's Poem · Found in His Desk After His Death · "The Success Family"

The father of success is work.
The mother of success is ambition.
The oldest son is common sense.
Some of the other boys are perseverance, honesty, thoroughness and cooperation.

The oldest daughter is character.
Some of her sisters are cheerfulness, loyalty, courtesy, care, economy, sincerity and harmony.
The baby is opportunity.
Get acquainted with the old man
And you will be able to get along pretty well with all of the family.

Yesterday is gone and belongs to eternity.
Tomorrow may never come and is God's.

Margie was left with seven children ranging in age from 7 to 21. The community held a six-hour benefit concert — jazz and rock — at the City Hall Annex in Santa Ana to raise money for the family and establish a scholarship fund in Norman's name.

She was forty-five years old when he died. She would live fourteen more years — the same number of years Norman had lived in Orange County. She died October 15 1988 in Garden Grove, California. She was fifty-nine years old.

"She carried memory when the world forgot to ask. That is not a small thing. It is everything."

The inheritance she carried

One Hundred and Sixty Years

Margie Nell Ballard Corbin was born 164 years after Patience was given to Patty Waldrup in Newberry County South Carolina. She never knew that. She never knew the name Patience, or Clarissa, or Rendy Blayn, or Easter Bell Nichols — the woman who lived to 97 and died when Margie was six years old. She may have met Easter Bell once. She almost certainly did not know she was in the presence of someone who had been born enslaved and had lived into the twentieth century.

She carried it anyway. That is what this family does. The women in this line carry what they were never told. Patience carried her children through bondage. Clarissa carried the family from South Carolina to Mississippi. Easter Bell carried freedom into a community that built itself out of nothing on the land where it had been enslaved. Hellen Armstrong carried the Armstrong-Nichols line to Fort Worth. Eva carried it to California against grief and violence and loss. And Margie Nell carried it through the Great Depression and San Antonio and Garden Grove without ever knowing she was part of a chain that went back to the 1790s.

She died at fifty-nine. It was not enough time. But the line continued.
