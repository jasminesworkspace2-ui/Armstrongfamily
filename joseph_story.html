February 1845 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Born Into the Same Household
as the Woman He Would Marry

Joseph Nichols was born in February 1845 in Bellville, Austin County, Texas — on the Piney Creek plantation of John Nichols. He was born enslaved. His father's birthplace and mother's birthplace were both Texas according to the 1900 census — meaning his parents had been enslaved in Texas before him, born into the same system that would hold him for twenty years.

He had a brother. Henry Nicholas — born ca. 1839 Texas — six years older than Joseph — also enslaved on the Nichols plantation, also registered to vote in Austin County after emancipation, also building a life in the same county where he had been held. The two brothers never left. After freedom they stayed on the same ground and built their lives there.

In the same household where Joseph was born and held, Easter Bell was also enslaved — born July 4 1838, three years before Joseph, daughter of Rendy Blayn. Joseph and Easter grew up together in the same captivity. They did not know, as children, that they would one day be the reason a family line survived into the twenty-first century.

John Nichols — the man who held Joseph — had received a massive land grant from the Mexican government on April 25 1830. The town of Bellville had been built on land John Nichols sold to the Bell brothers. Joseph Nichols was born enslaved on the land that would bear the Bell name — and twenty years later he would marry the woman who took that name as her own.

1845 — 1865 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Twenty Years

Joseph Nichols was enslaved for twenty years. From birth to freedom. The 1860 federal slave schedule records him without his name — only as one of nine people in John Nichols' household, counted by age and gender and nothing else.

The 1860 agricultural schedule for John Nichols' farm confirms what Joseph's labor built: 65 acres of improved land, 735 unimproved, a farm valued at $4,000, producing 1,100 bushels of corn. Nine enslaved people working that operation. Joseph Nichols was one of them — approximately fifteen years old, working someone else's land, producing crops he would never own.

John Nichols died March 3, 1864. Joseph was nineteen years old. The household was dissolving. The war was ending. One year and three months later — on June 19, 1865 — General Order No. 3 was read in Galveston. Joseph Nichols was twenty years old. He was free.

Where Joseph came from — who his mother was — is a question the documents have not yet fully answered. The 1880 census records his mother's birthplace as Tennessee. Primary source court records found in 2026 confirm that John Nichols fled Williamson County, Tennessee in 1835 with enslaved people including a woman named Milley, her daughter Harriet, and an infant named Fanny — all from a 24-person household he held in Tennessee before coming to Texas. One of the women in that Tennessee group — likely one of three females born ca. 1807–1820 confirmed in the 1830 slave schedule — is the most probable candidate for Joseph's mother. Her name has not been found in any primary source document. The record of her existence survives only in the birthplace Joseph reported to a census enumerator in 1880.

Henry Nichols — who appears in the 1880 census living immediately next to Joseph and Easter in Precinct 1 Bellville — was almost certainly Joseph's brother from the same John Nichols household. Henry was born ca. 1839 in Texas, approximately four to eight years before Joseph. He married Emmeline Nichols, who was born ca. 1842 in Shreveport, Louisiana. Both Henry and Emmeline are buried at Jefferson Cemetery in Bellville — the same cemetery as Easter Bell Nichols. Henry Nichols farmed the same land Joseph farmed, in the same precinct, for decades after emancipation. They did not drift apart. They stayed together on the land they had been freed from.

December 18, 1865 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Six Months After Juneteenth

The Texas county marriage index records it plainly: Easter Bell and Joseph Nichols, married December 18, 1865, Austin County, Texas. Six months after Juneteenth. That was her name — Easter Bell — before she married. The Bell surname was hers, carried from the Bell household where she had been enslaved. She brought it to the courthouse and registered their marriage, and became Easter Bell Nichols. A later record dated December 23, 1869 records the marriage again — likely a church ceremony or civil renewal. Both dates are confirmed. The 1865 date is the first.

Joseph took the surname Nichols — the name of the family that had held him for twenty years. Many formerly enslaved people took their enslaver's surname at emancipation. It was the name they knew. It was the name the community knew them by. It was the name that would be on every document for the rest of their lives.

Easter Bell took the surname Bell — the name of the Bell family whose land surrounded the town. Two formerly enslaved people from the same household. Two different choices about what to call themselves in freedom. One family.

On December 23, 1869 — four and a half years after Juneteenth — Joseph Nichols married Easter Bell in Bellville, Austin County, Texas. Marriage certificate confirmed. FHL Film No. 1019274. Austin County Texas. December 23 1869.

He was twenty years old. She was twenty-seven. They had been born into the same captivity. They built their freedom together on the same ground.

January 27, 1868 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Line No. 1802

Three years after the marriage. Four years after Juneteenth. Joseph Nichols walked into the voter registration office in Bellville, Austin County, Texas and registered to vote.

He was twenty-three years old. He had lived in Texas for twenty-three years — his entire life. He had been enslaved for twenty of those years. He had been free for four.

He could not read. He could not write. Twenty years of enslavement had ensured that — the deliberate, systematic denial of literacy to keep people from knowing their rights, their history, their options.

He registered to vote anyway.

His brother Henry Nicholas registered to vote in the same county ten months later — November 17, 1869, Line No. 2127. Two brothers. Both born into slavery in Austin County Texas. Both registering to exercise the rights that had been denied to them their entire lives.

This is the earliest confirmed primary source document naming Joseph Nichols as a free person. Line No. 1802. January 27, 1868. Bellville, Austin County, Texas. The man who could not read and could not write — and voted anyway.

After 1865 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

He Owned the Land
Where He Was Born Enslaved

At some point after emancipation — the exact date not yet confirmed by primary source — Joseph Nichols and Easter Bell acquired 2 acres of the John Nichols Survey, Abstract 73, Austin County, Texas. Volume 209, Page 228, Austin County deed records.

The John Nichols Survey was the same land grant that John Nichols had received from the Mexican government in 1830. The same land where Joseph had been born enslaved in 1845. The same ground where Easter had been born enslaved in 1838. After emancipation they put their names on a deed for 2 acres of that same survey.

By 1900 the census confirms they owned their home free of mortgage. Thirty-five years after Juneteenth — a man who could not read and could not write owned land outright, free and clear, in the county where he had been born a slave.

It would confirm exactly who Joseph's parents were and resolve the final generational link between Joseph and Clarissa's line. This is the highest priority pending call in the entire archive. Joseph Nichols died in 1905 — confirmed by Jeff Cemetery records in Bellville. He was approximately sixty years old. He had been free for forty years. He had voted. He had married. He had built a farm and owned it outright. He had raised nine children on land his labor had built before he was free to own it. His death certificate has not yet been located.

He Could Not Read.
He Could Not Write.
He Voted Anyway.
He Owned the Land.

Twenty years enslaved. Forty years free. He registered to vote four years after emancipation — Line No. 1802 — and walked out of that office a citizen. He farmed the same land where he had been born a slave. He married the woman who had been enslaved in the same household. He raised nine children free. He owned his home outright.

The archives do not record what he thought about any of this. They record only the facts: the registration number, the census entries, the marriage date, the agricultural schedule, the death year. A man who could not write left no letters. A man who could not read left no journals. What he left was a family — and a line that runs unbroken to the person reading this page.

Line No. 1802. January 27 1868. Bellville, Austin County, Texas. That is the earliest document that names him as a free man. It is enough.

Research by Jasmine M. Lee · Dock Ellis Foundation · Seven years · 2019–2026

Where Joseph Fits · Easter Bell's Husband

Joseph Nichols was born enslaved in Bellville, Texas ca. 1843. He married Easter Bell Nichols on December 23 1869 — four years after emancipation. Together they had nine children. Their daughter Hellen Nichols Armstrong is the direct ancestor of Eva, Margie, Hjordis, and Jasmine. Joseph registered to vote on January 27 1868 in Bellville — three years after emancipation — the first confirmed civic act in this family's documented history as free people.
