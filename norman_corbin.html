A Man for All People

Norman Caswell Corbin was born in 1921 in Cleveland, Ohio. He came to Orange County, California sometime in the late 1950s or early 1960s — fourteen years before his death. He settled in the Black community of southwest Santa Ana, and he never left. He built his life there. The community built itself around him.

He married Margie Nell Ballard — the granddaughter of Easter Bell Nichols, the great-great-granddaughter of Patience. They had seven children and lived first at 617 Shelley Street and later at 622 South Townsend Street, Santa Ana. Their home was open seven days a week at all hours of the day and night to anyone who needed help.

Norman was a non-denominational minister, a newspaper publisher, a school district liaison, an NAACP vice president, and a city council candidate. He spearheaded the formation of the Sunset Little League and the Senior Citizens Southwest Community Center. He served on the Santa Ana College Community Relations Advisory Council. He ran for Santa Ana City Council Ward 2 in the April 1971 election as a Democrat.

He published the Orange County Star Review — a weekly newspaper serving the Black community of Orange County. He served as intercultural specialist for the Santa Ana Unified School District — a liaison between the school district and the community it was failing to serve. He served as vice chairman of the Orange County Human Relations Commission and was photographed handing over the keys to the first subsidized housing unit to a family named Hernandez — the Human Relations Commission making good on its name.

He carried no life insurance. His riches were not in the form of money. When someone's welfare check was late he gave away his last dollar. When someone was hungry he fed them. When people came to him with problems at two in the morning he answered the door.

"People with problems came to him seven days a week at all hours of the day and night. He gave them the feeling that everything would be well, a feeling of security. He didn't stop until he'd done his best to solve their problems."

— Margie Nell Corbin · quoted in Orange County newspaper · 1974

On scouting — which had historically excluded Black families from southwest Santa Ana — Norman said plainly: "I never got into the scouts, never put my sons in it. It was an organization that never seemed real to me. Scouting never had a policy of excluding Blacks. But at the same time, they never exactly welcomed us with open arms. Now, all of a sudden, they're really out to win and woo us. But it can't be done overnight."

He was photographed by an Orange County newspaper handing the keys of the first subsidized housing unit to Jose Hernandez and his family — the Santa Ana Housing Authority and the Human Relations Commission working together to house a family. Norman Corbin is in the photograph, on the left, handing over the keys.

The Poem in His Desk

After Norman Corbin died on April 21 1974, the people who cleaned out his desk at the Santa Ana Unified School District found a poem he had written. The newspaper published it in his obituary. It is the only piece of writing confirmed to be in his own words. It is titled The Success Family.

Norman Caswell Corbin · Written before April 21 1974 · Found in his desk after his death The Success Family
The father of success is work.
The mother of success is ambition.
The oldest son is common sense.
Some of the other boys are perseverance,
honesty, thoroughness and cooperation.

The oldest daughter is character.
Some of her sisters are cheerfulness,
loyalty, courtesy, care,
economy, sincerity and harmony.
The baby is opportunity.

Get acquainted with the old man
and you will be able to get along pretty well
with all of the family.

Yesterday is gone and belongs to eternity.
Tomorrow may never come and is God's.
Published in Orange County California newspaper obituary · April 1974

The journalist who covered his death — Richard Buffum — wrote: "I'm sorry I didn't know him. Good men like him are hard to come by anywhere. I just thought you'd like to know that Norman Corbin was here."

April 21 1974 · Santa Ana California

How He Died and What Happened After

In April 1974 Norman Caswell Corbin traveled to Kentucky to visit a sick aunt. He became ill while he was there. He was brought to Riverview Hospital in Santa Ana and died on April 21 1974 of pneumonia. He was fifty-three years old.

He carried no life insurance. He had spent fourteen years giving away what he had. Margie was left with seven children — the youngest was eight years old, the oldest was twenty-one.

The community responded. A six-hour benefit jazz and rock concert — billed as a Conglomeration of Stars — was held at the City Hall Annex on Santa Ana Boulevard at Ross Street from 1 to 7 p.m. Tickets were $2 at the door. Every cent went to Margie and the children and to establish a scholarship fund in Norman's name. The performers included Art Robinson and Soul Providers, Johnny Adams, Hank Alexander, North Wind, Black Gold, Just Funk, Bertha Eason, Billy Higgins, and others.

1,500 people came to his funeral. Visitation was held at Winbigler Family Mortuary on North Grand Avenue. The funeral service was at Jerome Park at 2100 West McFadden. Interment followed at Fairhaven Memorial Park.

He was survived by his wife Margie, his mother Mattie Lou Simpson of Santa Ana, his sister Helen R. Bishop of Kentucky, his sister Mary F. Clark of Detroit, and his seven children.

His Seven Children

Norman and Margie had seven children. The obituary named them: Michael, Ronnie, Hordis, Deanne, Sonsorey, Orngiel, and Se Taun. At the time of Norman's death in April 1974 they ranged in age from 8 to 21.
