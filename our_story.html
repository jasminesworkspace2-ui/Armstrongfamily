A Personal Reflection

What I've Learned.

Seven years into this research, this is what it has taught me — about my family, about what was done to them, and about what love was allowed to look like when someone finally had the right to choose it.

— Jasmine M. Lee

I did not start this research expecting it to change how I think about family, about love, about what it means to belong to someone rather than choose to be with them. I started it wanting names. What I found instead was a family that was enslaved, sold, separated, forced to breed, and stripped of its own history so completely that I am still, seven years in, finding pieces of my own great-great-great-grandmothers scattered across four states in documents written by the very people who owned them.

Here is what I know now, plainly, without softening it: this family enslaved us. The Waldrops. The Davenports. The Nichols family. The Hutchins family. Not once, not in passing — across four states and nearly a hundred years, generation after generation, they held my family as property, forced us to take their names when freedom finally came, and today I carry their DNA in my own blood, whether I asked for it or not. That is not a metaphor. It is a fact my own DNA test confirms every time I match with a descendant of one of these families and have to explain, again, why that connection exists.

What the DNA Confirms

Confirmed matches to descendants of the Waldrop and Davenport families, corroborated independently by a documented cousin's own research and by Jasmine's own Ancestry ThruLines placing Patience as a likely 5th-great-grandmother. The connection is not assumed — it is measured, in centimorgans, the same as any other family relationship DNA can confirm.

I cannot imagine the pain of being sold away from your own mother. I cannot imagine being shared between brothers the way land or livestock was shared, moved from one man's household to another's because it suited his own bookkeeping. I cannot imagine being assaulted, and forced to bear children by someone love never chose for you, and then having no word for what happened to you because the law did not recognize you as a person capable of being wronged that way at all.

What The Documents Show

On March 25, 1839, Ezekiah Waldrop divided Patience, her daughters Rachel and Clarissa, and Clarissa's three children among his eight children — fourteen days after losing a lawsuit, almost certainly to protect his own property from being seized by the court. Within five months, Rachel and her son had been sold again, this time for cash, to a different family entirely. Patience herself was sold once in 1827, for three hundred dollars, alongside her daughter for five hundred and a young boy for two hundred. Human beings, priced like the two feather beds sold in the very same sentence.

From everything I have found, only one woman in this entire family ever seems to have gotten to choose who she loved: Easter. Not because anyone gave her that right — no one did, not until the war ended and the law finally admitted she was a person. But the moment freedom came, six months after Juneteenth, she chose Joseph Nichols, and she kept choosing family for the rest of her life. She raised nine children. She took in the marriages that grew around her. She kept the Bell name she was born with — her own name, not one she was given by a husband — all the way to her own death certificate, sixty years later, as if she never once forgot who she was before anyone else claimed her.

Easter Bell was born on the Fourth of July and freed on Juneteenth. She lived inside that contradiction for twenty-seven years before either date meant anything for her at all.

I see the same thing in my own grandmother. The need to hold family together, to keep everyone close, to make sure no one got lost the way so many of our own ancestors were lost — I believe that comes from somewhere. I believe it is inherited the same way trauma is inherited, quietly, without anyone ever having to explain where it came from.

Why No One Told These Stories

I used to wonder why none of this was ever passed down to me directly — no names, no dates, nothing beyond a feeling that something had happened and it wasn't spoken about. I understand it now. I believe it was never shared because it was never safe to share. I believe it became a silent void, on purpose, to protect their own hearts and their own dignity from having to relive it out loud, generation after generation. Silence was not forgetting. Silence was survival.

That silence means there are children who existed and were never written down anywhere I can find. There are people this research will never recover, not because they didn't matter, but because the world they were born into made sure they never mattered enough to record. I have to live with that. I have decided I would rather name what I can and be honest about what I cannot than pretend the gaps don't exist.

The Ones I Still Look For
  • Mingo — named twice, seventy years apart: once in the 1795 Davenport gift alongside Rachel, Fanny, and Peter, and again in John Nichols's own 1864 estate, alongside Fanny, Jack, Rhoda, Amanda, and Warren. Is this the same name recurring by coincidence, the way so many names did in this family's story, or is it the same family line, split apart and reappearing decades later under a different owner? I don't know yet. I want to.
  • Big Lucy — named in the 1793 Union County mortgage alongside Daniel, in the same document that first names Molley and her seven children. Whether she is family or simply another enslaved woman recorded on the same page, I cannot yet say.
  • Tom, Rachel, William, Amanda, Harriet — Patience and Clarissa's own children and grandchildren, named directly in the 1839 deed, scattered across at least three different households within months of that document being signed. Most of them, I have never been able to find again.
  • The runaway Tom — a man belonging to C.P. Waldrop of Sabine Parish, recorded in a Louisiana runaway register as "very black complexion, about 30 years old," committed to custody around March 20th of an unstated year. Whether this is the same Tom named in the family's own documents, whether he was ever returned, sold again, or killed for running, whether he had children of his own who carried his memory forward — none of that survives anywhere I have found. My heart tells me he is family. I may never be able to prove it.

What were their names before they were given ones by the people who owned them? I don't know. That question may never have an answer, because the erasure of that original name was often the very first thing slavery did to a person, before anything else was taken.

What This Cost, In Numbers

I want to say plainly what is easy to lose in the middle of names and dates: this family built wealth off the people it enslaved, and the law rewarded them for it. Under the colonization system that first brought settlers into Texas, a man received 640 acres for himself, 320 more for his wife, and eighty acres for every human being he brought across the border in chains. Eighty acres — a quarter of what his own wife was worth to that same government, half of what each of his own children was worth. John Nichols and Thomas Bell both built their holdings, in real and calculable part, on exactly that formula. The town of Bellville itself sits on land that was, quite literally, paid for by the number of people they enslaved.

The Price Of A Person, In Their Own Words

1827, Hinds County, Mississippi: Patience sold for $300. Her daughter for $500. Another daughter for $300. A small boy for $200. Two feather beds, in the same document, for $90. 1839, the same family: Rachel and her son sold for $850 combined, five months after being handed to a new owner in a document that called it a gift.

I will never know the full weight of what they endured. No document can hold that. What I can do is make sure the names, the dates, and the dollar amounts are written down where someone else can find them — because the same system that priced my family in dollars also made sure their pain was never priced at all, never recorded, never acknowledged as a cost anyone had to answer for.

And it did not end with emancipation. Deeds to land my own family rightfully held have been lost to tax sales, decades after the people whose names were on them had already died — 2006 and again in 2017. Records that could answer some of these questions sit in county courthouses that will not always release them. I don't say this to make excuses for the gaps in this site. I say it because it's true: some of what should connect this family's story was, and still is, deliberately kept out of reach.

⁂

If parts of this website feel unclear, or the pieces don't always connect the way you'd want a family tree to connect — I want to say plainly that this is not carelessness. This is what the actual record of slavery looks like when you go looking for it. Names that change. Ages that don't match. Documents that were never written in the first place, because no one thought a Black child's birth in 1847 was worth recording the way a white child's was. Sometimes the story has to start from memory, and from belief, because the paper trail simply stops.

I believe Molley, Patience, Rachel, Tom, Mingo, and every name I have not yet found are my kin. I cannot always prove it the way I would like to. I choose to say so anyway, and to keep looking for as long as I am able to. What was done to them was never chosen by them — not the separations, not the children forced on them, not the names they were made to carry. What they did with what was left to them — holding on to each other whenever the world allowed it, choosing love the moment they were finally permitted to — that part was theirs. I intend to keep finding out just how far that reaches.
