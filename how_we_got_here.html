Before the Records Begin
West Africa · ca. 1750–1770

Before Patience — Molley

The oldest ancestor this family can reach by name is a woman named Molley. She was found in multiple deed books. The 1793 James Sims mortgage in Union County, South Carolina names her alongside her seven children: Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, Tom, and Patience. And the index to the deed books of Laurens and Newberry Counties — two neighboring counties — confirms her name at page 118, Tom as her child at page 118, and Patience as her child at page 111. These are separate deed transactions beyond the mortgage. She was not a footnote in one document. She was known and recorded across multiple counties, multiple deed books, multiple years. The paper remembered her name.

But DNA tells us more. The family's DNA results point to the Yoruba and Dahomey regions of West Africa — what is now Nigeria and Benin — as the most likely origin. A ship called the Rainbow arrived in Charleston, South Carolina in 1758 carrying 201 people from that exact region. The investor on that voyage was a man named Hugh Ball. Whether Molley or her mother was on that ship, we cannot prove. But the geography matches. The timeline matches. The DNA matches.

Molley was probably born in West Africa or was the child of someone who was. She crossed the Atlantic — or her mother did — and ended up in the hands of a farmer in Union County, South Carolina. That is where her daughter Patience was born.

What the DNA says

The family's AncestryDNA results show significant Yoruba and Dahomey ancestry — consistent with the Bight of Benin region of West Africa. The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database confirms the Rainbow voyage of 1758 brought 201 people from that region to Charleston, South Carolina — where enslaved people were then sold to buyers across the state including Upcountry farmers like the Sims family who held Molley.

Chapter Two · 1795
Newberry County, South Carolina · ca. 1765

Patience

On August 4, 1795, a man named Francis Davenport gave his daughter Patty Waldrop an enslaved girl named Patience. He recorded this transaction in the Newberry County deed book. That document still exists. It is the reason this family can be traced at all.

Patience was Molley's daughter. She was in the Davenport family's household in Newberry County, South Carolina. On January 1, 1795 — seven months before the deed of gift — Wadsworth and Turpin, merchants, sold Patience in open market alongside her mother Moll, a girl named Esther age 7, and four others, to satisfy Francis Davenport's debt of £87. Davenport reacquired her, settled the debt, and on August 4, 1795 gave Patience to his daughter Patty Waldrop. That is the foundational document of this entire archive. The deed describes her as "a certain negro girl named Patience."

Patty Waldrop — the woman who received Patience — married a man named E.P. Waldrop. Over the following decades, Patience had children of her own. Among them: a daughter named Clarissa, and a son named Tom. The Waldrop family kept Patience and her children together as they moved west — from South Carolina to Mississippi.

The Document · Newberry County SC · Deed Book C: 367–368 · August 4, 1795

"Francis Davenport Sr. to Patty Waldrop, his beloved daughter — one enslaved girl named Patience — August 4, 1795 — certified Charles Griffin J.P. — recorded August 18, 1795."

This is the foundational document of the entire archive. Without it, this family's history before 1865 would be unrecoverable.

Chapter Three · 1807–1839
South Carolina → Mississippi · born ca. 1807

Clarissa

Clarissa was Patience's daughter — born around 1807 in South Carolina. She grew up in the Waldrop household, enslaved alongside her mother. When the Waldrop family moved west to Mississippi in the 1820s and 1830s, Clarissa went with them. She had no choice.

In 1839 a deed was signed in Rankin County, Mississippi that named Patience and her family for the last time. It transferred them — as property — from E.P. Waldrop to Patty Waldrop's eight children. One of those eight children was a woman named Fanny Waldrop Nichols. Fanny had already married a man named John Nichols and moved to Texas two years earlier. Clarissa's descendants came to Texas as Fanny's share of that transfer.

The 1839 deed also names Clarissa's own children: Amanda and Harriet. These are new names confirmed in 2026 — Clarissa's daughters, Patience's granddaughters, and the aunts of the generation that would reach Texas.

Important · This Is Not A Freedom Document

The 1839 deed has sometimes been misread as freeing Patience's family. It does not. The phrase "free from the claim of myself" means E.P. Waldrop released his own claim — not that Patience and her family were liberated. They were transferred as property to Patty's children. They remained enslaved.

Chapter Four · ca. 1817
Bellville, Austin County, Texas · born ca. 1817

Rendy Blayn

Rendy Blayn was Easter Bell's mother. She was held in the household of John Nichols in Bellville, Texas — confirmed by the 1860 slave schedule which lists a Black female age 43 in his household. Her name is confirmed by Easter Bell's 1935 death certificate, which lists the mother's name as Rendy Blayn.

The surname Blayn traces to the white family that enslaved her. A Blain family held a land grant in Fayette County — the county right next to Bellville — arriving in Texas between 1837 and 1840. That timeline is consistent with Rendy being in Texas when Easter Bell was born on July 4, 1838.

Rendy was born in North Carolina — confirmed by the 1860 slave schedule. Before she was in the John Nichols household, the evidence points to the Bell household of Bellville. The 1850 Austin County slave schedule for Thomas Bell records a Black woman age 33 — born ca. 1817 — the exact age and birth year that matches Rendy. Thomas Bell was one of the original colonists of Austin County and his family donated the land that became Bellville. When he died in 1858 his adopted son John Gideon Bell inherited the household. At some point before 1860, Rendy and her daughter Easter were transferred to the John Nichols household — the two families were neighbors on the same land grant for decades.

Chapter Five · The Heart of This Archive
Bellville, Texas · July 4, 1838 – December 17, 1935

Easter Bell Nichols

Easter Bell Nichols was born enslaved on July 4, 1838, in Bellville, Texas. She died free on December 17, 1935. She was ninety-seven years old.

In between those two dates she lived through slavery, the Civil War, Juneteenth, Reconstruction, the rise of Jim Crow, World War I, and the Great Depression. She buried children. She outlived two husbands. She watched her granddaughter Eva leave Bellville and build a new life in California. And through all of it she stayed in Bellville — on or near the same land she was born enslaved on.

After emancipation Easter Bell married Joseph Nichols — first confirmed on December 18, 1865, six months after Juneteenth. A later record shows December 23, 1869. The 1865 date is the primary source confirmed marriage. Her name before marriage was Easter Bell — the Bell surname came from the Bell household that had held her. She carried that name to the courthouse when she registered as a free married woman. Joseph had been born enslaved in the same household. He registered to vote in January 1868 — just three years after freedom — even though he could not read or write. Together they had nine children. Their daughter Hellen would become the grandmother of Eva Ballard. That is how Easter Bell connects to every Corbin, Ballard, and Armstrong in this family today.

When John Nichols died in 1864, the land he had held in Bellville descended — in fractional shares — to the people he had enslaved. Easter Bell Nichols held legal title to a piece of that land for 140 years after emancipation. In 2006, Austin County foreclosed on it for unpaid taxes. Her name — Easter Pink — was still on the deed.

Chapter Six · 1870–1931
Bellville, Texas · September 1870 – December 5, 1931

Hellen Nichols Armstrong

Hellen was Easter Bell's daughter — born in Bellville in September 1870, five years after emancipation. She grew up free but in a world that had not finished deciding what freedom meant for Black families in Texas.

She married John Armstrong. They had a daughter named Eva. Then in August 1911 John Armstrong was murdered. Hellen remarried — a man named Albert Reese — and lived out her years in Bellville. She died on December 5, 1931, and was buried at Jeff Cemetery in Bellville alongside her mother Easter Bell.

Three independent primary source documents confirm Hellen's identity — her death certificate, a Find a Grave memorial, and a 2006 court filing that names her as "Helen Armstrong (a.k.a. Helen Gilbert)" as a legal heir to the John Nichols Survey land. She went by multiple names across multiple marriages. The records are consistent. She is one person.

Chapter Seven · ca. 1899–1977
Bellville, Texas → Garden Grove, California

Eva Armstrong Ballard

Eva was Hellen's daughter — born around 1899 in Bellville. She married James Wilson Ballard Sr. — and with that marriage the Armstrong line and the Ballard line came together. This is where the name Ballard enters the family you know today.

Eva eventually moved to California — part of the Great Migration of Black families from the South to the West. She died in Garden Grove, California in 1977. Her daughter Margie grew up carrying both the Armstrong heritage from Bellville and the Ballard name.

Chapter Eight · 1929–1988
Texas · born 1929

Margie Nell Ballard Corbin

Margie was Eva's daughter — born in 1929. She married Norman Corbin — and with that marriage the Corbin name enters the family. She is the grandmother of Hjordis and the great-grandmother of Jasmine. If your family carries the Corbin name, this is where it comes from in this line.

Margie was four generations removed from Easter Bell. Nine generations removed from Patience in South Carolina. She may not have known any of these names. Seven years of research found them for her.

The 8th Generation

Hjordis L. Corbin

Hjordis is Margie's daughter — the 8th generation in the confirmed maternal line. Her AncestryDNA kit (SQ8499767) has been used throughout this research to confirm DNA connections to other descendants of this family across multiple states and generations.
