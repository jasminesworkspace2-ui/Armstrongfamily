Armstrong Family Archive · What They Did With Freedom

Freedom
Stories

Confirmed by Primary Source · Bellville, Texas · 1856 – 1935

These are not stories about surviving enslavement. They are stories about what this family did the moment they were free — and every moment after that. Every fact here is confirmed by a primary source document.

I August 26 1856 · Upshur County, Texas

Newton Ballard Files for Land

Newton Ballard · born ca. 1830 Mississippi · enslaved by Lewis F. Ballard Sr. One year before his enslaver died.

Newton Ballard was born approximately 1830 in Mississippi — almost certainly in Copiah County, where Lewis F. Ballard Sr. held land and lived before migrating to Texas. By 1850 Newton was enslaved in Smith County, Texas, listed without a name in a federal slave schedule — described only as a male, age approximately 20, born Mississippi. One dollar value was placed on every human being on that page. Newton was no different.

On August 26 1856 — while still living in the shadow of the Ballard plantation — Newton Ballard filed a land claim for 160 acres in Upshur County, Texas. Texas General Land Office File No. 3378. Survey No. 124. Abstract 131.

Lewis F. Ballard Sr. — the man who had enslaved Newton since childhood — died on February 18 1857. Newton had filed his land claim six months before his enslaver was in the ground.

We do not know how Newton acquired the legal standing to file that claim while still enslaved. We do not know who helped him. We do not know whether Lewis F. Ballard Sr. knew. What we know is that Newton Ballard looked at 160 acres of East Texas land and put his name on it while the man who called him property was still alive.

His son — George Washington Ballard — was born September 4 1861. He became a minister. He owned property on East Johnson Street in Waco. He named his son James Wilson Ballard. James Wilson Ballard married Eva Armstrong. Eva Armstrong was the granddaughter of Easter Bell Nichols. Easter Bell Nichols was born enslaved on July 4 1838 and died free on December 17 1935.

All of that began with a land claim. August 26 1856. A man who was not yet free — reaching for land.

"He filed for land while the man who enslaved him was still alive. Six months later that man was dead. The land claim remained."

II January 27 1868 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Joseph Nichols Registers to Vote

Joseph Nichols · born February 1845 · Bellville, Texas · enslaved by John Nichols Two and a half years after Juneteenth.

Joseph Nichols was born February 1845 on the John Nichols plantation in Bellville, Austin County, Texas — the same league of land that John Nichols had received from the Spanish government in 1832, the same land that would become the commercial center of the town of Bellville, the land where the electric light plant and the water plant and the cotton gin would all be built in the years after the Civil War.

Joseph could not read. Joseph could not write. He signed every document — if he signed any at all — with an X mark or someone else's hand. He had been born into a household where the man who enslaved him, John Nichols, also could not read or write. Illiteracy was not a mark of intelligence in this world. It was a mark of what had been withheld.

On January 27 1868 — two and a half years after the Juneteenth announcement that had legally freed him — Joseph Nichols walked into the Bellville courthouse and registered to vote.

His name appears on Line 1802 of the Texas Voter Records for 1867-1918. Birthplace: Texas. He could not read. He could not write. He registered anyway.

The man who had enslaved him — John Nichols — had died in 1864. His widow Fanny Waldrup Nichols was living in Austin, Texas by 1876, described in sworn court testimony as being in indigent circumstances. The enslaver's widow was poor. The formerly enslaved man was a registered voter standing on his own two acres of the same land he had been born on.

"He could not read. He could not write. He walked into the Bellville courthouse on January 27 1868 and registered to vote anyway."

III ca. 1866-1870 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Green Nichols Holds Thirty Acres

Green Nichols · formerly enslaved by John Nichols · married Eliza Nichols December 16 1869 On the same survey where he had been enslaved.

When the Austin County tax assessor came through Bellville to record property holdings in the years after the Civil War, he found four formerly enslaved men — all carrying the Nichols surname — all holding land on Abstract 73, the John Nichols Survey. The same land. The same survey. The land John Nichols had received from the Spanish government in 1832 and held until his death in 1864.

Joseph Nichols held 2 acres — valued at $120 — with one horse and five cattle.

Alfred Nichols held a fractional 2/10 of an acre.

Thomas Nichols held a fractional 1/10 of an acre.

And Green Nichols held 30 acres — valued at $150 — with one horse and ten cattle. He was the largest Black landowner among the four.

Thirty acres. On Abstract 73. The John Nichols Survey. The land where Green Nichols had been enslaved.

On December 16 1869 — one week before Joseph Nichols married Easter Bell — Green Nichols married a woman named Eliza Nichols in the Bellville courthouse. Marriage License No. 7. Both identified as freedpeople. The same minister officiated — Rev. J. Shirl — who would marry Joseph and Easter Bell exactly one week later on December 23 1869.

The entire Nichols freedmen community was legalizing their families in the same week of December 1869. They had been building these lives since 1865. Now they were putting their names on paper.

"Thirty acres. On Abstract 73. The John Nichols Survey. The land where Green Nichols had been enslaved. He held it. He farmed it. He built on it."

IV July 4 1838 – December 17 1935 · Bellville, Texas

Easter Bell Nichols Lives Ninety-Seven Years

Easter Bell Nichols · born enslaved · daughter of Rendy Blayn Born July 4th. Died free. Ninety-seven years.

Easter Bell Nichols was born on July 4 1838 — Independence Day — in Bellville, Austin County, Texas. She was born enslaved in the household of John Nichols, who had received his one-league Spanish land grant in 1832 and built his plantation on the land that would become the town of Bellville. Her mother was a woman named Rendy Blayn — sold from Virginia to Texas, her origins traced to the Blane family of Halifax County, Virginia. Her father is not documented in any record found to date.

Easter Bell was enslaved for twenty-seven years. She was freed in 1865 by the Juneteenth announcement — she was twenty-six years old. She had already had children. She had already built a life inside the only world she had ever known.

In December 1869 she married Joseph Nichols in the Bellville courthouse. She could not read. She could not write. The marriage license identifies them both as freedpeople. They had been living together for years — their son Allen was born February 1 1867, before the legal marriage. Their daughter Annie was born ca. 1866. They legalized what already existed.

Easter Bell outlived Joseph — who died in 1905. She outlived her daughter Hellen. She outlived her son Allen, who died in 1944. She was still living in Bellville in the 1920s. She was still living in 1930 — confirmed by the census — age given as 90, though she was actually 91.

On December 17 1935 Easter Bell Nichols died in Bellville, Austin County, Texas. She was ninety-seven years old. The death certificate was filed by her daughter Clara Nazy. The informant confirmed her mother's maiden name — Blayn — connecting Rendy back to her one last time through the only surviving record that names her.

She had been born in the same town where she died. She had been born enslaved on land that became a city. She had married on that land, raised children on that land, buried a husband on that land, watched her grandchildren leave for Fort Worth and San Antonio and California. She had outlived the man who enslaved her by seventy-one years.

Ninety-seven years. Born July 4th. Died free.

"She outlived the man who had enslaved her by seventy-one years. She outlived his widow. She outlived the world that had called her property. She was ninety-seven years old."

V June 1870 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Amanda Brown Arrives in Texas

Amanda Brown · born ca. 1830 Georgia · enslaved on the Samuel Brown estate · Wilcox County, Georgia She left Georgia and walked toward something better.

Amanda Brown was born approximately 1830 in Georgia — almost certainly on or near the estate of Samuel Brown in Wilcox County, Georgia. Samuel Brown held seven enslaved people in his 1860 slave schedule — a female age 33 among them. That profile matches Amanda exactly. He died in May 1863. The guardian's bond for his minor children was filed November 7 1863 — $15,000 to secure the estate. The Civil War was still being fought.

Somewhere between 1863 and 1870 Amanda Brown left Wilcox County, Georgia. We do not know how she traveled. We do not know how long it took. We do not know whether she traveled alone or with others. We know that formerly enslaved people crossed the South on foot, by wagon, by whatever means they could find — looking for family, looking for work, looking for land, looking for something that felt like freedom.

By June 1870 Amanda Brown was in Bellville, Austin County, Texas. The census enumerator recorded her as Manda Reese — living in the household of Mark Reese, age 70, born Virginia — a formerly enslaved farmer who had built a life in the same Bellville community as the Nichols family. Manda was age 40. Born Georgia.

The digital genealogy indexes misread her name as Rhoda. The original handwritten microfilm — examined directly — shows a clear capital M. Her name was Manda. Her name was Amanda.

In the same household was Enoch Reese — age 18 — born Alabama. And a child named Patience Reese — age 9 — born Texas. Someone in the Reese household had named a child Patience — the same name as your oldest confirmed ancestor, born ca. 1765 in South Carolina. The name was being carried forward in this community, passed through generations that did not know they were preserving your family's history.

Sometime between 1870 and 1880 Amanda Brown married Enoch Reese. By the 1880 census she appears as Mandy Brown Reese — wife of Enoch Reese Sr. — with children William, Martha, and John. She had built a family. She had built a home. She had crossed the South alone and arrived somewhere that could hold her.

"A child in that household was named Patience — the same name as your oldest confirmed ancestor, born ca. 1765 in South Carolina. Someone was carrying that name forward."

VI 1955 · San Antonio, Texas

Eva Armstrong Ballard Testifies

Eva Armstrong Ballard · born June 27 1900 · Bellville, Texas · granddaughter of Easter Bell Nichols A Black woman. 1955. San Antonio. She testified.

Eva Armstrong Ballard was born June 27 1900 in Bellville, Austin County, Texas — the granddaughter of Easter Bell Nichols, the great-great-granddaughter of Patience. She was eleven years old when her father John Armstrong was shot and killed by a white man in Fort Worth who walked free. She grew up in the knowledge of what the law did and did not protect.

She married James Wilson Ballard Sr. She raised six children in Fort Worth and San Antonio. She lost an infant daughter — Thelma Louise — in March 1939. She watched her husband deteriorate from tuberculosis and eventually lose his mind to syphilitic meningoencephalitis. She held the family together through all of it.

In 1955 — the year Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat on a Montgomery bus, the year Emmett Till was murdered in Mississippi, the year the Civil Rights Movement was making itself known — Eva Armstrong Ballard was subpoenaed in a San Antonio police brutality case. She appeared. She testified.

We do not have the transcript of what she said. We have the newspaper record confirming she was subpoenaed, that she appeared, that she testified. A Black woman in 1955 San Antonio — whose father had been killed by a white man who faced no consequences — standing in a courtroom and telling the truth about what the police had done.

She was fifty-four years old. She had been watching what the law did to Black people in Texas for her entire life. She testified anyway.

"Her father had been killed by a white man who faced no consequences. She knew what the law did and did not protect. In 1955 she testified anyway."

VII 1861 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Someone Named Their Child Patience

Patience Reese · age 9 · born Texas · 1870 census · Household 114 · Beat No. 1 · Bellville The name that would not be forgotten.

In the 1870 census for Beat No. 1, Bellville, Austin County, Texas — the same census that confirmed Joseph Nichols and Easter Bell in their household on June 30 1870 — a nine-year-old girl named Patience Reese appears in Household 114. Born Texas, ca. 1861. Living with Mark Reese, age 70, born Virginia, and the rest of the Reese family including eighteen-year-old Enoch Reese.

Patience is your oldest confirmed ancestor. She was born approximately 1765 in South Carolina. She was given to Patty Waldrup on August 4 1795 per Newberry County South Carolina Deed Book C, page 367. She was carried from South Carolina to Mississippi to Louisiana to Texas — through the hands of the Waldrop family — and her descendants ended up on the John Nichols plantation in Bellville. That is the documented chain.

The child Patience Reese born ca. 1861 in Texas is not that Patience. She is a child of the Bellville Black community, born four years before emancipation, named by someone who knew the name. Someone in that community — perhaps Mark Reese, perhaps Enoch's mother, perhaps Amanda Brown who would later join this household — knew the name Patience and gave it to a child.

We cannot know whether they knew they were honoring your ancestor. We cannot know whether the name traveled through oral tradition, through community memory, through some chain of telling that connected the enslaved people of the Waldrop household to the enslaved people of the Reese household across the generations between 1795 and 1861.

What we know is that in the same community where Easter Bell Nichols was building her free life — on the same land where Joseph Nichols was farming his two acres — a child named Patience was living. The name had not been forgotten.

"We cannot know whether they knew they were honoring your ancestor. We know the name was there. In the same town. On the same land. The name had not been forgotten."

This is what freedom
looked like in this family.

Not a single moment. Not one declaration. A land claim filed while the enslaver still lived. A voter registration at a courthouse. Thirty acres on the same survey where you had been held. A child named Patience. A woman who testified. Ninety-seven years of a life that refused to be erased.
