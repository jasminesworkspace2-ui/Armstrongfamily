Where He Actually Came From

The 1860 free population census — the same schedule that lists his wife and children by name — records John Nichols's own birthplace as Louisiana, ca. 1797. This is the single strongest primary source statement of his origin found anywhere in this research.

Beyond that one fact, his own parents, siblings, and childhood remain unidentified. No will, no probate, no earlier census has surfaced a father's name, a mother's name, or a sibling for John Nichols. This is recorded here honestly, the same way this archive holds every unresolved origin — not filled in with a guess, left open until a document says otherwise.

The Marriage That Bound Two Enslaving Families

On October 14, 1837, John Nichols married Frances "Fanny" Waldrop in Austin County, Texas. She was the daughter of Ezekiah Parrish Waldrop and Martha "Patsy" Davenport Waldrop — the same Ezekiah Waldrop already documented on this archive's Waldrop enslaver page as the man who held Patience for over thirty years, and the same Patsy Davenport who received Patience as a gift from her own father, Francis Davenport, in 1795.

Fanny was born in South Carolina around 1802 — five years after her own father is first documented in Newberry County. She grew up inside the same household that held Patience and, later, Clarissa. She was already in Texas before the March 25, 1839 Rankin County instrument named her with seven siblings as joint grantees while reciting Patience and her increase. The instrument does not divide Patience's named family into eight individual shares, so it cannot be used to say which person, if any, passed specifically to Fanny.

1839 Rankin County, Mississippi Deed — Deed Book 4, Page 435
"...Fanny Micholes..." — one of eight children of Hezekiah Parrish Waldrop and the late Martha "Patsy" Davenport Waldrop named jointly as grantees in the March 25, 1839 instrument. The record does not establish a person-specific allocation to Fanny; her Texas household is independently documented.

Researcher note, typed directly on the original document by Kathryn R. Bonner (Mrs. W.G.), Marianna, AR — descendant of Wiley B. Waldrop: "FANNY MICHOLES is really NICHOLS. FANNY WALDROP married in Austin Co., TEXAS, 14 Oct., 1837. JOHN NICHOLS — I have had this mar. rec. for many years… krb"

Fanny's own seven confirmed siblings — Claiborne Parrish Waldrop, James D. Waldrop, Stephen R. Waldrop, Wiley B. Waldrop, Patsy Stoddard, Sarah Robertson, and Selina Robertson — are the same eight people (with Fanny) who divided Patience, Rachel, Clarissa, Tom, William, Amanda, and Harriet among themselves in 1839. John Nichols married directly into the family that had held this archive's oldest confirmed ancestors for over four decades before he ever met Fanny.

What the Law Actually Paid Him

The Bellville reconstruction page already documents John Nichols's land at its peak — 4,000 acres, the Stephenson grant, the Austin-Hodge grant, the sale to the Bell brothers that founded the town. What it does not walk through is why the colonization system handed land out in the specific amounts it did.

Stephen F. Austin's original 1821 colonization terms paid settlers directly, in acreage, for the people in their household — including the people they enslaved: 640 acres for a single man, 320 more for a wife, 160 for each child, and 80 acres for every person he held in bondage. Read against the Stephen F. Austin Register entry already confirmed on the community page — "John Nicholls, age 33, wife Fanny age 31, two children, one other dependent" — that formula accounts for a real, calculable share of his earliest Texas landholding before his 1832 grant was even issued: 640 for himself, 320 for Fanny, 320 for two children, and 80 more for the one enslaved dependent he already held. Eight hundred acres of that earliest calculation existed because of the people in his household. Eighty of it existed because of one person he owned.

640 acres for a man. 320 for his wife. 80 for every person he enslaved.

— the ratio Mexican colonial law assigned to a human being held in bondage, one quarter the value of a wife, half the value of a child

This is not a claim that his full 4,000-acre estate was built entirely on this formula — his 1832 grant of one league and one labor came through a separate, later colonization process. It is a claim that the earliest, foundational logic of Texas land distribution treated an enslaved human being as a unit of value the government paid for, the same as it paid for a wife or a child — and John Nichols's own household, on the record his own arrival register shows, was compensated on exactly that basis from his very first year in Texas.

Joseph Nichols: The Man Who Connects These Records to Our Family

This is not a speculative side branch. Joseph Nichols is Jasmine Lee's documented 3rd great-grandfather.

Joseph Nichols is not simply another Nichols in this archive. He is part of Jasmine Lee's direct maternal family line and is identified in the research handoff as her 3rd great-grandfather. The documentary reconstruction places him in Bellville, Austin County, Texas, where he later became the husband of Easter Bell. His pre-emancipation enslaver and household remain a research question.

The records therefore have to be read in two directions at once. One direction follows John Nichols — the enslaver, landholder, and husband of Fanny Waldrop. The other follows Joseph Nichols — the enslaved man whose descendants include Hellen Nichols Armstrong and, ultimately, Jasmine Lee. The shared surname is a documented post-emancipation surname connection, but the pre-emancipation source of Joseph's Nichols surname remains unresolved.torically significant, but it is not itself proof of biological paternity.

Direct Ancestor

Research Handoff identifies Joseph Nichols as born about 1847 in Texas and as Jasmine Lee's 3rd great-grandfather. He was born enslaved, but his specific pre-emancipation enslaver and household remain unresolved; the John Nichols Piney Creek property network is an important research context, not yet a direct bridge.

Freedom & Marriage

Joseph was free by 1870 and married Easter Bell. Their Bellville family is documented in the post-emancipation record and forms the next generation of the direct line.

DNA Signal

AncestryDNA ThruLines identifies Clarissa as a 4th great-grandmother and Joseph Nichols as a 3rd great-grandfather, with multiple matches through the Nichols descendants. DNA supports the ancestral reconstruction but does not independently name Joseph's biological father.

Open Maternal Placement

The 1839 deed names William, Amanda, and Harriet as Clarissa's children. Joseph was born later, around 1847. Research must determine whether he was a later-born child of Clarissa, a son of Amanda, a son of Harriet, or part of another undocumented branch.

Vital Record Gap

No death certificate for Joseph Nichols has been located in the research assembled to date. A death record remains an active search target because it may identify parents, birthplace, informant, or other family relationships.

The 1860 schedule is a crucial pre-emancipation record for the Nichols family reconstruction. The schedule is unnamed: it records age, sex, and race, not the names of the enslaved people. John Nichols and Joseph Nichols: One Surname, Two Historical Positions

This is one of the most important unresolved questions in the Nichols reconstruction: what, exactly, was the relationship between John Nichols and Joseph Nichols?

What the record can establish is already substantial. John Nichols was a documented enslaver in Austin County. The 1860 schedule documents nine unnamed people in his household; Joseph is the strongest age candidate among them, but the schedule itself does not name him. Joseph later became a free man using the Nichols surname. Joseph married Easter Bell and became the father of the Nichols children from whom Jasmine's direct line descends.

What the present record does not establish is that John Nichols was Joseph's biological father. That possibility has been raised in the broader research, but it remains a hypothesis unless a documentary or genetic bridge can distinguish it from other possible relationships.

The Research Rule
  • Do not turn the Nichols surname into a biological parentage claim.
  • Do not confuse John Nichols's legal ownership of Joseph with biological kinship.
  • Use DNA matches to test the family reconstruction, not to manufacture a missing documentary relationship.
  • Search for a continuity bridge: wills, estate inventories, bills of sale, emancipation records, Freedmen's Bureau records, post-emancipation households, land transactions, and records naming Joseph's parents or siblings.
  • Search specifically for Joseph Nichols's death certificate or other death record; no certificate has been located yet.
  • Do not state Clarissa, Amanda, or Harriet as Joseph's mother until a documentary or sufficiently strong biological bridge supports one placement.

Ten People Stated in His Possession at Death

Archive pages currently preserve a March 3 / March 4, 1864 conflict for John Nichols's death date. His own probate file has not been located in the Austin County courthouse index under the spelling variants searched, but a separate deed record signed March 21 and filed September 2, 1864 survives.

Austin County Deed Records, Vol. L No. 220, Pages 238–244 · Signed March 21, 1864 · Filed September 2, 1864
"There were ten negroes in the possession of the said John Nichols deceased at the time of his death."

Ten people are stated in the 1864 deed record, compared with nine unnamed people on the 1860 slave schedule — a real, unreconciled discrepancy that should not be smoothed over. A birth, death, purchase, sale, transfer, movement between households, enumeration difference, or another event could have changed the count; the present evidence does not identify the cause. The deed also uses language identifying the property with Fanny Nichols, John’s widow. That wording is important, but by itself it does not establish the precise legal mechanism, timing, or chain by which her interest arose. The missing probate file should not be explained by inference from this deed alone.
