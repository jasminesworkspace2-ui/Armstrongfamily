A Personal Note · From Jasmine M. Lee · Dock Ellis Foundation A Letter to Our Family Why This Research Matters — And What I Hope It Becomes

To every member of this family —

Seven years ago I started asking questions. Simple ones at first. Where did we come from? Who were the people before Grandma Margie? What were their names? Where did they live? What happened to them?

What I found changed how I understand myself, this family, and what it means to be who we are. I found Easter Bell Nichols — born enslaved on July 4, 1848, in Bellville, Texas. I found her mother Clarissa, and Clarissa's mother Patience, who was born in South Carolina in the 1780s. I found a great-grandmother named Eva Armstrong Ballard who ran a café on San Antonio's East Side and was subpoenaed to testify in a police brutality case in 1955 — and told the truth. I found a great-uncle named James Wilson Ballard Jr. who made the front page of the San Antonio Light for helping break a cover-up case. I found ministers and porters and waitresses and gamblers and Air Force sergeants and community organizers. I found people who built things under impossible conditions and kept going.

I built this website so that what I found does not disappear. So that the names don't get lost again. So that the next generation doesn't have to start from zero the way I did.

This research is ongoing. You will find small errors — a death date that still needs confirming, a married name I haven't been able to verify, a birth town that records disagree on. Genealogy is never finished. Every generation adds to the story. What matters is that the foundation is here, sourced to primary documents, and honest about what we know and what we don't.

"You can't know where you're going unless you truly understand where you came from."

Teach your children who they are. Tell them Easter Bell's name. Tell them Eva's. Tell them that James Ballard Jr. stood up in 1954 and told the truth to a district attorney in a city where telling the truth about a white police officer could get you killed. Tell them that Margie Nell Ballard made it from Fort Worth to California with seven children and kept going. Tell them they come from people who kept going.

One day I hope we can all be in the same room. A reunion. A dinner. Something. People who share blood and history and these names sitting together and knowing — really knowing — who we are. That is the dream behind all of this work.

Until then, this website is our gathering place.

If you have family photographs, documents, stories, or information about any family member — especially birth dates, marriage records, death dates, or details about people whose information here is incomplete — please reach out. Every piece helps. No detail is too small.

Your Great-Grandfather · August 26, 1897 – March 13, 1953 · Palestine TX · Austin State Hospital James Wilson Ballard Sr. Son of Rev. George W. Ballard · Husband of Eva Armstrong · Father of Margie Nell Ballard

James Wilson Ballard Sr. was born on August 26, 1897 in Palestine, Anderson County, Texas — the son of Rev. George W. Ballard and Mary Ann Edwards Ballard. He grew up in a family that moved constantly across Texas following his father's ministry. By 1924 he was in Fort Worth with Eva Armstrong, his wife, working as a porter and living at 905 East 2nd Street — a row of working-class Black duplexes now considered eligible for the National Register of Historic Places.

James Wilson Ballard Sr. was not an easy man to know from the records. He appears and disappears. His children were born in Fort Worth and San Antonio while the family moved between cities. By 1942, when he registered for the WWII draft in San Antonio, he listed not his wife Eva but a man named Louis Durvin as his next of kin — evidence that by that point he and Eva were already separated, though they never divorced.

By 1948, James was a patient at Austin State Hospital in Austin, Travis County — a psychiatric institution. He was suffering from psychosis with syphilitic meningoencephalitis, a late-stage neurological complication of untreated syphilis that destroys the brain over years. He had been sick since at least June 11, 1948.

The 1950 United States Federal Census found him at Austin State Hospital — Dwelling V32, Apartment Ma — a segregated Black male ward. He was 51 years old, listed as Separated, in a ward with approximately 50 other Black men ranging in age from 19 to 90. Austin State Hospital in 1950 maintained racially segregated wards under Texas state policy. The conditions in Black wards were consistently inferior to white wards — less staffing, less treatment, less visibility. James Wilson Ballard Sr. spent his last years in that ward, cut off from his family, while Eva was building 150 Paul Street in San Antonio and raising their children largely alone.

He died at the hospital on March 13, 1953 — the same date as his daughter Margie's 24th birthday. He had been institutionalized for at least five years. He never left.

Eva Armstrong Ballard had been building the household and the business at 150 Paul Street largely alone for years before his death. She purchased the Paul Street property in April 1944 in her own name. Their son James Jr.'s 1944 draft card listed only Eva as next of kin — no mention of his father. James Wilson Ballard Sr. was alive during all of it but not present for any of it.

His marital status on his death certificate is listed as Separated. His last known address was 1224 Verbena Street, Fort Worth — the same address where his mother Mary Ann Edwards Ballard had died in 1949.

His Father — The Ballard Line Confirmed

James Wilson Ballard Sr.'s death certificate names his father as G.I. Ballard — born Keller, Texas. This is Rev. George W. Ballard, whose death certificate (Texas State File No. 51612) confirms his birth on September 4, 1861 in Keller, Tarrant County, Texas and names his father as Newton Ballard. The chain is documented by primary sources at every generation.

His mother's maiden name is listed on the death certificate as Mary Edward Ballard — born LaGrange, Fayette County, Texas. This connects the Ballard family to Fayette County — the same county where Theodore Williams Jr., the confirmed biological father of James Jr.'s half-brother Michael Corbin Sr., was also born.

The Note on August 26

James Wilson Ballard Sr. was born on August 26, 1897. His grandfather Newton Ballard filed his land patent for 160 acres in Upshur County, Texas on August 26, 1856 — exactly forty-one years earlier. The same calendar date. Whether that is coincidence or family commemoration is not something the records can answer. But it is noted here.

What We Don't Know

We do not know when James Wilson Ballard Sr. and Eva Armstrong formally separated. We do not know who Louis Durvin was or what his relationship to James was. We do not know whether James ever saw his children after moving to Austin. We do not know if his children visited him at the hospital.

What we know is that Eva Armstrong Ballard built everything — 150 Paul Street, The Spot, the community standing, the life in San Antonio — without him. And that he died on his daughter's birthday, at a state institution, and is remembered here because his children's story cannot be told without knowing where they came from.

James Wilson Ballard Sr. was born on the same date his grandfather filed a land patent. He died on his daughter's birthday. The records do not explain what kind of man he was. They only tell us he was here, and then he wasn't.

Your 3rd Great-Grandmother · July 4, 1848 – December 17, 1935 Easter Bell The Woman This Entire Story Is About

If there is one person at the center of this family's history, it is Easter Bell. She was born enslaved on July 4, 1848 — Independence Day — in Bellville, Austin County, Texas. She died free on December 17, 1935, in the same town, at 87 years old. Between those two dates she raised at least eleven children by multiple men, survived two husbands, held onto two acres of land for the rest of her life, and watched her grandchildren begin building lives across Texas and eventually California. She did all of this on land that bore the name of the man who had once owned her.

She did not leave. She did not need to. The land was hers now.

Her life was not simple. She was enslaved until age 17. Her first child Katie was born when Easter Bell was approximately 11 years old — conceived in bondage, under conditions that were not her choice. She married Joseph Nichols in 1864 and had eight children with him over thirty years. She also had John Nagy in 1872 — a child whose surname remains unexplained. After Joseph disappeared from records around 1900 she eventually married Hannibal Pink on July 30, 1914. She outlived him too. She was widowed again before 1920. She kept going. At 82 she was living in Ballinger, Runnels County with her son William Willie. At 87 she fell, fractured her left femur, and died.

Easter Bell Nichols was not a symbol. She was a woman who lived 87 complicated, surviving, fully human years.

June 1900 · The Whole Family in the Bellville Jail What the Census Found on Piney Creek

On census day in June 1900, the U.S. Census enumerator walked through the Austin County Jail in Bellville and counted everyone present. What he found on Sheets 12 and 13 of that census was Easter Bell's entire family — imprisoned together.

Sheet 12: John Armstrong (age 22), Helen Armstrong / Hellen Nichols (age 23), and baby Eva Armstrong — just 4 months old. Easter Bell's daughter, her son-in-law, and her infant granddaughter.

Sheet 13: Joe Nichols (age 52) — Easter Bell's husband. Oscar Nichols (age 26) — Joseph's son.

The building they were held in was the Austin County Jail at Bellville — built in 1896 on land that had originally been granted to John Nichols on October 20, 1832 by the Mexican Government. The man who had enslaved Easter Bell's mother had owned the very land where her family was now imprisoned. Easter Bell herself was not in the jail that day — she was 52 years old, living on Piney Creek, on the land her family owned free and clear, waiting.

This was almost certainly convict leasing — the system used across the post-Reconstruction South where Black men and women were arrested on minor or fabricated charges and forced to work as essentially free labor. John Armstrong's occupation was listed as Section Hand — railroad labor — one of the primary users of convict lease workers in Texas. Joseph was 52 years old, a farmer who owned his home. Being in that jail on census day likely had nothing to do with any crime.

Joseph Nichols does not appear in any record after June 1900. He was alive in that jail. He was gone by 1910. What happened to him between those two dates has not been found.

July 10, 1862 · Three Years Before Freedom General Order No. 5 — Easter Bell Was 14 Years Old

On July 10, 1862, the Provost Marshal of Austin County published General Order No. 5 in the Bellville Countryman newspaper. Easter Bell was 14 years old. Rendy Blayn was approximately 45. This is the specific law that governed their daily lives on Piney Creek three years before emancipation.

"All persons owning or having control of slaves are notified and required not to allow such slaves to leave their premises at all at night without written passes specifying where they are to go, and when to return... all residents of this jurisdiction are authorized and required to arrest any slave found away from home at night in violation of this order." — Provost Marshal N. Holland, Austin County, July 10, 1862

Easter Bell could not leave the Nichols plantation at night without a written pass. Any white resident of Austin County was authorized to arrest her if she did. She could not carry a weapon. Any weapon found on her could be seized and kept by whoever took it. This was the legal framework of her daily life at age 14, in Bellville, Texas, three years before Juneteenth.

June 5, 1917 · The Draft Registration List Easter Bell's Grandchildren Register for War

On June 5, 1917, every male between 21 and 31 in Austin County was required to register for the World War I draft. The Bellville Times published the list by precinct, segregated — white registrants first, then "Colored" at the bottom. Easter Bell's grandchildren were on that list.

Born Into Bondage · July 4, 1848 Independence Day, Piney Creek, Bellville, Texas

Easter Bell was born on July 4, 1848 — Independence Day — on the John Nichols plantation along Piney Creek in Bellville, Texas. She was born enslaved. Her mother was Rendy Blayn, a woman born in North Carolina around 1817. Mother and daughter appear together in the 1860 slave schedule: a female age 43 and a female age 13 — no names, just numbers, because that was all the law required.

Her father is listed as unknown on her death certificate. That silence in the record says a great deal on its own — it was a common and painful reality for enslaved women, whose children often had unknown or unacknowledged fathers.

Easter Bell spent the first seventeen years of her life on that plantation. In 1865, everything changed.

Freedom · 1865 · What She Did With It Eleven Children · Two Husbands · 141 Years of Land

On December 18, 1865 — six months after emancipation — Easter Bell married Joseph Nichols. The man she married was himself the son of John Nichols — the enslaver who had held her in bondage. Joseph's mother was an enslaved woman whose name is not recorded. Joseph was enslaved on the same plantation as Easter Bell, carrying the same enslaver's name. When they married, two people who had both been property of the same man chose each other as free people and built a family together on the land where they had been held.

Joseph Nichols last appears in records in June 1900 — in the Austin County Jail alongside his son Oscar and Easter Bell's daughter Hellen. After that he is gone. By 1910 Easter Bell was listed as widowed. He likely died before Texas began mandatory death registration in 1903. No record of his death has been found.

At some point after emancipation the family acquired two acres of land in the J. Nichols Survey — the same survey named for the man who had enslaved them. Her family held that land for 141 years before losing it to a tax sale in 2006.

Later in life Easter Bell married Hannibal Pink on July 30, 1914. By 1920 she was listed as widowed again and head of household. She kept going. She always kept going. She died on December 17, 1935 in Bellville — 87 years old, having outlived two husbands, two daughters, and the entire institution that had once owned her.

Your Great-Grandmother · ca. 1899 – November 24, 1977 Eva Armstrong Ballard The Woman Your Family Remembers

Eva Armstrong Ballard is the person your mother and her siblings actually remember. Not through documents — through stories. The way she carried herself. The business she ran. The way she held her family together across cities and decades and hardships that would have broken most people. She is the living memory anchor for the generation that knew her, and she is the reason this family made it to California.

She was born approximately early 1899 in Bellville, Texas. She died on November 24, 1977 in Garden Grove, California. In between she traveled the full length of this family's story — from the plantation land of Austin County to the Pacific coast — and she did it on her own terms.

Bellville to Fort Worth · 1899–1911 The Daughter of Hellen Nichols Armstrong

Eva Armstrong was born approximately early 1899 in Bellville, Texas — the 1900 census shows her as 4 months old in June 1900, correcting the previously recorded birth year of 1900. Her mother was Hellen Nichols Armstrong, Easter Bell's daughter. Her father was John Armstrong.

When Eva was just 4 months old, she was in the Austin County Jail in Bellville with her parents. The June 1900 census found Hellen (age 23), John Armstrong (age 22), and baby Eva (4 months) all listed as inmates on Sheet 12 — and her grandfather Joe Nichols and Oscar Nichols on Sheet 13 of the same building.

Eva was eleven years old when her father was killed. On August 30, 1911, John Armstrong was shot dead near the East Front Street entrance to the Terminal Hotel in Fort Worth by a white transfer line operator named Jack Thompson. Thompson claimed Armstrong had assaulted his son earlier that day. The bullet entered Armstrong's mouth and came out the top of his head. Thompson surrendered to police, was released on $3,000 bond, waived his preliminary trial, and was never convicted. The verdict: justifiable homicide.

Four months before his death, on May 6, 1911, John Armstrong had published a legal notice in the Fort Worth newspaper severing his business connection with C.A. Steele & Co. He was a literate, independent businessman operating in Fort Worth's commercial world. Eva was old enough to understand what her father had been building. She was old enough to understand what had been taken.

Fort Worth to San Antonio · The Business Years Proprietor of The Spot · 1501 Burnet Street

Eva married James Wilson Ballard Sr. and had children in Fort Worth and San Antonio. But it is in San Antonio where Eva Armstrong Ballard built something that newspapers would write about for years.

Eva Armstrong Ballard owned and operated The Spot — a cafe at 1501 Burnet Street on San Antonio's East Side. She held the beer license for the establishment in her name. Her address was 150 Paul Street, San Antonio. Her son James Wilson Ballard Jr. lived with her at the same address. She was the proprietor of record. She was present. She ran the place.

The San Antonio Light identified her directly in March 1954 as "Mrs. Eva Ballard, 150 Paul, proprietor of The Spot." When police officers arrived at The Spot that night to investigate an alleged liquor violation, it was Eva who gave them the key to the storeroom. She cooperated. No charges were ever filed against her.

What happened next was one of San Antonio's most significant police brutality cases of the 1950s. Officer O.R. Graves shot and killed John Wesley Caldwell in the back office of The Spot — demanding Caldwell open a safe that belonged to the establishment's gambling operator. Caldwell said he didn't know the combination. Graves shot him anyway. Then Graves handed a knife to Detective Hubble and told him to place it under Caldwell's right hand. Two officers swore to the cover-up in sworn statements.

The entire East Side community mobilized. The NAACP got involved. The district attorney launched a grand jury investigation. Eva's son James Ballard Jr. was a witness. Eva herself was a witness. The community demanded accountability. Officer Graves was ultimately tried — but in Atascosa County, far from the San Antonio jury pool that might actually hold him accountable.

Eva Armstrong Ballard ran a business at the center of a civil rights fight. She kept running it.

The Full Story · For the Family Eva, James, Margie, and The Spot on San Antonio's East Side

To understand Eva Armstrong Ballard, you have to understand what San Antonio's East Side was in the 1940s and 1950s. It was the center of Black life in San Antonio — its own economy, its own politics, its own newspaper, its own network of people who took care of each other because nobody else was going to. The East Side was not a place people ended up. It was a place people built.

Eva Armstrong Ballard built something there.

How Eva Got to San Antonio

Eva was born approximately early 1899 in Bellville, Texas — the granddaughter of Easter Bell Nichols, who had been born enslaved on that same Piney Creek land in 1848. When Eva was 4 months old, she was counted in the Austin County Jail census alongside her mother Hellen, her father John Armstrong, and her grandfather Joseph Nichols. The whole family, imprisoned together on census day in June 1900.

When Eva was eleven, her father John Armstrong was shot and killed in Fort Worth by a white man named Jack Thompson. Four months before he died, her father had published a legal notice in the Fort Worth newspaper — cutting ties with a business partner, protecting what he had built. He was literate. He was a businessman. He was trying to build something. Then he was gone. The verdict: justifiable homicide. Thompson was never convicted.

Eva grew up in Fort Worth after that. She married James Wilson Ballard Sr. and had children — James Jr., Margie Nell, and others. She ran a Night Club and Cafe. She owned property. She kept moving. When San Antonio opened up, Eva Armstrong Ballard was ready.

The Spot · 1501 Burnet Street

By the early 1950s Eva had established herself on the East Side. She lived at 150 Paul Street. She held the beer license for an establishment at 1501 Burnet Street — at the corner of Nolan and North New Braunfels Avenue — called The Spot. The San Antonio Light would later identify her directly as "Mrs. Eva Ballard, 150 Paul, proprietor of The Spot."

Her son James Wilson Ballard Jr. lived with her at the same address, embedded in the same East Side community. Her daughter Margie Nell Ballard was there too — at 24 years old, operating in her mother's world with enough authority that a police officer's sworn statement named her directly, saying he had told Margie Ballard to either make the man stop bootlegging or fire him.

Margie was not a bystander. She was known. She had standing.

The Night Everything Changed · 1954

In early 1954, Officer O.R. Graves and two other San Antonio police officers arrived at The Spot claiming to investigate a liquor violation. Eva gave them the key to her storeroom. What happened next in the back office became one of the most significant police brutality cases in San Antonio's history.

Graves demanded that an employee named John Wesley Caldwell open a safe. Caldwell said he couldn't — the safe wasn't his. Graves pointed his pistol and clicked it twice. Then it went off. Caldwell slumped forward in his chair with a bullet hole above his left ear.

Graves claimed Caldwell had attacked him with a knife. But the other two officers later gave sworn statements under oath telling a different story. Detective Hubble had a knife he had picked up at a different scene earlier that night. After Caldwell was shot, the knife was placed under the dead man's right hand. Two officers swore to it. The knife had been planted.

The official verdict: justifiable homicide. The community response: mobilization. Sixty community representatives formed a citizens committee. The NAACP got involved. They went to the district attorney. They went to the city manager. The San Antonio newspapers reported everything.

Police found no record against Mrs. Ballard. No charges were ever filed against Eva. She had given investigators the key to her own storeroom. She had cooperated. She had done nothing wrong.

Eva kept her business.

The Ice Cream Family · Who Leroy Armstrong Was

Leroy "Icecream" Armstrong Jr. — the man at the center of The Spot's gambling network — did not get his nickname from the streets. He inherited it from his grandfather.

Cornelius Armstrong Sr. was born in Paul Junction, Austin County, Texas — the same county as Bellville where Eva grew up. He was orphaned at age three and raised by the white family that had employed his mother. As a child in that household, he ate so much frozen dessert that the family nicknamed him "Ice Cream." He carried that name for forty-four years. When he died in December 1944, the San Antonio Register called him "a widely known San Antonian" whose funeral was "largely attended."

At age fourteen, Cornelius went to work for Charles Bellinger — the most powerful Black political figure on San Antonio's East Side — and stayed for the rest of his life. When Charles died, Cornelius continued working for his son Valmo Bellinger. The Armstrong-Bellinger connection ran through two generations on both sides.

Cornelius had two sons with a woman named Edna Josephine Burse. The first, Cornelius Jr., also called "Little Ice Cream," died in July 1944 at age twenty-five from a ruptured appendix. The second, Leroy Armstrong Sr., was born April 12, 1915. Leroy Sr. had a son with a woman named Audrey Armstrong — Leroy Armstrong Jr., born February 15, 1932 in San Antonio. This was the man who ran The Spot. The man Eva held the beer license for. The man whose employee was shot and killed in Eva's back office. His nickname: Little Joe Cream.

Officer O.R. Graves was tried for the murder of John Wesley Caldwell in Atascosa County — thirty-five miles south of San Antonio — on a change of venue. The trial began January 4, 1955. On the night of January 5, 1955, the jury deliberated for forty-five minutes.

Not guilty.

Graves wept. His wife rushed to embrace him. He shook hands with the jurors. He told reporters: "I want to live in this part of the country. This is where I belong."

John Wesley Caldwell was thirty-eight years old when Graves shot him. He died the next day. Two officers had admitted in sworn statements that a knife was placed on his body. Historical Context · Bellville, Austin County, Texas · ca. 1900 The World John Armstrong Was Born Into

The Town

Bellville in 1872 was a small county seat of about 1,500 people. The Black community clustered around the Piney Creek area — descendants of enslaved people who had worked the John Nichols plantation and surrounding farms. John grew up in this tight-knit community where everyone was connected through the experience of enslavement and emancipation.

The Era

Reconstruction had ended. Jim Crow laws were hardening across the South. Black men in Texas who were educated, employed, and independent — as John would become — navigated a world designed to limit their advancement at every turn. John Armstrong moved through that world anyway.

The Fort Worth Years
Building a Life in Cowtown · 1899–1911

By the turn of the century the Armstrong family had moved to Fort Worth — the fastest-growing city in Texas, a railroad hub and cattle market that drew Black workers and families from across the state. August 30, 1911 · Fort Worth, Texas
The Killing of John Armstrong — The Full Record

On the evening of Wednesday, August 30, 1911, John Armstrong — a Black truck driver employed by the Denver News Service — was shot and killed near the East Front Street entrance of the Terminal Hotel in Fort Worth, Texas. It was 6:30 in the evening. Jack Thompson, a white man who operated a transfer line with an office at Fifteenth and Commerce Streets, fired a single shot from a Smith & Wesson revolver. The bullet entered Armstrong's mouth and exited through the top of his head. Armstrong was dead within three minutes.

Thompson was seated in his buggy when the confrontation occurred. Armstrong had driven up in his wagon, hitched his horse, and approached Thompson as he was driving toward the Texas & Pacific depot. Thompson did not remain at the scene. He drove around the corner onto Houston Street, where he encountered George H. Mulkey, the former Fort Worth police commissioner. Accompanied by Mulkey, Thompson drove to police headquarters and surrendered voluntarily. His bond was set at $3,000 by Justice Tom Maben, signed by Walter Scott, Clarence Parker, W. R. Parker, George W. Mulkey, and W. P. McLean Jr.

The Backstory — What the Newspapers Reported
A Prior History Between the Two Men

The confrontation did not arise without prior history. Thompson's twelve-year-old son, Ernest, had reportedly been slapped and choked by Armstrong earlier that same Wednesday morning — around 11 o'clock — at the corner of Fifteenth and Commerce Streets. According to the boy's account, Armstrong had been driving an express wagon and followed Ernest for several blocks before pursuing him into his father's transfer office, where the assault allegedly took place. It was further alleged that Armstrong made additional threats against Thompson that afternoon and called to him in front of the Terminal Hotel shortly before the fatal encounter.

As Reported in the Newspapers · Ernest Thompson · Age 12 What Jack Thompson's Son Said

These are Ernest Thompson's words as recorded by the Fort Worth press. They represent one side of a story told entirely without John Armstrong's voice.

On a Previous Incident

"The other day this negro slapped me and I told him that some time I was going to get even with him."

On the Morning of the Shooting — Wednesday, August 30, 1911

"This morning I met him and had a whip in my hand. I hit him with the whip, and he ran me into my father's office and slapped and choked me before they pulled him off of me."

On Armstrong's Departure from the Office

"When he went out the door he said he was going to 'get somebody.' I couldn't understand whether he said he was going to get me or my father. When my father came in I told him about it."

The Eyewitness · Lon Sullivan · Coroner's Inquest
The Only Civilian Witness on Record

Witness Lon Sullivan — also identified in some accounts as Lon Sullins — was walking east along East Front Street, between Main and Houston Streets, when the shooting occurred. He testified at the coroner's inquest presided over by Justice Maben. His account was the key witness testimony recorded in the press and aligned almost entirely with Thompson's self-defense plea.

Sullivan's Testimony — What He Described

Position

Sullivan was within ten feet of Armstrong when the shot was fired. Armstrong had just driven up in his wagon and hitched his horse when Thompson came along and stopped his buggy.

Armstrong's Approach

Armstrong stepped off the curb with his left hand raised and his right hand behind him, cursing Thompson and telling him that no white man could run over him. He was still moving toward Thompson the entire time.

The Shot

When Armstrong reached the buggy wheels, Thompson raised his pistol and fired. Armstrong reeled and fell to the pavement. Thompson drove off. Armstrong was dead within three minutes.

Sullivan's Own Words

"I could not tell where the gun came from, but it was in his right hand when I first saw it."

What This Testimony Does Not Tell Us
Reading the Record Against the Grain

Sullivan was essentially the only civilian witness whose testimony was recorded in the newspapers. His account aligned almost perfectly with Thompson's self-defense plea. He described Armstrong's right hand as being behind his back — implying a possible weapon — yet no weapon was found on Armstrong's body.

The coroner's inquest and verdict focused entirely on how Armstrong died, not on whether the killing was justified. Armstrong's own community, his coworkers at the Denver News Service, or anyone who might have spoken to his character or his version of events was never quoted in any article. The press covered the shooting as Thompson's story — with Armstrong reduced to the body on the pavement.

Sullivan's account of Armstrong saying no white man could run over him was framed as aggression. It could equally be read as a man defending his basic dignity in a city and a time that demanded Black men accept being run over without complaint. John Armstrong would not accept that. That refusal may have cost him his life.

His death was recorded without ceremony. His name highlighted in newspaper searches but his life reduced to a footnote in a story centered almost entirely on the man who shot him and the legal fate that — as history so often showed in cases like this — never came for Jack Thompson at all.

"Armstrong's own community, coworkers, or anyone who might have spoken to his character or his side of events was never quoted in any of the articles. The law of Texas in 1911 called it justifiable homicide. His family called it murder. Both things are documented. Only one is true."
Understanding What Happened
1911 Texas — The World That Killed John Armstrong

What happened to John Armstrong on August 30, 1911 was not an isolated event. It was part of a pattern that defined Black life in Texas in the early twentieth century. Between 1877 and 1950, Texas recorded more lynchings than any other state — over 600 documented cases. Thousands more killings of Black men by white men were classified as justifiable homicide, settled out of court, or simply never investigated.

A Black man who was employed, literate, independent, and assertive — as John Armstrong clearly was — occupied a particularly dangerous position in Jim Crow Texas. His May 1911 newspaper notice shows he was willing to act publicly and legally to protect his interests. His visibility and assertiveness may have made him a target. We know that Jack Thompson shot John Armstrong at close range. We know Thompson went free. We know Eva Armstrong grew up without her father.

The Fort Worth Star-Telegram reported the story. The legal system processed the charge and returned a verdict. Everything happened according to the law of 1911 Texas — a law that did not value John Armstrong's life equally to Jack Thompson's.

"He was shot near the East Front Street entrance to the Terminal Hotel. He was 39 years old. His daughter Eva was 11. Jack Thompson was never convicted. The law of Texas in 1911 called it justifiable homicide. His family called it murder. Both things can be documented. Only one is true."
What He Left Behind
John Armstrong's Legacy

John Armstrong left behind a wife, a daughter, and a family name that carries forward to this day. Hellen Nichols Armstrong never left Bellville — she returned to Piney Creek after John's death and died there on December 5, 1931. Eva Armstrong grew up in Fort Worth without her father, eventually making it to San Antonio and then to California.

Eva never forgot where she came from. She named her home state in every document. She kept the Armstrong name alive through her daughter Margie Nell Ballard and her granddaughter Hjordis L. Corbin. John Armstrong's great-great-granddaughter is Jasmine M. Lee — who found his name in a newspaper archive in 2026, 115 years after his death, and made sure the family knew his story.

John Armstrong was born free on Piney Creek seven years after his parents were freed. He learned to read. He drove wagons through Fort Worth. He put his name in the newspaper. He was killed before he turned forty. He deserved more years. He deserved justice. This page is what we can give him instead.

Chapter One Patience — and What Was Done to Her Newberry County, South Carolina · ca. 1780s – 1839

Sometime in the 1780s, in Newberry County, South Carolina, a woman was born. Her name was Patience. We do not know who her parents were. We do not know what she looked like, what language her grandparents spoke, what country her ancestors were taken from. The document that first names her does not record any of that. It records only that she was given away.

On August 4, 1795, a white man named Francis Davenport Sr. signed a deed transferring Patience to his daughter Patty, who had married a man named Hezekiah Waldrop. The deed describes Patience as "one Negro girl." She was approximately fifteen years old.

The deed contained four words that would define the lives of every child Patience would ever have: "with her increase." Under South Carolina law, this clause meant that all of Patience's future children — every child she had not yet conceived, every child not yet born, every grandchild she might live to hold — were automatically the property of the Waldrop family from the moment of their first breath. No transaction required. No auction block. Patience's body, and the bodies that came from her body, belonged to the Waldrops forever and by law.

Patience had children. We know the names of six of them — Clarissa, Rachel, Tom, William, Amanda, and Harriet — because they appear in the 1839 Waldrop estate deed, distributed among white heirs after Hezekiah Waldrop died. Six people. Six lives. Itemized in a legal document alongside livestock and furniture.

We do not know what happened to Patience after 1839. We do not know if she was still alive when her children were divided up. We do not know if she watched them go. The record ends where the law allowed it to end — which is to say, at the boundary of what the enslaving family needed to document.

But her daughter Clarissa survived. And Clarissa's descendants — confirmed now by 46 living DNA matches — survived. Which means Patience survived, in the only way the law allowed her to.

"one Negro girl named Patience with her increase" Newberry County, South Carolina · Deed Book C, Pages 367–368 · August 4, 1795
Clarissa ca. 1807 — unknown · South Carolina → Mississippi → Texas

Clarissa was Patience's daughter. She was born into bondage before she drew her first breath — owned by the Waldrop family the instant she existed, by the terms of her mother's 1795 deed. She never knew a day of legal freedom in South Carolina or Mississippi.

In 1839, when Hezekiah Waldrop died and his estate was divided, Clarissa was distributed among his heirs. Her mother Patience disappears from the record here. Her siblings disappear. Whether the family was kept together or torn apart is not documented.

What we know is that Clarissa eventually reached Texas — and that her descendants stayed. 46 separate living DNA matches on Ancestry all trace back to Clarissa as a common ancestor. She is the genetic root of this family's documented history. The path from Clarissa to Joseph Nichols — the man Easter Bell married in 1865 — is still being researched. But the DNA confirms the connection across 200 years and counting.

Clarissa is the reason this family exists. We do not know her face. We do not know her voice. We know her blood — because it runs in us, confirmed by science that did not exist when she lived, answering a question she never knew anyone would ask.

Chapter Two Piney Creek — The Land Where Easter Bell Was Born Bellville, Austin County, Texas · 1834 – 1865

In 1830, Mexico granted a league of land — 4,428 acres — to a man named John Nichols, along a creek called Piney Creek in Austin County, Texas. The land grant document describes the terrain in careful Spanish — the blackjack trees, the post oaks, the bearing of the survey lines. What it does not describe is the enslaved people who would work that land for the next three decades.

The Nichols and Waldrop families were connected by marriage. Frances Jane Waldrop — called Fanny, daughter of Hezekiah Waldrop — married into the Nichols family. This is the specific mechanism by which enslaved people from the Waldrop household entered the Nichols plantation. Fanny Waldrop Nichols brought with her the people her father had owned. The DNA connection between this family and the Waldrop line — confirmed in March 2026 by a living descendant named Sherry Newman Welty who grew up in Bellville and knew the Nichols family by name — locks this chain in place.

Somewhere in that chain was a woman named Rendy Blayn, born in North Carolina around 1817. She did not choose to come to Texas. She was brought. By 1860 she was 43 years old, enslaved on the Nichols plantation, and she had a daughter. Her daughter's name was Easter Bell. Easter Bell was twelve years old in 1860, born on the banks of Piney Creek on July 4, 1848 — Independence Day.

Let that land in for a moment. A child born into slavery on Independence Day. Born on the same day the Declaration of Independence proclaimed that all men are created equal with the unalienable right to liberty. Easter Bell would wait seventeen more years for a liberty that the country had supposedly already declared was hers.

In 1860, the John Nichols slave schedule recorded nine enslaved people on the Piney Creek plantation. They have no names in that document — only ages, sexes, and a note on their complexion. A female age 43, born in North Carolina: almost certainly Rendy. A female age 12, born in Texas: almost certainly Easter Bell. Mother and daughter. No names in the ledger. Just numbers in a column.

Among the enslaved people on that plantation was a man who would become Easter Bell's husband. Joseph Nichols was born approximately 1847 — the biological son of John Nichols himself by an enslaved woman whose name was never recorded. Under the law of the time, Joseph's enslaved status came from his mother, regardless of who his father was. The enslaver John Nichols had a son who was also his property. When Easter Bell and Joseph married in December 1865, two people who had been enslaved by the same man — one the child of an unknown enslaved woman by that man — chose each other in freedom and built a family together on the same land where they had been held. This is not a detail the records announce. It is something you only find when you look closely enough.

"Female, 12, Black, born Texas." — That was how the record described Easter Bell Nichols in 1860. No name. No family. No future anyone was required to acknowledge. 1860 U.S. Federal Census Slave Schedule · John Nichols household · Austin County, Texas
Rendy Blayn ca. 1817, North Carolina — unknown after 1865 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas

Rendy Blayn is Easter Bell's mother. We know her name because Easter Bell's 1935 death certificate — signed seventy years after emancipation — named her: Mother's Maiden Name: Rendy Blayn. Easter Bell remembered her mother's name until she was 87 years old. She made sure it was written down.

Rendy was born in North Carolina around 1817. She was transported to Texas as part of the enslaved labor force connected to the Nichols-Waldrop family network. The distance from North Carolina to Austin County, Texas is approximately 1,400 miles. She made that journey in chains, or under threat of chains, leaving behind whatever family she had — her own mother, her siblings, anyone who had known her before.

She appears in the 1860 slave schedule as a woman, age 43, Black, born in North Carolina. That is the last confirmed sighting of her in any document. After emancipation in 1865, her name does not appear in any census record we have found. Whether she survived, where she went, whether she was buried in Bellville alongside the family she helped create — we do not know. We are still looking.

What we know is that she raised a daughter who lived 87 years and never forgot her name.

Chapter Three June 19, 1865 — What Freedom Cost Bellville, Texas · Upshur County, Texas · 1865 – 1900

On June 19, 1865 — Juneteenth — General Gordon Granger arrived in Galveston, Texas and read General Order No. 3 aloud to a crowd of enslaved people who had been legally free for two and a half years without knowing it. The Emancipation Proclamation had been signed in January 1863. Texas had simply refused to comply. Easter Bell was seventeen years old when freedom finally arrived at Piney Creek. She had been alive for every one of the 912 days between the legal end of slavery and its actual end in Texas.

Freedom in 1865 was not a door opening. It was a door being unlocked with no guarantee that anyone on the other side would let you through. The formerly enslaved people of Austin County — and across the South — walked out of bondage into a world that had built its entire economy on their labor and saw no reason to compensate them for any of it. No land. No back wages. No reparations. Forty acres and a mule was a promise briefly floated and immediately betrayed.

Easter Bell stayed in Bellville. On December 18, 1865 — six months after Juneteenth — she married Joseph Nichols, a man who had also been enslaved on the same plantation, who had also taken the Nichols name from his enslaver. They had twelve children. They built a life on Piney Creek. And at some point after emancipation, through some transaction we have not yet fully documented, this family acquired two acres of the J. Nichols Survey — two acres of the same land where Easter Bell had been born and held in bondage — and they made it their own.

141 Years That Land Stayed in the Family Easter Bell's family acquired two acres of the J. Nichols Survey — the same land where she had been born into slavery. They held it for 141 years. A tax suit filed in 1997 resulted in a sheriff's deed to Bellville ISD in 2006. The family held that land from the Civil War through two World Wars, through the Great Migration, through the Civil Rights Movement, through the invention of the internet — and lost it to a tax debt in 2006.

Meanwhile, one hundred miles north, another formerly enslaved man was also staking his claim.

Newton Ballard had been enslaved in Mississippi before being brought to Texas. By 1856 — nine years before emancipation — he had somehow managed to file a land patent in Upshur County, Texas: Texas GLO File No. 3378, 160 acres on Caney Creek. We do not know how he accomplished this while still legally enslaved. We do not know who helped him or what it cost. What we know is that on August 26, 1856, Newton Bullard's name appeared on a land patent for 160 acres of Texas soil.

By 1870, Newton was gone. His wife Rebecca was listed as head of household in Upshur County, alone, raising three children. Newton Ballard had survived slavery, claimed 160 acres of land, fathered a son who would become a minister in Waco, and then disappeared from every record. The Freedmen's Bureau in Texas documented hundreds of murders of formerly enslaved Black men in the years immediately following emancipation — men killed by white Texans who refused to accept that the war had changed anything. Newton Ballard may have been one of them. We may never know.

What we know is that his son Rev. George W. Ballard was born in Keller, Texas in 1861, became a minister, moved to Waco, and had a son named James Wilson Ballard Sr. who married Eva Armstrong. And Eva Armstrong's mother Hellen was Easter Bell's daughter. Two families emerging from slavery — the Nichols line and the Ballard line — joined together in Fort Worth in the 1920s when James and Eva married and began building the family that eventually reached California.

Newton Ballard ca. 1830, Mississippi — disappeared ca. 1865–1870 · Upshur County, Texas

Newton Ballard was enslaved by Lewis F. Ballard in Smith County, Texas. He appears in the 1850 slave schedule. He somehow — through means we have not yet fully documented — filed a land patent in Upshur County, Texas in 1856 for 160 acres on Caney Creek. He sold that land legally in 1873 for $250 in gold. His name appears on Rev. George W. Ballard's 1937 death certificate as his father.

By 1870, Newton was gone. Rebecca Ballard — his wife — was head of household in Upshur County with three children. The historical context of Reconstruction-era East Texas makes his disappearance comprehensible in the most devastating way: formerly enslaved men were being murdered at a rate the Freedmen's Bureau could barely document, let alone prevent. A Black man who had claimed land, who had his name on legal documents, who had proved he could own property — he was exactly the kind of man white supremacists targeted in 1866 and 1867.

We are still searching. If there is a record of what happened to Newton Ballard, we have not found it yet. His family — his wife Rebecca, his son George, his grandchildren — carried his name forward without him.

Chapter Four Dave Armstrong — A Man Who Took His Enslaver's Name South Carolina · Upshur County, Texas · Bellville, Texas · 1845 – 1918

Dave Armstrong was born in South Carolina around 1845. He was transported to Upshur County, Texas — the same county where Newton Ballard would later claim land — and enslaved by the John K. Armstrong household. After emancipation he did what millions of formerly enslaved people were forced to do: he took his enslaver's surname. He became Dave Armstrong, keeping the only last name the law had ever allowed him to use.

By August 29, 1868, Dave Armstrong was in Bellville, Austin County, Texas — the same community where Easter Bell Nichols lived, where the Nichols plantation had stood. He married Clarissa Ann Morgan, a woman from North Carolina. They settled in Bellville. They had children: John, born 1872. Charlie, born 1874. Ida, born 1875. Joshua, born 1879. Bettie, born 1885.

These five children grew up free — the first generation of Armstrongs born without the weight of the law pressing down on them. They grew up in the same Bellville community as Easter Bell's children. They were neighbors. They were part of the same freedmen's community that had formed on the ash of the plantation system.

In 1900, the Bellville census page shows the Armstrong household a few doors from the Nichols household. Rosa Lott lived nearby. Her daughter Lula would marry Dave's son Joshua in 1903. The formerly enslaved families of Bellville were building a world together, intermarrying, buying land, going to the same churches, raising their children in the same neighborhoods.

Then on August 30, 1911, John Armstrong — Dave's eldest son, Eva's father — was shot and killed at the Terminal Hotel in Fort Worth by a white man named Jack Thompson. The verdict: justifiable homicide. Thompson was never convicted. Eva Armstrong was eleven years old.

Freedom was not safety. Freedom was not protection. Freedom was a legal status that white Texans were willing to ignore whenever it suited them. The Reconstruction-era violence that may have taken Newton Ballard did not end in 1865. It continued through 1911 and well beyond. John Armstrong's murder is documented. His killer's acquittal is documented. The pattern they represent has never ended.

Dave Armstrong and Clarissa Ann Morgan's family continued. Their son Joshua married Lula Lott, connected the Armstrong and Lott families across the Bellville community network. Their DNA — confirmed in 2025 through a living match named Debra Louise Carter Gholston whose grandmother descended from Dave's daughter Ida Armstrong Twiggs — runs through this family and can be measured in centimorgans two hundred years after Dave was born in South Carolina.

Chapter Five What Freedom Actually Built Fort Worth · San Antonio · Orange County, California · 1900 – Present

Three generations after emancipation, this is what the descendants of enslaved people had built:

Eva Armstrong Ballard — Easter Bell's granddaughter, Dave Armstrong's granddaughter — was born in Bellville in 1900 and died in Garden Grove, California in 1977. In between she owned property in San Antonio, held a beer license, ran a café on the East Side, survived a police shooting in her own back office, was subpoenaed to testify in a murder trial, and built a community standing that newspapers wrote about for years. She did all of this as a Black woman in Texas in the 1940s and 50s.

Her daughter Margie Nell Ballard was born on March 13, 1929 in Fort Worth — the same day her father James Wilson Ballard Sr. would die twenty-four years later, in a psychiatric institution in Austin. Margie raised seven children on Townsend Street in Santa Ana, California. She registered to vote in 1964. She stayed.

Her daughter Hjordis L. Corbin was born in Los Angeles in 1960. Her daughter Jasmine M. Lee was born in Orange County on January 25, 1982.

Jasmine is the founder and CEO of the Dock Ellis Foundation. She spent seven years finding the names on this page. She found Patience. She found Clarissa. She found Rendy Blayn. She found Newton Ballard and Dave Armstrong and Easter Bell and Hellen and Eva and all the rest. She found them because the DNA survived, the documents survived, and this family — against every mechanism designed to erase it — survived.

June 19, 1865 · Galveston, Texas · Our Family Was There Juneteenth How the Day America Honors Changed Our Family's Life Forever
June 19, 1865 General Order No. 3 · Galveston, Texas

Before June 19, 1865, Easter Bell's movements were controlled by law. On July 10, 1862 — three years before Juneteenth — the Provost Marshal of Austin County published General Order No. 5 in the Bellville Countryman, requiring all enslaved people to carry written passes to leave their enslaver's premises at night, authorizing any white resident to arrest any enslaved person found away from home, and prohibiting enslaved people from carrying weapons of any kind. Easter Bell was 14 years old when this order was published. Rendy Blayn was approximately 45. This was their daily legal reality on Piney Creek.

On June 19, 1865, Union soldiers arrived in Galveston, Texas and read General Order No. 3, announcing that all enslaved people in Texas were free. This was two and a half years after the Emancipation Proclamation had been signed. The delay was not an accident — Texas was one of the last Confederate states to receive the news, and enslavers had suppressed and withheld the information as long as they could.

That day is now celebrated as Juneteenth — a federal holiday, a day of remembrance, and a day of joy. And it connects directly to this family.

Our Family's Connection Easter Bell Was Seventeen Years Old

When General Order No. 3 was read in Galveston on June 19, 1865, Easter Bell Nichols was seventeen years old. She was living on the John Nichols plantation on Piney Creek in Bellville, Austin County, Texas — approximately 150 miles from Galveston.

Easter Bell had been born enslaved on that plantation on July 4, 1848. She had spent her entire life there. When the announcement reached Bellville in June 1865, she was free for the first time in her life. Six months later — on December 18, 1865 — she married Joseph Nichols.

Juneteenth is not an abstract holiday to this family. It is the specific moment when Easter Bell — your 3rd great-grandmother, the woman at the center of this entire family history — became legally free. Everything that followed in this family's story flows from that date.

Where This Family Came From The Migration Path · South Carolina → Mississippi → Texas

Our ancestors did not begin in Texas. They began in South Carolina — held by the Davenport and Waldrop families — and were carried westward through Georgia, Tennessee, and Mississippi as those enslaving families migrated in search of new land. Each move west meant more distance from any community, any family, any hope of escape. By the time Rendy Blayn and Easter Bell appear in the Bellville, Texas slave schedule in 1860, this family had been moved across four states over three generations.

Understanding this migration is understanding the violence behind it — not just the physical uprooting, but the deliberate destruction of family ties that made resistance harder and community building nearly impossible.

The Nichols Family · Austin County, Texas John Nichols · 4,428 Acres · Piney Creek Plantation

John Nichols (1797–March 3, 1864) was one of Stephen F. Austin's original colonists — among the first Anglo settlers to receive land grants in Mexican Texas. He received 4,428 acres on Piney Creek on October 20, 1830, under the State of Coahuila and Texas. The original Spanish-language land grant document survives and has been located by this research — it names the surveyor, the creek, the neighboring landowners, and the specific legal boundaries of the land where Easter Bell was born.

Nichols sold portions of his original grant to James and Thomas Bell in the 1830s. That land became the town of Bellville — named for the Bell family — on the same survey where your ancestors were enslaved. John Nichols's estate in 1864 listed ten enslaved people. He died before emancipation. His widow and the estate held Easter Bell and Rendy Blayn until June 19, 1865.

The creek described in this document — Piney Creek — is the creek Easter Bell was born beside in 1848. The landmarks named in the survey — the blackjack trees, the post oaks, the neighboring Stephenson and Cummins leagues — defined the boundaries of the world our ancestors lived in. This document, written in Spanish under Mexican law eighteen years before Easter Bell's birth, is the legal foundation of the land where this family's story in Texas began.

Why These Records Matter

Enslaved people had no legal right to create records. They could not sign deeds, file wills, or register births. Every document that confirms Patience, Clarissa, Rendy, and Easter Bell existed was created by the people who enslaved them — for the purpose of documenting property, not people. The Waldrop deeds, the Nichols land grants, the slave schedules — all of these were made to track ownership. We use them now to track survival.

The DNA evidence connects the living family to these historical records across 200 years and five generations. Forty-six matches to the Waldrop line. Eighteen matches to Claiborne Waldrop's branch. A confirmed DNA match to Sherry Newman Welty who grew up in Bellville and knew the Nichols family by name. The records and the DNA together confirm what the family has always known: we were here. We survived. We are still here.

What Happened to Newton Ballard? The Five Years Between Freedom and Silence · 1865–1870

Newton Ballard was alive in June 1865 when emancipation came to Texas. He was approximately 35 years old — a man in his prime who had survived a lifetime of enslavement. By 1870, he was gone from every record, and his wife Rebecca was raising their children alone in Upshur County, Texas. What happened to him in those five years is unknown. But the historical context of that period tells us the range of possibilities — and none of them are peaceful.

Possibility One · Racial Violence

Between 1865 and 1870, the Freedmen's Bureau in Texas documented hundreds of murders of formerly enslaved Black men by white Texans. The vast majority were never investigated, never prosecuted, and never recorded in newspapers. A Black man killed in Upshur or Smith County in 1866 or 1867 would leave no death record, no court filing, no newspaper notice. He would simply be gone. This is the reality of Reconstruction-era East Texas.

Possibility Two · Natural Death

The years immediately after emancipation were medically devastating for formerly enslaved people. Decades of inadequate nutrition, forced labor, and no medical care left many freed people physically depleted. Disease — yellow fever, cholera, tuberculosis — moved through East Texas communities with devastating speed in the late 1860s. Newton may have simply died of illness, without a record being created, before Texas began mandatory death registration in 1903.

Possibility Three · Convict Leasing

Texas had a thriving convict lease system after the Civil War. Black men were arrested on minor or fabricated charges and forced to work as essentially free labor — on railroads, in mines, on farms. We know Easter Bell's son-in-law John Armstrong and her husband Joseph Nichols were in the Austin County Jail on census day in 1900. Newton may have been taken into the convict system and died in those conditions, leaving no family-facing record.

Possibility Four · He Left

Some freed men left their families — not by abandonment but by necessity. Looking for work, following labor opportunities, or fleeing danger, some men became separated from families and were never found. It is less likely in Newton's case because Rebecca stayed in Upshur County and raised three children there, which suggests she had community support. But it cannot be ruled out. He may be in another state under a different spelling of his name.

We do not know what happened to Newton Ballard. What we know is that he survived slavery, he was freed, and he fathered children who built a line that reaches all the way to Jasmine M. Lee in 2026. Whatever took him — violence, illness, the convict system, distance — it did not take what he left behind. Research is continuing. — Jasmine M. Lee, April 2026

Fort Worth to San Antonio. San Antonio to California. Each move was driven by opportunity, necessity, and the desire to build something better than what the South allowed. The Armstrong line and the Ballard line traveled that distance separately and arrived at the same place — California — where the next generation was born free.

What Is a DNA Test? The Basics

A DNA test for genealogy (ancestry tracing) works by analyzing your genetic material — usually from a saliva sample — and comparing it to thousands of other people who have tested. When you share a significant amount of DNA with someone, the testing company flags it as a match. The more DNA you share, the more closely related you are.

The most important number in DNA genealogy is called the centiMorgan (cM). Think of it as a unit of genetic similarity. The higher the number, the more closely related two people are.

What Do the cM Numbers Mean?
3,400+ cMParent or child — you share about half your DNA
1,700–2,300 cMGrandparent/grandchild, sibling, aunt/uncle, or half-sibling
700–1,200 cMFirst cousin, great-grandparent, great-aunt/uncle
200–700 cMFirst cousin once or twice removed, half first cousin
50–200 cMSecond cousin, second cousin once removed
Below 50 cMThird cousin or more distant — still related but very far back

The Austin County Jail · Built on John Nichols's Original Land 1832 Land Grant · 1896 Jail · 1900 Census

The Austin County Jail in Bellville — the building where Joseph Nichols, Oscar Nichols, Hellen Nichols Armstrong, John Armstrong, and 4-month-old Eva Armstrong were all counted in the June 1900 census — was built in 1896 on land that had originally been granted to John Nichols by the Mexican Government on October 20, 1832.

John Nichols received this land grant two years before he appears in Texas GLO records in 1834. He was one of Stephen F. Austin's Old 300 — the original Anglo settlers of Texas. The jail where Easter Bell's entire family was imprisoned in 1900 sat on land John Nichols had owned 68 years earlier. The man who enslaved them owned the very ground under the building that held them.

Source · National Register of Historic Places · Austin County Jail · Bellville TX

"This land had been granted to John Nichols on October 20, 1832 by the Mexican Government. Nichols over the next several years sold portions of his original grant to James and Thomas Bell. In March of 1846, Austin County purchased a total of 145½ acres of land from the Bells for a sum of $2.00." The jail was built on Lot 6 of Block F of this original Nichols land grant. Architects F.B. and W.S. Hull of Dallas, 1896. Romanesque Revival style. A hanging gallows was housed on the fourth floor — used once, on March 14, 1901, nine months after Joseph Nichols and his family were counted in that building on census day.
