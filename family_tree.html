To every member of this family —

Seven years ago I started asking questions. Simple ones at first. Where did we come from? Who were the people before Grandma Margie? What were their names? Where did they live? What happened to them?

What I found changed how I understand myself, this family, and what it means to be who we are. I found Easter Bell Nichols — born enslaved on July 4, 1838, in Bellville, Texas. I found her mother Rendy Blayn, and Clarissa's mother Patience, who was born in South Carolina in the 1780s. I found a great-grandmother named Eva Armstrong Ballard who ran a café on San Antonio's East Side and was subpoenaed to testify in a police brutality case in 1955 — and told the truth. I found a great-uncle named James Wilson Ballard Jr. who made the front page of the San Antonio Light for helping break a cover-up case. I found ministers and porters and waitresses and gamblers and community organizers. I found people who built things under impossible conditions and kept going.

The more I searched, the more I realized I didn't know much. You can live your entire life believing things were seamless or perfect because of the love you share for one another. But the more you learn, you find out your loved ones were human — their past wasn't easy or smooth. And you start to understand your own defaults, your blessings, and the generational curses that were passed down without anyone meaning to pass them. You realize you are not starting from scratch. You are starting from somewhere. That somewhere is what this history is about.

My mother is Hjordis L. Corbin — born January 20, 1960, the daughter of Margie Nell Ballard Corbin. She grew up at 622 South Townsend Street in Santa Ana and later at 12182 Candy Lane in Garden Grove — the home where I have my earliest memories of NaNa. Hjordis carried the weight of losing her mother at 28 years old, navigated a probate process no one explained to her, and kept going. She is the reason I know this family at all. She is the thread that connects me to everything in this history.

"Teach your children who they are. Tell them Easter Bell's name. Tell them that Margie Nell Ballard made it from Fort Worth to California with seven children and kept going. Tell them they come from people who kept going."

I built this website so that what I found does not disappear. So that the names don't get lost again. So that the next generation doesn't have to start from zero the way I did.

This research is ongoing. Every generation adds to the story. What matters is that the foundation is here, sourced to primary documents, and honest about what we know and what we don't. If you have family photographs, documents, stories, or information about any family member — please reach out. Every piece helps.

A Personal Memory · Jasmine M. Lee

“Growing up on Candy Lane was love — real, warm, full love — mixed with the complications of generational things none of us had names for yet.”

Losing a grandmother at a young age does something to a family that is hard to put into words. It leaves a shape in the room where someone used to be. And the strange thing is — some of us were old enough to remember, and some of us were not. I sit somewhere in between. Old enough to carry the feeling, young enough that I have spent years reaching back through records and photographs trying to fill in what memory couldn't hold. I hear about the love she had for people. The parties. The holiday celebrations. The way her house was never empty and never quiet. I try to live vicariously through the records and imagine what that must have felt like — to walk into that house on a Christmas morning, to smell whatever she was cooking, to hear the noise of a full family that had no idea what was coming.

What I do remember, I hold onto tightly.

The house was a wrap-around — you could come in through the front door or through another entrance that led straight into the pool table room, which sat right in the middle of everything like the heart of the house. Uncle Ronnie had made himself a room in the garage. Aunt Se'Taun's room was the first one on the right — she drove a yellow Bug. NaNa's room was at the very back. Five bedrooms. Pictures on the walls. A home that looked like somebody actually lived there and loved it.

She ran a licensed board and care home — adults with mental or developmental disabilities who lived with the family as part of the family. Her house was always full. The clients were not separate from us. They were just there, part of the rhythm of the house, part of what normal looked like on Candy Lane. That was her calling — caring for people who needed a home when the world wasn't built to hold them. She built one herself and opened the door.

I remember Christmas there. I remember the sibling arguments — the kind that are loud and fast and forgotten by dinner. I remember Jackson, the family pet turtle, slow and unbothered by all of it. I remember NaNa driving a station wagon, wearing a reddish-colored wig — though her own hair underneath was long and beautiful and naturally reddish-brown. She always smelled amazing. She made pudding for dinner like it was the most natural thing in the world. One year she gave us Nintendos. We didn't live far from Disneyland.

She made sure we grew up without having to feel the weight of the things so many Black families carry — the worry, the scarcity, the instability. We didn't know we were being shielded. We just accepted what was there, and loved what we had. That is what she gave us. And losing her — at 59, too soon, before most of us were old enough to ask her the questions that matter — is something this family is still reckoning with. This website is part of that reckoning. This is me trying to give back what time took.

Before the Ships · Before the Names They Gave Us

Before Patience.
Before all of it —
we were already someone.

The DNA test didn't tell us who we became. It told us who we already were.

A people. Multiple peoples. From nations that built universities while Europe was still figuring out feudalism. From empires whose cities rivaled anything on earth. From civilizations so sophisticated that when the slave traders came, they had to break those civilizations apart deliberately — separating languages, separating families, separating memory — because a people who remember who they are cannot be made to believe they are nothing.

They failed. The DNA is the proof. Seven members of this family took the test. And what came back was not a list of percentages. What came back was a map. A map of where we were before any of this happened. This is that map.

Stop One · Nigeria & Yorubaland

Jasmine 24% · Hjordis 19% · Deanna 18% · Se'Taun 23% · Sonsorey 17% · Brendyal 21% · Ronnie 12%

Nigeria is the strongest signal in this family's DNA — and it runs through every single person who tested. Before there was a country called Nigeria, there was the Yoruba people — one of the largest ethnic groups in the world, over forty million people today, among the most influential civilizations Africa has ever produced.

The Yoruba built Ile-Ife, the city they called the cradle of the world — and the bronze sculptures they cast there predate the European Renaissance by two centuries. They built the Oyo Empire, one of the largest and most militarily sophisticated states in West African history. They developed Ifa, a system of divination and philosophy so rich and complex that UNESCO designated it a world heritage practice. They built cities. They built courts. They built a whole cosmology of how the universe works — the idea of Ori, that each person carries their destiny inside them, that your purpose is not given to you from outside but is something you were born already holding.

When the Oyo Empire fell in the early 1800s, the Yoruba became one of the most heavily trafficked peoples in the Atlantic slave trade — exported through the ports of Lagos and Ouidah by the hundreds of thousands. Your 24% Nigeria is the single strongest signal in the family. You carry more Yoruba ancestry than anything else in your blood. Ori — personal destiny, the thing you were born holding — traveled with every one of them across the water. It is still traveling. Right now, in you.

02

Stop Two · Benin & Togo · The Kingdom of Dahomey

Jasmine 14% · Hjordis 6% · Deanna 8% · Se'Taun 6% · Sonsorey 6% · Brendyal 14% · Ronnie 10%

Just west of Nigeria, along the coast of what is now Benin and Togo, lived the Fon, the Ewe, and the Aja peoples. They built the Kingdom of Dahomey — one of the most formidably organized military states in West African history. And within Dahomey's army was something that stunned every European soldier who encountered it: the Agojie — an all-female military regiment, the most feared fighting force in the region. Women who trained from childhood. Women who shaved their heads, wore the uniform, and fought with a ferocity that European soldiers wrote home about in disbelief. They were not a symbol. They were an army.

The Fon people also gave the world Vodun — a sophisticated religious tradition that survived the Middle Passage and became what we now know as Voodoo in Louisiana and Haiti, Candomblé in Brazil, Santería in Cuba. The ancestors did not leave their spirituality at the shore. They carried it across the ocean in their mouths.

And it was from this kingdom's coast that the port city of Ouidah operated the largest slave-trading operation in West Africa. Millions of people passed through what they called the Door of No Return — a gate on the beach where the Atlantic begins. Your 14% Benin and Togo means some of your ancestors stood at that door. They walked through it. And everything they were — the Agojie spirit, the Vodun memory, the Fon resilience — they carried with them onto the ship.

Stop Three · Mali & The Sahel

Jasmine 12% · Hjordis 4% · Deanna 13% · Se'Taun 7% · Sonsorey 12% · Brendyal 10% · Ronnie 18%

Ronnie carries 18% Mali — the highest reading in the entire family. And the Mali that percentage is pointing toward is not a small or quiet place in history. The Mali Empire was, at its height, one of the wealthiest empires on the face of the earth. Its emperor Mansa Musa — who ruled from 1312 to 1337 — is still considered by historians the richest individual human being who ever lived. When he made his pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324, he brought 60,000 people, 12,000 enslaved servants, and so much gold that he crashed the economy of Egypt and the Mediterranean for a decade just by spending it.

Timbuktu was not a punchline. Timbuktu was a city. The University of Sankore in Timbuktu had 25,000 students and over a million manuscripts. It was operating centuries before Harvard was founded. The Mande-speaking peoples — the Bambara, the Mandinka, the Soninke — built trade routes across the Sahara, developed complex oral traditions, and gave the world the griot — the professional oral historian whose job was to hold the family's entire history in memory and pass it down, generation to generation, so it would never be lost.

The griot tradition is exactly what this website is. Jasmine M. Lee is the griot. Seven years of research, primary sources, DNA evidence, family interviews — all of it is the griot tradition continuing in a new form. The Mande people brought that tradition across the ocean. It is still running.

Stop Four · Senegal & Senegambia · The South Carolina Signal

Jasmine 3% · Hjordis 5% · Deanna 1% · Se'Taun 3% · Sonsorey 2% · Brendyal 3% · Ronnie 0%

This may be the oldest traceable thread in the family

The Wolof, Serer, Mandinka, and Lebu peoples of Senegambia built the Jolof Empire along the Atlantic coast of West Africa. They were sophisticated farmers, fishermen, traders, and scholars. And they knew something about rice that would make them the most sought-after enslaved people in the American colonies.

South Carolina planters placed orders. Literally placed orders — specifying in letters and trade contracts that they wanted enslaved people from Senegambia specifically, by name, because the Wolof and Mandinka had been cultivating rice for generations in conditions similar to the South Carolina lowcountry. The planters did not know how to grow rice. The people they enslaved did. And those people's expertise built the rice economy that made South Carolina one of the wealthiest colonies in British North America.

Patience came through South Carolina. Clarissa came through South Carolina. Francis Davenport Sr. gave Patience to his daughter in Newberry County, South Carolina in 1795. That Senegal percentage in this family's DNA may be tracing directly back to the person who knew how to grow rice — who was brought to South Carolina specifically for that knowledge — and who became part of this family line.

One more thing: the Wolof language gave American English the words banana, yam, jive, and hip — as in hip hop. The most influential musical tradition in the world traces a direct linguistic line back to the Wolof people of Senegal. Your family carries their blood.

05

Stop Five · Ivory Coast & Ghana · The Gold Coast

Jasmine 3% · Hjordis 10% · Deanna 6% · Se'Taun 4% · Sonsorey 10% · Brendyal 4% · Ronnie 10%

The Europeans called it the Gold Coast because of what they found there — but the Akan, Ashanti, Baule, Agni, and Fante peoples of what is now Ghana and Ivory Coast did not name themselves after what others wanted from them. They named themselves after who they were.

The Ashanti Empire was the most powerful kingdom in West Africa in the 18th century — a military and political force so formidable that it fought the British to a standstill four times. The Akan developed Kente cloth — the woven textile of kings, each pattern a sentence in a visual language. They developed the Adinkra symbols — a system of visual communication so elegant and precise that designers worldwide still use it today. And among those symbols is one that belongs on this page.

Sankofa. A bird flying forward while looking backward. Its meaning: go back and fetch what was left behind. In Twi, the Akan language: Se wo were fi na wosankofa a yenkyi. It is not wrong to go back for what you forgot. This symbol — a bird who knows that moving forward requires knowing where you came from — is the most precise symbol in the world for what this research project is. Jasmine is the Sankofa bird. This website is the Sankofa bird. The seven years of research are the backward glance that makes the forward flight possible.

The enslaved people from the Gold Coast were loaded onto ships at Cape Coast Castle and Elmina Castle — two of the largest slave fortresses in the world, still standing on the coast of Ghana today. Hjordis, Sonsorey, and Ronnie all carry 10% from this region. That is not a coincidence. That is the same people, showing up in the same family, across generations.

Stop Six · Cameroon & Western Bantu Peoples

Brendyal 11% · Jasmine 2% · Hjordis 3% · Deanna 1% · Se'Taun 4% · Sonsorey 3% · Ronnie 1%

Brendyal Corbin — Michael E. Corbin Sr.'s daughter — carries 11% Cameroon. That is the highest single Cameroon reading in this entire family, and it almost certainly came from her father's side, since Michael's paternal line remains unidentified. The Bamileke, Duala, and Beti peoples of Cameroon built complex chieftaincy systems, extraordinary brass and bronze work, and a tradition of community economic organization called tontines — rotating savings and credit associations where community members pool money and take turns receiving the full sum — that survived the Middle Passage and became the foundation of Black American mutual aid societies, church financial systems, and community banking throughout the South.

This is not a small connection. The mutual aid tradition — the church collection, the community pot, the neighbors who show up when someone needs help — traces a direct line back to the Bamileke tontine system. Every Black church that ever passed a collection plate was running a system that originated in Cameroon.

And Cameroon enslaved people were shipped heavily to Louisiana and Texas specifically — through the Gulf of Guinea ports. Newton Ballard was enslaved in East Texas. Easter Bell was born on the Nichols plantation in Bellville, Austin County, Texas. The Cameroon signal in this family's DNA fits the Texas geography of your ancestors exactly. Brendyal's 11% may be the thread that, when followed, finally identifies who Michael E. Corbin Sr.'s father was.

Stop Seven · Central & Eastern Bantu Peoples

Jasmine 9% Western Bantu · 2% Eastern Bantu · Hjordis 5% Western Bantu · 2% Southern Bantu

The Bantu migrations are one of the greatest population movements in human history — a millennia-long expansion of Bantu-speaking peoples from their origin in what is now Cameroon and Nigeria across the entire southern half of the African continent, all the way to the eastern coast. Over 400 million people today speak Bantu languages. The Bantu gave the world the Kingdom of Kongo, one of the most powerful states in central Africa, with a population of over two million people and diplomatic relationships with Portugal as far back as the 1480s.

Your family carries Western Bantu, Eastern Bantu, and Southern Bantu ancestry across multiple testers. This is not a single people — it is an entire linguistic and cultural family that stretches across the continent. Juneteenth · Our Family

A Voice From Bellville · Laura Smalley · 1941 Interview

“Old master didn't tell, you know, they was free. I think now they say they worked them six months after that. Six months. And turn them loose on the 19th of June. That's why, you know, we celebrate that day.”

Laura Smalley was freed from a plantation near Bellville, Texas — the exact same town where Easter Bell was enslaved. She gave this interview in 1941 at Prairie View A&M University. She and Easter Bell were in the same community, on the same land, in the same moment. Laura Smalley may have known Easter Bell personally.

Former Enslaved Person Felix Haywood · San Antonio, Texas

“Everybody went wild. We all felt like heroes … just like that we were free. We knew freedom was on us, but we didn't know what was to come with it. We thought we were going to get rich like the white folks … But it didn't turn out that way. We soon found out that freedom could make folks proud but it didn't make them rich.”

Felix Haywood was freed in San Antonio — the same city where Eva Armstrong Ballard would later build her life and run her cafe. His words describe exactly the world Easter Bell walked into at 17 years old.

General Order No. 3 · June 19, 1865 · Galveston, Texas

“The people of Texas are informed that, in accordance with a proclamation from the Executive of the United States, all slaves are free. This involves an absolute equality of personal rights and rights of property between former masters and slaves, and the connection heretofore existing between them becomes that between employer and hired labor.”

Delivered by Maj. Gen. Gordon Granger. This was two and a half years after the Emancipation Proclamation. The delay was not an accident — Texas enslavers had suppressed the information as long as they could. Juneteenth did not become a legal state holiday in Texas until 1980. It became a federal holiday on June 17, 2021.

Before Juneteenth · The Legal Reality on Piney Creek

On July 10, 1862 — three years before Juneteenth — the Provost Marshal of Austin County published General Order No. 5 in the Bellville Countryman, requiring all enslaved people to carry written passes to leave their enslaver's premises at night, authorizing any white resident to arrest any enslaved person found away from home, and prohibiting enslaved people from carrying weapons of any kind.

Easter Bell was 14 years old when this order was published. Rendy Blayn was approximately 45. This was their daily legal reality on Piney Creek in Bellville, Texas. Three years later it was over.

Our Family's Direct Connection

When General Order No. 3 was read in Galveston on June 19, 1865, Easter Bell Nichols was seventeen years old. She was living on the John Nichols plantation on Piney Creek in Bellville, Austin County, Texas — approximately 150 miles from Galveston. She had been born enslaved on that plantation on July 4, 1838. She had spent her entire life there. When the announcement reached Bellville, she was free for the first time in her life.

Research Resource · Information Wanted Database

After emancipation, freed people placed ads in Black newspapers — primarily the Christian Recorder and the Southwestern Christian Advocate — searching for family members who had been sold away during slavery. Clarissa and Patience in South Carolina — the same names as your ancestors, in the same state, in the same era.

This Clarissa was from the Winkfield family, not the Waldrop family — a different enslaving household operating in the same Newberry County, South Carolina Methodist community. The connection to your Clarissa is not confirmed. But it shows how the same names — Patience, Clarissa — traveled through the same networks and communities. These women were real. San Antonio · 1954 · Police Brutality

The Caldwell Case

The night a man was killed in Eva's back office — and your great-grandmother told the truth when she didn't have to.

What Happened · San Antonio, Texas · 1954–1955

Johnie W. Caldwell was shot and killed by San Antonio police officer O.R. Graves in the back office of Eva Armstrong Ballard's establishment on the East Side. The officers who responded claimed Caldwell had been armed and threatening. Eva gave investigators the key to the office. She cooperated fully. She told the truth about what she saw.

The Cover-Up · What the Officers Did

Two officers later confessed to planting a knife on Caldwell's body after the shooting. The knife was used to justify the killing — planted evidence to make a murdered man look dangerous. When that came out, the case became front-page news.

No charges were filed against Eva. She had done nothing wrong. But being a Black woman who ran a business on San Antonio's East Side and was subpoenaed to testify in a police brutality case in 1955 — and who told the truth on that stand — was not nothing. That took something.

James Wilson Ballard Jr. · Front Page · San Antonio Light

Eva's son James Wilson Ballard Jr. was also connected to the case — he lived with Eva at 150 Paul Street and was listed on the federal gambling tax stamp records at the establishment at 207 N. Center Street. He made the front page of the San Antonio Light in 1954. He was part of breaking open the cover-up.

Note on the gambling tax stamp: This was an IRS tax under the Revenue Act of 1951 on what were effectively illegal gambling operations. It was not a voting record or a criminal conviction — it was a federal tax stamp required to operate. Many Black-owned East Side businesses held these. It reflects the economic reality of Black entrepreneurship in 1950s San Antonio, not a moral failing.

1943 Bexar County Deed · James + Eva Ballard · Confirmed Primary Source

In 1943 James Ballard and Eva Ballard were trying to buy a home. They received $175.00 for a home they had been trying to buy. The United States of America paid them $175 and took the property.

This document is signed James Ballard and wife Eva Ballard, of Bexar County, Texas — a confirmed primary source placing both of them in San Antonio seven years before the 1950 census. They were not just passing through. They were trying to build something. And it was taken.

DNA Made Simple

How to Read a DNA Match

Most of what we proved about this family came from DNA. Here is what it actually means — explained for someone who has never thought about it before.

What is a DNA Test?

You spit in a tube, mail it to AncestryDNA, and they analyze your genetic material. They compare it to millions of other people who have tested. When you share a significant amount of DNA with someone, it means you and that person have a common ancestor. The more DNA you share, the more closely related you are.

What is a centiMorgan (cM)?

The number you see next to a DNA match — like "41 cM" — is called a centiMorgan. Think of it as a unit of genetic closeness. The higher the number, the more closely related you are.

3,400+ cMParent or child — you share about half your DNA
1,700 cMGrandparent or grandchild
850 cMAunt, uncle, or half-sibling
425 cMFirst cousin
106 cMSecond cousin
41 cMThird cousin range — like Jahna Bradshaw, who confirmed Lois Ballard's place in the tree

What is ThruLines?

ThruLines is Ancestry's tool that connects your DNA matches back to a specific ancestor you both share. When it says "46 ThruLines matches through Clarissa" — that means 46 living people have tested their DNA and they all trace back to the same ancestor: Clarissa, your 4th great-grandmother, born enslaved around 1807. That many independent matches pointing to the same person is about as close to proof as genealogy gets.

What Did DNA Actually Prove for This Family?

It confirmed Easter Bell's line goes back to Clarissa. 46 living people match through Joseph Nichols as the common ancestor. This connects to the 1839 Waldrop estate deed that names Clarissa as a daughter of Patience.

It found Lois Ballard. A match of 41 cM to Jahna Bradshaw led us to Jahna's grandmother — Lois Bradshaw, born Elois Maud Ballard — the last living child of James Wilson Ballard Sr. and Eva Armstrong Ballard. She is 93 years old and confirmed living in 2023.

It confirmed Hellen Nichols Armstrong and Helen Reese are the same person. A 53.5 cM match to the Helen Reese connection supported the documentary evidence and the profiles were merged in July 2026.

The Two Acres

Piney Creek · Bellville, Austin County, Texas · 1865–2006

Six months after Juneteenth, Easter Bell Nichols — seventeen years old and newly free — married Joseph Nichols on December 23, 1869. They stayed in Bellville. They stayed on Piney Creek. They stayed on the same ground where Easter Bell had been born enslaved on July 4, 1838. And then they did something extraordinary.

They bought it.

Two acres in the J. Nichols Survey — still bearing the enslaver's name on the survey map, because that is how Texas land worked — became theirs. Not rented. Not sharecropped. Owned. Deeded. Two acres of Piney Creek that had once worked Easter Bell's mother Rendy Blayn, that had worked Easter Bell herself from the time she could walk, that the John Nichols plantation had built its operation around — that ground became the Nichols family's ground.

Easter Bell and Joseph had twelve children on that land. Hellen Nichols was born on that land. Eva Armstrong was born a few miles away, but grew up knowing that land as home. Three generations built their lives in its orbit.

The 160 Acres

Upshur County, Texas · 1856 · Texas GLO File No. 3378

Nine years before emancipation. Nine years before Juneteenth. Nine years before any law in Texas said Newton Ballard was a free man — Newton Ballard claimed 160 acres in Upshur County, Texas.

The Texas General Land Office File No. 3378 documents it. An enslaved man, navigating a legal system designed entirely to exclude him, found a way to claim land. How exactly this happened — whether through a sympathetic white intermediary, through a legal technicality, through sheer determination that left no paper trail — is not yet fully known. What is known is that the land record exists. Newton Ballard is in it. And in 1870, when the census taker came to Pittsburg, Upshur County, Texas, a nine-year-old boy named George W. Ballard — Newton's son — was living right there.

The family stayed on Newton's land after emancipation. They didn't leave. They had somewhere to be.

724 East Johnson Street

Waco, McLennan County, Texas · Rev. George W. Ballard · Lost 2017

Rev. George W. Ballard — son of Newton, father of James Wilson Ballard Sr. — owned a home at 724 East Johnson Street in Waco, Texas. He died there on October 24, 1937. The property stayed in the Ballard name after his death, deed recorded in McLennan County (Volume 289, Page 655).

It stayed in the family for eighty years after his death. And then, on November 7, 2017, it was lost to a Waco tax sale — Cause No. 2014-3167-4. A property that Newton's son had built and owned and died in, that had carried the Ballard name through three generations, was taken for unpaid taxes.

04

San Antonio · James + Eva Ballard

Bexar County, Texas · 1943–1957

In 1943, James Wilson Ballard Sr. and Eva Armstrong Ballard were trying to buy a home in San Antonio. They had an active purchase contract on Lot 1, Block 16, Boulevard Gardens Addition — a real neighborhood, a real house they were working toward. The property was swept into a federal receivership case — Schaefer vs. Milner, Cause No. F-8673, 73rd District Court, Bexar County — and a court ordered it sold to the United States of America.

James and Eva appeared before a notary. Eva was examined separately — as Texas law required for married women — to confirm she signed willingly. They received $175.00. The U.S. Government paid them $175 and took the property. This is the primary source that places both of them in San Antonio by 1943 — Bexar County Deed No. 295786, filed March 8, 1943.

They kept building. On April 22, 1944, Eva purchased 150 Paul Street for $3,600. She ran her Night Club from that address. The 1950 census shows her there as head of household, proprietor, working 60 hours a week, own business. On January 22, 1957 — after the Caldwell trial, after everything — she sold 150 Paul Street and left Texas forever.

What The Land Means

Newton Ballard in 1856. Easter Bell in 1865. Rev. George W. Ballard in Waco. James and Eva in San Antonio. Every generation of this family, as soon as the law allowed — and sometimes before it did — tried to own ground. Not rent it. Own it.

Some of it was taken. The Boulevard Gardens house for $175. The Waco property in a 2017 tax sale. But two acres on Piney Creek stayed in the family for 141 years. And 12182 Candy Lane in Garden Grove, California — Margie's board and care home, the house Jasmine grew up around — still carries the Corbin name on its license in 2026. The thread never broke. They just kept finding new ground to stand on.
