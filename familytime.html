1Jan
Emancipation Proclamation Takes Effect
January 1, 1863 · United States
History
▼
📖 What happened in history

On January 1, 1863, President Abraham Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation officially took effect. But here is what most people don't know: it did not free all enslaved people. It only freed enslaved people in Confederate states that were in rebellion against the Union. Enslaved people in border states that had stayed with the Union — like Kentucky and Missouri — were not freed. And crucially, Texas was still fully under Confederate control, which means Easter Bell Nichols was not freed on January 1, 1863. The Proclamation could not be enforced in Texas until Union soldiers arrived.

🏠 In our family

Easter Bell Nichols was 14 years old on January 1, 1863. She was enslaved on the John Nichols plantation in Bellville, Austin County, Texas. She would wait two more years and six months before the news reached her. The Emancipation Proclamation was real — but it was a piece of paper that couldn't get to Texas while Confederate soldiers still controlled the state.

💡 Why it matters now

This is why Juneteenth exists. Freedom was declared in January 1863. It arrived in Texas in June 1865. Two and a half years. That gap is the whole story.

Patience Sold in Open Market
January 1, 1795 · Newberry County, South Carolina
Family + History
▼
🏠 In our family

On New Year's Day 1795, Patience — your 5th great-grandmother, born ca. 1765 — was sold in open market in Newberry County, South Carolina alongside Moll, Hannah, Lin, Violet, Esther, and George. Seven people sold to Wadsworth and Turpin, Merchants to settle a debt of £87 12 shillings. Patience was approximately 30 years old. The deed is documented in Deed Book C, Page 124 — the same collection that confirmed her name, her age, and who bought her.

📖 What this meant in 1795

An "open market sale" meant Patience was sold publicly — like an auction. This was legal, common, and documented by a clerk of court. The same man who recorded this sale — Frederick Nance, Clerk of Newberry County — recorded the deed that gave Patience to Patty Waldrop seven months later. One clerk. Three documents. One woman's life changed forever.

💡 Why it matters now

January 1 is New Year's Day. A day of celebration and new beginnings. In 1795 it was the day your oldest known ancestor was sold to settle a debt. That tension — what this day means and what it meant — is the whole history of this family.

This is the reality of what slavery meant for Black women and girls.

Juneteenth · The Day Freedom Arrived in Texas
June 19, 1865 · Galveston, Texas · Federal Holiday since 2021
Family + History
▼
📖 What happened in history

On June 19, 1865, Union General Gordon Granger arrived in Galveston, Texas and read General Order No. 3: "The people of Texas are informed that in accordance with a Proclamation from the Executive of the United States, all slaves are free." This was two and a half years after the Emancipation Proclamation. Texas had resisted. Confederate soldiers had kept the news from spreading. Enslaved people in Texas had been kept in bondage long after they were legally free — because the people holding them never told them.

🏠 In our family

Easter Bell Nichols was 17 years old on June 19, 1865. She was in Bellville, Austin County, Texas — 60 miles from Galveston where the order was read. Six months after Juneteenth, on December 18, 1865, she married Joseph Nichols. Six months after freedom she chose her own life. She stayed in Bellville. She bought two acres on Piney Creek — the same land where she had been enslaved. The family held that land for 141 years.

💡 Why it matters now

Juneteenth became a federal holiday on June 17, 2021. For this family it was never just a holiday — it was the day that made the rest of the story possible. Every person in this archive exists because of what happened on June 19, 1865 in Galveston, Texas. Easter Bell was there. She survived long enough to see it. And then she built something.

Independence Day · Easter Bell Born Enslaved
July 4, 1848 · Bellville, Austin County, Texas
Family + History
▼
📖 What Independence Day meant in 1848

July 4, 1776 is the day America declared independence from Britain. By 1848 it had been a national celebration for 72 years — fireworks, speeches, parades. The Declaration of Independence said "all men are created equal." In 1848 there were approximately 3.2 million enslaved people in the United States — none of whom were considered equal under the law. Frederick Douglass gave his famous speech in 1852 asking: "What to the slave is the Fourth of July?" His answer: a day that exposed the hypocrisy of a nation that celebrated freedom while practicing slavery.

🏠 In our family

Easter Bell Nichols was born on July 4, 1848 — Independence Day — enslaved on the John Nichols plantation in Bellville, Austin County, Texas. She was born into a country celebrating its freedom on the day she was born with none. She died on December 17, 1935 — 87 years old, free, on her own land. When someone asks what July 4th means to this family: it is the birthday of the woman who held 141 years of family land together. It is the irony that became a legacy.

💡 Why it matters now

Every July 4th this family celebrates two things at once — the national holiday and the birthday of Easter Bell Nichols. She was born on a day that was supposed to be about freedom. She spent 87 years making that word mean something real.

Patience Given to Patty Waldrop · The Transfer
August 4, 1795 · Newberry County, South Carolina
Family + History
▼
🏠 In our family

On August 4, 1795, Francis Davenport Sr. of Newberry County, South Carolina gave "one Negro girl named Patience" to his daughter Patty Waldrop as a gift. Patience was approximately 30 years old — not a girl. The deed was recorded in Deed Book C, Pages 367-368 and witnessed by Isaac Davenport, Edward Turner, and Elizabeth Towling. This is the transfer that set everything in motion. Without this deed, there is no Clarissa, no Easter Bell, no Hellen, no Eva, no Margie, no Hjordis, no Jasmine.

📖 What a "Deed of Gift" meant

A Deed of Gift was a legal document transferring property — including enslaved people — from one person to another without money changing hands. It was often used when parents wanted to give their children a "start in life." In 1795 South Carolina, enslaved people were legally property. Giving Patience to Patty Waldrop was recorded the same way giving a piece of land would be recorded. The clerk signed it. The witnesses signed it. And Patience had no say.

💡 Why it matters now

Read the full story of Patience and every document that ever wrote her name down on The Paper Trail page — linked from the main archive. This date is where the paper trail begins.

30Aug
John Armstrong Shot and Killed · Fort Worth
August 30, 1911 · Fort Worth, Texas
Family + History
▼
🏠 In our family

John Armstrong — Eva's father, Hellen's husband — was shot and killed near the East Front Street entrance of the Terminal Hotel in Fort Worth, Texas on August 30, 1911. He was approximately 33 years old. The man who killed him, Jack Thompson, a white transfer line operator, claimed Armstrong had assaulted his 12-year-old son. Thompson was released on a $3,000 bond and never convicted. Eva was 11 years old. Texas Death Certificate No. 18682. The bullet entered Armstrong's mouth and came out the top of his head.

📖 What this meant in 1911 Texas

In 1911 Fort Worth, when a white man killed a Black man and claimed self-defense, the legal system rarely intervened. From 1877 to 1950, Texas had more documented lynchings than almost any other state. Racial violence was not an exception — it was a mechanism of social control. Jack Thompson's $3,000 bond was standard. His acquittal was expected. John Armstrong's murder was one of thousands of documented racial killings in this era where the perpetrator faced no consequences. Hellen was left to raise Eva alone. She ran an ice cream parlor. She survived.

💡 Why it matters now

The pattern that killed John Armstrong in 1911 — a Black man, a white accusation, no conviction — did not end in 1911. Understanding what happened to John Armstrong means understanding something that this country has never fully resolved. His death shaped everything Eva became.

Reference Guide

Terms You Might Not Know

Tap any term to read a plain-language explanation — and how it connects to this family.

Emancipation Proclamation ▼

The Emancipation Proclamation was an executive order issued by President Abraham Lincoln on January 1, 1863. It declared that all enslaved people in Confederate states that were in rebellion against the Union were free. But it had major limits: it did not apply to enslaved people in border states that stayed with the Union, and it could not be enforced in Confederate territory where Union soldiers were not present. In Texas, enslaved people did not learn of their freedom until June 19, 1865 — two and a half years later — when Union soldiers arrived in Galveston.

🏠 In our family: Easter Bell Nichols was 14 years old when the Emancipation Proclamation was issued. She was still enslaved in Bellville, Texas. She would wait until June 19, 1865 to be free.
Juneteenth ▼

Juneteenth (June 19th) marks the day in 1865 when Union General Gordon Granger arrived in Galveston, Texas and read General Order No. 3 — announcing that all enslaved people in Texas were free. This was more than two years after the Emancipation Proclamation. The name comes from combining "June" and "nineteenth." Texas became the last Confederate state to receive the news. Juneteenth became a federal holiday on June 17, 2021.

🏠 In our family: Easter Bell Nichols was freed on Juneteenth 1865 in Bellville, Texas — 60 miles from where General Granger read the order in Galveston. Six months later she married Joseph Nichols. Six months after that she started building a life.
Convict Leasing ▼

Convict leasing was a system used by Southern states after the Civil War to re-enslave Black people through the criminal justice system. The 13th Amendment abolished slavery — except as punishment for a crime. Southern states quickly passed Black Codes that criminalized minor activities like loitering, not carrying proof of employment, or "vagrancy." Black people arrested under these laws were leased to private companies — railroads, mines, sugar plantations — as unpaid labor. From 1870 to 1912, 60 percent of Texas prisoners were Black. The system was slavery by another name.

🏠 In our family: In the 1900 census, John Armstrong and his family are listed at a Bellville address classified as "Inmate of Jail" with John's occupation left blank. This likely reflects the convict leasing system — not a census error. John was a 22-year-old Black man in Austin County, Texas in 1900.
Jim Crow Laws ▼

Jim Crow laws were state and local laws that enforced racial segregation in the American South from roughly 1877 to 1965. They covered everything: where Black people could eat, sit, go to school, ride, work, and live. They were named after a minstrel character and enforced by both legal penalties and racial violence. The Supreme Court's 1896 ruling in Plessy v. Ferguson legalized "separate but equal" facilities — which were never equal. Jim Crow laws were dismantled by the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

🏠 In our family: Jack Thompson shot and killed John Armstrong in 1911 and was never convicted. Eva Armstrong ran a nightclub on San Antonio's East Side — the historically Black side of a segregated city. The family's Great Depression addresses in Fort Worth's Southside and East Side reflect where Black families were permitted to live under Jim Crow housing policies.
Redlining ▼

Redlining was a discriminatory practice in which banks, insurance companies, and the federal government refused to offer mortgages, loans, or insurance to people in predominantly Black neighborhoods — often literally drawing red lines on maps around those areas. The Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) created maps in the 1930s grading neighborhoods — and Black neighborhoods were consistently graded "D" (hazardous), making it nearly impossible for Black families to buy homes, build equity, or accumulate generational wealth. Redlining was officially outlawed by the Fair Housing Act of 1968 — but its effects on Black wealth persist today.

🏠 In our family: When Eva Armstrong Ballard purchased 150 Paul Street in San Antonio in 1944, she was buying on the East Side — the side of a redlined city where Black families were permitted to own property. The U.S. government took the Ballard family's Boulevard Gardens purchase contract in 1943 for $175. The systems that constrained where this family could live and own are documented in their property records.
The Great Migration ▼

The Great Migration refers to the movement of approximately 6 million Black Americans from the rural South to cities in the North, Midwest, and West between 1910 and 1970. They were leaving Jim Crow laws, racial violence, and sharecropping — and seeking factory jobs, better schools, and safety. The first wave (1910–1940) went primarily to Northern cities like Chicago, Detroit, and New York. The second wave (1940–1970) expanded to include Western cities like Los Angeles, Oakland, and San Francisco. California received hundreds of thousands of Black Southerners during this period.

🏠 In our family: Norman Caswell Corbin was born in Cleveland, Ohio — a Great Migration destination. His family moved to Detroit — another major destination. Margie and Norman eventually settled in Santa Ana, California. The Corbin family's path from the South through the Midwest to California is a direct reflection of the Great Migration routes taken by millions of Black families.
Deed of Gift ▼

A Deed of Gift was a legal document used to transfer property — including land, animals, furniture, and enslaved people — from one person to another without money changing hands. It was commonly used by parents transferring assets to their children. In the context of slavery, a Deed of Gift transferring an enslaved person was recorded the same way any property transfer was recorded — by a clerk, with witnesses, in a deed book. The person being transferred had no legal standing and no say.

🏠 In our family: On August 4, 1795, Francis Davenport Sr. used a Deed of Gift to transfer your 5th great-grandmother Patience to his daughter Patty Waldrop. The document is recorded in Deed Book C, Pages 367-368, Newberry County, South Carolina. It is the document that changed everything for this family.
Reconstruction ▼

Reconstruction was the period from 1865 to 1877 when the federal government attempted to rebuild the South after the Civil War and integrate formerly enslaved Black Americans into civic life. During this period, Black men could vote, hold office, and buy land. Hundreds of Black politicians served in state and federal government. But Reconstruction ended in 1877 when federal troops were withdrawn from the South, leaving Black Southerners vulnerable to the violence and legal systems — Jim Crow, convict leasing, Black Codes — that would dominate the next century.

🏠 In our family: Easter Bell and Joseph Nichols bought their two acres on Piney Creek during the Reconstruction era — when Black land ownership was briefly possible and protected. They held it for 141 years. The family's ability to acquire that land was a direct product of Reconstruction. The threats to that land — and to John Armstrong's life — were products of Reconstruction ending.
The Transatlantic Slave Trade ▼

The Transatlantic Slave Trade was the forced transportation of African people to the Americas by European powers from approximately 1500 to 1900. An estimated 12.5 million people were kidnapped from Africa and transported across the Atlantic — the Middle Passage — under brutal conditions. Approximately 10.7 million survived the crossing. They were taken primarily from West and Central Africa — the same regions that show up in this family's DNA: Nigeria, Benin and Togo, Mali, Senegal, Ivory Coast and Ghana, Cameroon. The trade was formally ended by the United States in 1808, but slavery itself continued until 1865.

🏠 In our family: Your DNA results show Nigeria (24%), Benin & Togo (14%), Mali (12%), Western Bantu Peoples (9%), Ivory Coast & Ghana (3-10%), and Senegal (3%). Each of those percentages represents ancestors who were taken from those regions and brought across the Atlantic. The DNA is the evidence of that journey. The family archive is the story of everything that survived it.
