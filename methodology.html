Research Methodology

How records become conclusions — and how this archive distinguishes fact, supported connections, leads and unresolved questions.

For Family Members · Plain Language

This page explains how the research was done — what counts as evidence and what does not. If you are a family member just reading the family story, you do not need to read this page. If you are a researcher who wants to verify or build on this work, this page explains the standards used to evaluate conclusions and why some things are labeled confirmed while others are labeled hypothesis.

The Proof Standard

This archive works toward the Genealogical Proof Standard. A reasonably exhaustive search is one part of that standard, not the whole test. Each conclusion must be evaluated on its own evidence; a status label is not a blanket guarantee that every relationship in the archive has been proved.

  1. Search widely enough. Look for relevant records, name variants, neighboring households and changes in county boundaries.
  2. Make sources traceable. Record the creator, record type, date, jurisdiction, volume and page, repository, image or file number, and where the image was accessed.
  3. Compare the evidence. Check names, ages, places, relationships, informants and the independence of the sources.
  4. Address conflicts. Preserve competing dates and identities and explain which evidence supports the conclusion.
  5. Write the reasoning. State what the records establish, what is inferred, and what remains unresolved.

Standards reference: Board for Certification of Genealogists · Ethics and Standards and applying the proof standard.

What the Labels Mean

Documented fact

A cited record directly documents the stated event or wording. A named person in a deed is documented; that alone does not establish their identity in a later record.

Supported connection

Multiple independent clues support a relationship or identity. Read the evidence argument and any remaining conflicts.

Research lead

A plausible route for investigation. It is not added to the confirmed family line as an established relationship.

Unresolved

The evidence is missing, incomplete or conflicting. Similar names and nearby households do not close the gap.

Core Principles

Evaluate the Source and Its Information

Prefer original record images and identify abstracts, indexes, transcripts and family recollections as such. An original document can still contain secondhand information: a death certificate's birth details depend on the informant, and census answers may come from someone other than the named person. Assess who knew the information, when it was supplied and whether independent records agree. Trees and automated hints are leads to verify, not proof by themselves.

Hypothesis Discipline

Unconfirmed connections are always labeled as hypotheses — never stated as facts, never silently incorporated into the confirmed line. The Louisa Hypothesis, which proposes a community connection between Newton Ballard and the John Bullard estate enslaved people in Louisiana, is supported by three primary source documents and significant geographic evidence. It is still labeled a hypothesis because no document directly names Newton in relation to that community. This discipline protects the integrity of the confirmed line and ensures that future researchers can clearly see what is proven and what is still being investigated.

Conflation Risk Awareness

Multiple white Bullard and Ballard families with overlapping geographies and similar names appear throughout this research. Early in the project, a land claim belonging to a white Newton Bullard — son of John William Bullard and Rebecca Fortenberry Bumgarner, born 1832 Tennessee — was incorrectly associated with our Newton Ballard. That error was caught and corrected when five independent record types failed to support the connection. Every new record is verified against the established generational timeline before being incorporated into the archive.

Census Birthplace Data Caution

Preserve conflicting census ages and birthplaces rather than choosing the most convenient answer. Changes may reflect informant knowledge, recording error, uncertain dates or mistaken identity. Compare the full record sequence. Proximity to an event helps evaluate a source, but no record type automatically overrides every other source.

Geography Filtering

Begin with places established by the records, then expand to adjacent counties, earlier jurisdictions and migration routes. Modern Bellville and Austin County boundaries are not limits on where the family could have lived. Document the places and periods actually searched, including unsuccessful searches.

Generational Math

Every new record is cross-checked against the established generational timeline before it is accepted. A record for a person with the right name in the right county is not automatically the right person — the birth year implied by the record must be consistent with what the confirmed documents already show. The 1952 Tulsa marriage record for a James Wilson Ballard was flagged as likely belonging to a different individual precisely because the birth year implied by the record created a generational impossibility.

How a Record Becomes a Conclusion

Keep the image, transcript and interpretation separate

Preserve the record's wording. Mark uncertain readings with brackets or a question mark; do not silently expand initials or modernize a name. OCR and AI-assisted reading are drafts to check against the image. A cropped image needs its surrounding page and citation.

Follow people through the community

Compare family, associates, neighbors, witnesses and estate recipients across time. Appraising an estate does not prove ownership, and a transfer between enslavers does not establish a biological relationship. Keep enslaver/property networks separate from the family tree.

Document the limits of a search

A failed name search is not proof that an event did not happen. Record the collection, date range, jurisdiction, variants and missing volumes before drawing a conclusion from an absence.

Use DNA with documentary evidence

Evaluate matches and proposed relationships alongside records and the shared-match context. Ethnicity estimates and automated tree suggestions do not prove a particular ancestor or an enslaver connection. Obtain consent before sharing living people's DNA details or private records.

Record corrections

When evidence changes a conclusion, record what changed, why, and the source that prompted it. Contributions should include the full record image and citation. Send corrections through Contact the Archive.

About the Researcher

This research was conducted by Jasmine M. Lee, founder and CEO of the Dock Ellis Foundation, and a direct descendant of every confirmed ancestor documented in this archive. The research began as a personal family history project and grew into a multi-year archive spanning primary sources across eight states and spanning 1778 to the present. Jasmine reviewed original documents directly — voter registers, deed books, tax rolls, probate records, slave schedules, land patents, and notarial records — and uploaded her findings directly into this research archive.
