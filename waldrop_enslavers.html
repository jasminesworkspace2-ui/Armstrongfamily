The Waldrops.

Three generations of one family held Patience. Not one man, once — a father, then two of his sons, passing her between their own households for seventy-five years, the same way land or livestock changed hands when it suited them. This page is about them: who they were, where they came from, and exactly who they held.

The Patriarch
Generation One Ezekiah Parrish Waldrop b. 1774, Newberry County, South Carolina · d. between 1839–1847, Holmes County, Mississippi

Son of John Waldrop (d. 1794, Newberry County) and Tabitha Parrish Waldrop. Married Martha "Patsy" Davenport before August 5, 1795 — the same week her own father, Francis Davenport, gave Patience to the couple as a gift. Fought in the War of 1812, Tennessee Militia, alongside his own eldest son Claiborne, November 1814 – May 1815.

Moved constantly across his own lifetime: Newberry County, South Carolina (to 1801) → Franklin and Jackson Counties, Georgia (1802–1806) → Maury County, Tennessee (by 1812) → Lawrence County, Mississippi (1820) → Hinds County, Mississippi (1827) → Rankin County, Mississippi (1839). Patsy died around 1824–1825. He remarried Sarah Knight in 1825 and had at least three more children with her.

Why the 1839 Deed Was Really Signed A Lawsuit, Not a Gift

In March 1839, Ezekiah lost a major lawsuit. Fourteen days later — March 25, 1839 — he signed the deed dividing Patience, Rachel, Clarissa, and Clarissa's three children among his eight children. Within five months, one of those children had already resold two of the people he'd just been handed, for cash. This was not generosity. It was a man moving everything he owned out of his own name before a court could take it.

Who Actually Held Patience
Generation Two Stephen R. Waldrop b. 1805, Georgia · d. Jackson, Louisiana · m. Matilda Prestridge, 1833, Mississippi

Ezekiah's own son. Received Patience directly from his father twice: first in a private sale, August 11, 1827 — $300 for Patience, alongside Rachel ($500), Clara ($300), and Tom ($200) — and again through the 1839 deed. Within five months of that second transfer, he had already sold Rachel and her son to his own father-in-law, Howell Prestridge, for cash. He lived in Bienville and later Jackson Parish, Louisiana through 1850, 1860, and 1870. The last confirmed record of Patience anywhere places her in his household in 1870 — age 90, born Virginia, recorded only by the initial "P."

Generation Two Claiborne Parrish Waldrop b. 1797, Newberry County, SC · d. 1872, Sabine Parish, Louisiana

Ezekiah's eldest son. Married Mary Ann "Polly" Bowlin, 1815, Maury County, Tennessee. Relocated to Sabine Parish, Louisiana between 1838–1841. His own granddaughter's firsthand account, recorded decades later, states plainly that he "killed a little negro slave because he didn't obey his command quick enough to suit him." His own 1850 and 1860 census records show a household of enslaved people that this archive, for years, believed included Patience and Clarissa directly — a reading since superseded by the stronger 1870 record placing Patience with Stephen instead. Whether Clarissa herself remained in Claiborne's household through her own life is still the working belief, though not fully closed by a single named document.

Seventy-Five Years, One Family.

From 1795 to 1870, Patience was never once free of this family's own ledgers. She was given, sold, transferred, and sold again — always inside the same eight children's own descendants, never fully out of their reach. The last document that names her at all is a single letter, an age, and a birthplace, in the household of the same family that had owned her from the start.
