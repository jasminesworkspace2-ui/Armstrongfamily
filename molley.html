Molley

ca. 1750–1765 · Union County, South Carolina · Your 6th Great-Grandmother

Her name appears once in a mortgage document. She is listed as the mother of seven children. She kept them together across two states for nearly fifty years. She is the beginning of this entire family's documented history.

March 22, 1793 · Deed Book B, Page 467 · Newberry County, South Carolina

The Document That Named Her

On March 22 1793 a man named James Sims Sr. of Union County South Carolina signed a mortgage to Peter Braselman of Newberry County for £1,000 sterling. The mortgage listed 36 enslaved people as collateral — alongside 850 acres of land, ten horses, furniture, hogs, and tools. These 36 people were not property. They were a community. They were families. But the law of South Carolina in 1793 recorded them as things to be pledged against a debt.

Halfway down the list of names — after Jack and Doll and their children, after Isaac and Tabby and their children — came this line:

Deed Book B · Page 467 · March 22 1793 · Newberry County South Carolina · Primary Source

"Molley and her children Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, Tom and Patience..."

Recorded by Frederick Nance, Deputy Clerk. Witnessed by James Sims Jr., Robert Powell. Recorded June 29 1793.

Seven children. Molley is listed as their mother. No husband is named. No father is acknowledged. Seven children born into bondage — and a mother who is named only because her children increased the value of the debt being secured.

That is the only document that names Molley. It is enough. Her name survived. Her children survived. You are one of them.

She kept seven children together in a system designed to separate them. That is not luck. That is a mother's determination across decades and state lines.

— Jasmine M. Lee · Dock Ellis Foundation · 2026
What the records tell us and what they cannot say

What We Know — and What Was Taken

Molley was born approximately 1750 to 1765 in South Carolina. She was enslaved by James Sims Sr. of Union County — a planter with 850 acres and enough debt to mortgage 36 human beings against it. She had at least seven children — Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, Tom, and Patience. Whether she had more children who were sold away before 1793 is unknown. Whether she had a husband who was sold away is unknown. The records do not say because no one recording the mortgage cared to ask.

Her origins — where she was born, who her parents were, what community she came from before the Sims household — are not in any document found to date. She arrived in the historical record already a mother of seven, already in Union County, already pledged against a debt she had no part in making.

What the document does not say is what mattered most. It does not say that she sang to those children. It does not say what language her mother spoke or what name her grandmother carried. It does not say whether she mourned when Patience was taken from her in 1795 — given away by James Sims to his son-in-law's family — or whether she ever saw Patience again. The mortgage recorded value. It did not record a life.

No man is named as Molley's husband in any document. Her children carry only her name into the record. She raised them. She is who they came from. That is what the archive holds — and it holds it because she was strong enough to keep them close long enough for the documents to record them together.

— Research note · Primary source confirmed · March 22 1793
The community Molley kept together

Her Seven Children · The Community in the Sims Household

Molley's children are listed in the 1793 mortgage alongside twenty-nine other people — the full community of the Sims household. These are the people who raised each other, who knew each other's names before any document ever recorded them, who formed the bonds that James Sims Sr. could mortgage but could not own.

These thirty-six people knew each other. They worked the same fields, lived on the same land, raised each other's children, mourned each other's losses. They were a community — not a list. What happened to them after James Sims mortgaged them against his debt in 1793 is not fully documented. Some of them may have been sold when the mortgage was called. Some may have stayed with the Sims family. Some — Patience and Tom — were transferred to the Davenport and then the Waldrop household, where they remain documented for another 46 years.

The others — Charles, Edward, Delf, Abram, Sawny, and Molley herself — disappear from the record after 1793. Not because they ceased to exist. Because no one recording the documents after that point cared to name them again.

August 4, 1795 · Deed Book C, Pages 367–368 · Newberry County, South Carolina

When Patience Was Taken

Two years after the mortgage was recorded, Patience was gone from the Sims household. On August 4 1795 Francis Davenport Sr. — a 77-year-old planter in Newberry County dying and distributing his property among his children — gave Patience to his daughter Patty Martha Davenport Waldrop. The deed said Patience was "one negro girl." She was approximately 15 to 30 years old. She had been part of Molley's household. Now she belonged to the Waldrop family.

Tom went with her. Molley's son and Molley's daughter — transferred together into the Waldrop household. Whether Molley knew where they were going. Whether she was able to say goodbye. The document does not say.

What the document does say is that Patience went as "with her increase" — meaning any children she might ever have would automatically belong to the Waldrop family by law. The law of South Carolina in 1795 reached forward in time and claimed Patience's unborn children before they existed. One of those children was Clarissa — born into the Waldrop household after 1795 — who became the grandmother of Easter Bell Nichols — who became the great-great-grandmother of Jasmine M. Lee.

The chain began with Molley. It ran through Patience. It ran through Clarissa. It ran through generations of women who held onto each other across states and centuries and a system that tried to break every bond they formed.

Deed Book C · Pages 367–368 · August 4 1795 · Newberry County South Carolina · Primary Source

"Francis Davenport Sr. of the District of 96 and Newberry County to his beloved daughter Patty Waldrop — one negro girl named Patience — with her increase."

Witnessed by Isaac Davenport, Edward Turner, Elizabeth Towling. Proved before Charles Griffin, Justice of the Peace.

This document is the first primary source confirmation of Patience in the Waldrop household. It is the legal instrument that begins the chain connecting Molley to Jasmine M. Lee.

1795–1839 · South Carolina to Mississippi

46 Years Together

Patience and Tom — Molley's daughter and Molley's son — stayed together for 46 documented years. From the 1793 mortgage in Union County South Carolina to the 1839 declaration in Rankin County Mississippi, Tom is present beside his sister in every document. They were transferred together into the Waldrop household. They traveled together as the Waldrop family migrated westward. They appear together in the 1827 transfer from Hezekiah Waldrop to his son Stephen R. Waldrop of Yazoo County Mississippi. They appear together in the 1839 Rankin County declaration — Patience and her descendants — Clarissa, Rachel, Tom, William, Amanda, Harriet — all together.

The Waldrop family could move them. The law could transfer them. A mortgage could pledge them. But Molley's children stayed beside each other for nearly half a century. That is the inheritance Molley gave them — not property, not freedom, not a name recorded in any official document — but each other.

March 25 1839 · Rankin County Mississippi · Hezekiah Parrish Waldrop Declaration · Primary Source

Following the death of his wife Patty Martha Davenport Waldrop — the woman who received Patience in 1795 — Hezekiah Parrish Waldrop formally declared and transferred Patience and all her descendants to his and Patty's children. He cited the original August 4 1795 Francis Davenport deed as legal foundation.

Named in the 1839 declaration: Patience · Rachel · Clarissa · Tom · William · Amanda · Harriet

Tom — Molley's son — is still present. 46 years after the 1793 mortgage. Beside his sister. In Mississippi.

Ten Generations · 1793 to 2026 · Every Link a Primary Source

From Molley to Jasmine

This is what Jasmine M. Lee found. Not a story passed down through family memory — though that matters too. A chain of primary source documents connecting ten generations of women across two hundred and thirty years. Every link below is confirmed by an original document — a deed, a mortgage, a slave schedule, a death certificate, a census record, a probate declaration.

Most African American families cannot get past 1870 — the first census to record Black Americans by name. Some get to 1865. A rare few reach 1850. This line reaches 1793. To Molley. To a mother listed in a mortgage document in Union County South Carolina. To the beginning.

Molley appears in one document. One line in a mortgage recorded by a clerk in 1793. But her daughter Patience was the beginning of a line that crossed two states, survived slavery, claimed land, built businesses, raised families, and produced a researcher who found her name 233 years later and brought it home.

This page is dedicated to Molley — whose surname was never recorded, whose husband was never named, whose origins are still unknown — but whose children were documented together in 1793, and whose line has never been broken since.
